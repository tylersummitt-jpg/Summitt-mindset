Lesson 1 of 8
Roadblocks for Women in Leadership

Now that we’ve discussed some of the overall obstacles to women in the workforce, let’s take a look at specific obstacles to women in leadership. If you’re like many organizations, you may have plenty of women leaders at your company. You may even have women at the senior leadership level and think, Hey, two out of our eight board members are women…not bad. The problem is, despite decades of being in the workforce, women are having a much harder time reaching and staying at the upper echelons of leadership, through no lack of qualifications of their own.

According to the LeanIn.org and McKinsey annual survey, 75 percent of C-Suite roles are held by men, compared to 25 percent of women. As the C-Suite generally guides the direction of the business, this means many organizations are being helmed by a female voice of 25 percent, despite women representing half of the actual workforce. When you factor in underrepresentation in certain fields, like technology, this number becomes even more slim at the top. Add diversity to the mix and the situation is even more dire—of that 25 percent, only 1 in 20 is a woman of color.¹

Lesson 3: Roadblocks for Women in Leadership 
 
Joan Cronan 
If you’re like many organizations, you may have several women in key leadership positions 
at your company. You might look at the legacy of female board members or C-suite 
executives and think, “Hey, we’re getting there!”  
The problem is, despite decades of being in the workforce, women are having a much 
harder time reaching and staying at the upper levels of leadership.  
And the problem trickles down to C-Suites and corporate boardrooms all over America. 
Again…why? 
Well, experts have a variety of theories. One is a “leak in the pipeline.” One is expectations 
around traditional gender roles. Women often face stereotypes that label them as “less 
competent” or less suited for leadership roles compared to men – hence the old adage of 
“having to work twice as hard to be thought of as half as good.” 
You know I remember so many times myself as an AD would be sitting in a boardroom and I 
would be the only female in the room. Somebody said, “How do you do it?”  
“One, I was more prepared. Two I wanted to fit in and be part of the discussion, so I just 
didn’t hide at the back of the room.”  
I remember one time we were negotiating television contracts – big money contracts. I was 
the only woman in the room, and a gentleman comes in to do his presentation, and he 
says, “You guys...and Joan.” And then he goes on to give his proposal. I took a deep breath, 
and I said to myself, “OK.”  
Then, a second gentleman comes in the room and says, “You guys...and Joan.” That’s 
when I called timeout, and I said, “Let’s talk about this.”  
I said, “I am here to make a decision just like everybody else. It never bothered me to be 
called ‘you guys’ in a setting like this. I never coached ‘woman-to-woman’ defense, I 
always coached man-to-man. So be sure you don’t try to point out the difference in men 
and women sitting around the board table because you’re all there to do the same job.”  
To be seen as competent, women need to project confidence and assertiveness, but are 
often stereotyped as “unfeminine” or “aggressive” when they do, stereotypes their male 
counterparts don’t encounter when advancing their own careers.

Some include organizational barriers – ingrained practices and policies that inadvertently 
favor men, such as 8-5:00 working hours that weren’t designed for parents who had to pick 
up children from school. Or male-dominated industries where women are constantly 
having to perform as minority players. 
Then, there’s a lack of mentorship – a lack of other females who have been in leadership 
positions to show the way, leaving women to be groomed by men who might have had an 
entirely different set of career struggles than they have along the way. 
In the upcoming lesson, we’ll take a look at some of these examples and more.

## cw_mp_04_st_002 — A Leak in the Pipeline

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 2

Lesson 2 of 8
A Leak in the Pipeline

If women are opting out at a slightly higher rate than men, this begs the question: Why? Historically, many analysts would have pointed to childbearing as the natural culprit. But according to several surveys, surprisingly, the issue isn’t entirely due to childbearing.

A study by Harvard Business Review, for example, found that high-achieving women pursued their careers an average of 11 years, and 60 percent worked well past the birth of their second child.² Most fascinating is the number of women who continue working well beyond their child bearing years and still drop out.

We’ve already discussed gender biases and the isolation of being the only one in the room in certain fields. Like their non-managerial counterparts, future female leaders are often paddling upstream from the start. Then there’s the factor of hidden work, specifically:

Women work more at home after their corporate hours are through.

Women often work more at work, shouldering special projects and initiatives that don’t directly contribute to the bottom line, but contribute to the overall work culture or environment.

Studies show that women are looking for more flexibility in the post-COVID world, and work-life balance is a big reason more women are leaving corporations than men. While both sexes appreciate a healthy work-life balance, women typically have more work at home, making this more of a need than a want.

## cw_mp_04_st_004 — Death by 1,000 Cuts

Type: interactive_scenarios · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 8
Death by 1,000 Cuts

So what does all this look like in reality? Many women describe their decision to leave the workplace not as something that happened in one fell swoop, but rather death by a thousand cuts. Take these fictitious examples of three women: Lei, Allison, and Felicia.

Scenario 1
Lei

Lei is an associate director of quality assurance in IT. She has held her position for about a year and a half and is finding that she’s questioned more than the male mentor who trained her. She was even asked to provide a weekly report of what she’s working on.

In four of her five standing meetings, she’s the only woman in the room. In one of these meetings, a recurring lunch meeting, the team frequently volunteers her to go pick up lunch—“because no one else knows how to order it the right way,” they say. She begins to wonder if her personal brand is becoming “sandwiches.”

When Lei asks about a raise a year into her role, she’s told raises will be scarce this year and she should be grateful for what she has. Lei is puzzled because her male counterparts didn’t seem to have issues getting a raise six months earlier. She debates taking it up, but worried she might look like she’s playing the minority flag, or worse, complaining. After all, she might not have all the facts, and discussing salary is taboo. Lei continues to work, but feels like a sparrow in a hen house.

Scenario 2
Allison

Then there’s Allison, a newly hired VP of product with 20 years of experience at an organization with all its VPs at the senior board level: the VP of finance, VP of marketing, VP of human resources, VP of operations and VP of creative.

The company hires two new male VPs, the VP of technology and the VP of sales at the same time they hire Allison. The two new VPs are placed on the board immediately, but Allison is told she needs to “prove her worth” before she can be considered. Right away this puts Allison at a disadvantage—because the VPs of sales, technology, and marketing, whom she needs to collaborate with regularly, are all on the board and she isn’t.

In addition, whenever she tries to present the new product line, the male VP of Sales constantly interrupts Allison, making it difficult to get through her slideshow. When Allison mentions this to her leader, she’s told she’s “probably being too sensitive about it.”

The company is preparing for a major conference, with Allison’s team putting in extra work in the office to revamp conference room space. While her male counterparts stay till 7 p.m., Allison has to leave at 5 p.m. to pick her kids up from the after-school program, driving back to work at 6:30 p.m.and working until 8:30 p.m.

The conference networking event is a golfing tournament hosted by the company’s CEO, followed by a bourbon tasting at a local distillery. Allison attends, but is relegated to the sidelines, as she doesn’t know how to play golf and doesn’t particularly care for bourbon. While Allison went to school to be a product manager and loves her job, she wonders if she’s ever going to fit in at this new company and secretly dreams of going into real estate, like her sister-in-law.

Scenario 3
Felicia

Lastly, there’s Felicia, a 35-year-old associate creative manager with a degree from a highly respected private college. Felicia worked for eight years before having her first child. Eager to continue her career, she puts the child in daycare at $800 per month. Two years later when she has her second child, she puts that child in daycare as well, for a total of $1600 a month. Felicia’s bi-monthly take-home pay is only $2,100 per paycheck.

Her company is bought out, and Felicia is hopeful for more flexibility with the new leadership, but they reduce the number of work-from-home days from eight days a month to two. A male counterpart from another division is then promoted to creative director over Felicia, in spite of having four and a half less years of experience. Felicia is crushed. She then learns that her first child has been diagnosed with hearing loss and will require special attention and additional therapy appointments. She decides to opt out of the corporate workforce and become a freelancer, hoping to replace 60 to 70 percent of her corporate paycheck, but on her own terms.

Summary

While these are fictitious examples, they represent the myriad of nuanced challenges women face in their careers. Have you ever known someone like a Lei, Allison, or Felicia?

## cw_mp_04_st_005 — The Confidence Gap

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 5

Lesson 5 of 8
The Confidence Gap

Because of having to face more resistance in their careers than men, women sometimes run into the “confidence gap.” While some might attribute this lack of confidence to their sensitive and perceptive social nature (aka picking up on nonverbal clues and slights others might miss), studies point out that it may also be learned due to an accumulation of years of gender biases in the workplace.

PERSON A
Take Person A, whose input in meetings is often received well and rarely questioned.

PERSON B
Take Person B, who is interrupted twice as much as Person A and often has their statements questioned in front of everyone, in addition to being lectured (however innocently) on basic principles. Through no lack of confidence, Person B would naturally learn to be more hesitant in speaking up and would question their capabilities, especially if everyone else in the room accepts this behavior as normal.

To add insult to injury, Person B is told they need to “speak up more” to be thought of as leadership material.

The same goes for assertiveness: Person A is socially rewarded for their candor, frankness, and overall drive. Yet when Person B exhibits the same characteristics, they are told they sound bossy or aggressive.

If you’ve always been in the role of Person A, it may be hard to identify the life of Person B, yet these are the types of interactions women repeatedly observe and report.

Because of having to face more resistance in their careers than men, women sometimes run into the “confidence gap.”

## cw_mp_04_st_006 — The Leadership Trap

Type: labeled_graphic_reading · Source sequence: 6

Lesson 6 of 8
The Leadership Trap

The Leadership Trap: He’s a Baller, She’s Scary.

This leads us to one of the next hurdles for women, the “leadership trap.” When it comes to being potential candidates for leadership, oftentimes women report:

Having to be assertive enough to be thought of as a leader, yet when exhibiting assertive qualities, you run the risk of being stereotyped as bossy.

Being a team player, only to find you’re seen as too much of a team player to be thought of as a leader.

Taking advantage of work-life balance policies to achieve caregiving needs, only to be labeled as not as committed.

Being told you are greedy or unrealistic when trying to negotiate salary, despite a 12 percent pay gap, and the fact that men negotiate salary more often than women.

Attempting to be likable, but not too likable, or they’ll think you’re not leadership material.

On top of it all, there exists a smaller margin of error for women. Men in leadership roles are historically forgiven for domineering personalities and social faux pas as long as they get the job done (even being praised as a good leader in spite of it all), whereas women are penalized for similar behavior.

On top of it all, there exists a smaller margin of error for women.

So, given all the above, what can an organization do to support and nurture strong female leaders at their organization? The good news is, the answer may not be as hard as you think. We’ll discuss that specifically in upcoming lessons.

### Complete source transcript

Remembering Pat: Pat’s Legacy 
 
 
Sue Nokes 
 
Pat Summitt was probably the most incredible woman or person I ever met. Pat Summitt 
was everything you ever dreamed she would be and thought a role model or an example 
should be.  
 
She tried to live it every day to the fullness of who she was. She was a great mother. She 
was a great wife. She was a great leader. She was a wonderful friend, and she was as 
competitive as heck, but she had such integrity and you would have never outworked her. I 
mean, she would work you to death, but she never asked you to do anything that she wasn't 
willing to do herself. And again, her integrity and her honesty was just exceptional, and she 
just was everything I hoped she would be.  
 
During my time at T Mobile, I had the great privilege to work with a lot of the NBA players 
and the WNBA players. And I did a lot with Magic Johnson, and Magic Johnson would keep 
saying, “Could you get Pat to come out to LA? Could you get Pat?” They respected her.  She 
was so respected in the industry, in the profession and in the game. That it was amazing to 
me to see.  
 
The other thing I would tell you, is that Pat readily shared just as she went to others to learn. 
And she's infamous for who she went and learned about their offense or their defense, or 
she was always that student that was always trying to learn and evolve and develop her 
game and her coaching ability and her leadership so that she could deliver better for the 
girls and the girls had more success, whereas most people are afraid and we don't want to 
share because we're afraid they'll use it and then use it against us and they'll get the 
promotion or whatever and we won't. 
 
She wasn't like that at all. She would tell you, she'd say, “Well, here's what I found works. 
Here's what doesn't work. Do this. Don't do that.”  
 
She shared, just as much as she asked others to share with her.
