Lesson 2: How Did We Get Here? 
 
Christina Reckard 
So, when it comes to women in the workplace, “How did we get here?”  
That is an excellent question. The last century has seen a remarkable transformation in the 
role of women in the U.S. workforce.  
At the beginning of the 20th century, the role of women in the workforce was largely 
restricted to a handful of careers such as teaching, nursing, and domestic work. Social 
expectations and legal rules limited women’s employment opportunities and rights.  
World War I in the early 20th century marked a pivotal moment. With millions of men 
deployed overseas, women began filling roles in factories, offices, and other industries 
essential to the war effort. This shift demonstrated women's capabilities beyond 
traditional domestic roles and laid the groundwork for change. 
The passage of the 19th Amendment in 1920 granted women the right to vote, symbolizing 
greater political and social empowerment. Women increasingly entered the workforce, 
particularly in clerical positions, thanks to the expanding economy and technological 
advancements like the typewriter. 
However, the Great Depression of the 1930s brought severe economic hardship, leading to 
widespread job losses. During this period, societal attitudes hardened against working 
women, especially married women, who were often seen as taking jobs from men and time 
from their children. Despite this, many women continued to work out of necessity – often in 
poorly paid positions. 
World War II was another turning point. As millions of men went to war, women once again 
stepped into roles traditionally held by men. The image of "Rosie the Riveter" became 
iconic, symbolizing women's contributions to the war effort as factory workers, engineers, 
and in other critical positions. By 1945, women made up nearly 37% of the U.S. workforce, 
a 10% jump in just five years. 
The 1950s saw a return to traditional gender roles, but the 60s brought significant social 
upheaval and the official rise of the women's liberation movement. This decade was 
marked by landmark legislation aimed at reducing gender discrimination, such as The 
Equal Pay Act of 1963 and the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibited employment 
discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or nationality.

The 1970s – around the time Pat herself first entered the workforce – saw a substantial 
increase in the number of women entering higher education and the professional job 
market. The 1980s continued this trend, with women increasingly entering more advanced 
professions such as law, medicine, and business. However, this decade also highlighted 
ongoing challenges. The term "glass ceiling" was coined – an invisible barrier preventing 
women from reaching top executive positions – and it became a prominent issue. Women 
also faced challenges related to work-life balance as they juggled professional 
responsibilities and family duties. 
The 1990s, around when Pat hit her stride as a coach, was marked by significant growth in 
women's professional achievements. Women began to occupy prominent leadership roles 
in politics, sports, and the decade also saw the implementation of family-friendly policies 
in many workplaces like The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) of 1993. 
Yet in spite of all of this growth, a myriad of stereotypes and biases still plagued women in 
the workforce. While women in were catching up educationally and legally, the old 
attitudes and traditions of corporate America were slow to adjust. The perception of what 
was a “woman’s work” and what was “male work” still persisted. Were women just as 
ambitious as men? Did they have the necessary leadership skills? 
And that brings us to where we are today, examining more of the landscape of the modern 
workplace and the obstacles facing female leaders. As we look to the future, continued 
advocacy, policy changes, and cultural shifts are necessary to ensure women can fully and 
equally participate in and benefit from the workforce. 
Let’s learn more!

It [Was] a Man's World

As the familiar song says, it truly was a “man’s world” at the beginning of the last century. In the early 20th century, only 20 percent of women in the U.S. worked outside the home. Secondary education was scarce for everyone in those days, and nearly nonexistent for women. Add to this the facts that women were less likely to do manual labor and that it was socially taboo for married women to work (only 5 percent of married women were gainfully employed).

By 1930, the percentage had grown to 50 percent of single women and 12 percent of married women working outside the home. Forty years later, the number had only slightly climbed to 50 percent of single women and 40 percent of married women. By 1990, however, 74 percent of women had entered the workforce (compared with 93 percent of men). An almost 20 percent gap still persisted, but finally—a mere 30 years ago—women finally entered the workforce in their own right.¹

According to the U.S. Census Bureau, in the early 20th century only 20 percent of women in the U.S. worked outside the home.

The point is, the entrance of a majority of U.S. women into the workforce is a relatively new one, having come about largely within the last 50 years. Until then, the overall workforce structure, including rules, social norms, and expectations, had understandably been created by men. This yielded a climate for disparities like the pay gap, caregiving, and other gender biases to emerge and create the landscape we have today.

While college became more of an expectation for men in the 1950s through the 1970s, it wasn’t yet for women, giving them a distinct educational disadvantage when entering the workforce. Furthermore, when women began to enter the workforce en masse toward the second half of the 20th century, their involvement tended towards noncorporate roles, like waitress, secretary, receptionist, bookkeeper, or school teacher.

Lesson 4 of 8
The Present State

2020-Present Day

Fast forward to the present. Higher education for women is no longer an issue—in fact, as of 2019, female college graduates began to outnumber men.² However, many of the traditional issues facing women in the workforce remain due to persistent biases, some of them including:

Women are less qualified than men.
Women are less committed to their careers than men.
Women aren’t as ambitious as men.
Women weren’t designed to do certain jobs, especially in the areas of math and sciences or manual labor.
Women lack inherent leadership qualities such as assertiveness, negotiation, or decision-making skills.

As mentioned, women are relatively new to the workplace. They are new players in a game that has been historically male-dominated, meaning the rules and behavioral rewards are largely established by men. For example, leadership characteristics such as assertiveness and drive are seen as positive qualities in men, but looked on as unfeminine in women, or the fact that the traditionally set U.S. corporate office hours of 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. don’t favor employees who have to pick children up from school. And females aren’t the only players who have workplace culture stacked against them, as there were little traces of minorities or immigrants in corporate America up until the mid-20th century.

Let’s take a closer look at some of these specific biases.

## cw_mp_03_st_006 — Biases About the Biases

Type: interactive_reading_audio · Source sequence: 6

Lesson 6 of 8
Biases About the Biases

As mentioned earlier, one of the most long-standing biases is around caregiving and family-rearing. While it’s illegal to discriminate outright, many women carry the silent toll of being working mothers long past their childbearing years.

Myth
Women are less committed to their careers.

Reality
Women have historically displayed a great deal of commitment to their careers, going around, under, and through obstacles like childbearing, prejudices, and the increasing demands of work-life balance.

Myth
Women are less ambitious than men.

Reality
Male-female ambition starts off largely equal.

Myth
Women weren’t designed to do certain jobs.

Reality
Career stereotypes have pushed the perception of acceptable work for women out of bounds farther than any documented biological disadvantages would suggest.

Myth
Women lack corporate qualities.

Reality
The qualities of corporate America were largely decided by males, the founders of U.S. corporate culture. Females do exhibit these qualities, but when they do, they may face more stereotypes and biases.

Many of the women who do choose to remain in the workforce rise before the sun comes up just to be able to juggle childcare duties, transport kids to school, work a job, pick the kids up from school or extracurriculars, help with meal preparation and homework, only to get up and do it all over again. Add to this the stigma of staying home, getting a nanny or au pair (in addition to the financial cost), and women seem to be “darned if they do, darned if they don’t” on this one.

Behind the stereotype of “Women are less committed to their careers” are the biases of “Women would rather be stay-at-home moms” or “Women don’t want to work outside the home.” While this may be true for some women, it certainly isn’t true for all women. Further complicating matters is that some women who have resigned themselves to stay at home, do so because of childcare complications or other logistics—not due to a lack of enthusiasm for an outside career.

Perhaps most notable in all this is the fact that, despite all the progress of the modern century, the division of labor in most U.S. households still remains largely unequal.

“Looking back on it, I don't think anyone in the family worked as hard as my mother or got less credit for it.” — Pat Summitt

“As I got older I understood that women had to fight for respect in more ways than one,” Pat once recalled.

“It seemed to me that my mother worked as hard or harder than my father and my brothers. One impression has stayed with me all these years: at the dinner table when my brothers would finish their tea, they'd hold up their empty glasses and rattle them. They wouldn't say a word; they [would] just lift their glasses and shake them until my mother served them. It was their way of saying, “Come fill my glass.” I can still see those hands holding up those glasses rattling the ice. My mother waited on them and I thought, that isn't right.”

“My dad and my brothers wouldn't do any work around the house, whether it was to make their beds or work in the garden or mow the lawn. They just worked the farm—that was it. But my mother did the cooking and the ironing and the cleaning and the milking and worked the garden and worked in the store and in the dry cleaners. When my dad got into the house-building business, my mother was the one who painted the houses and laid the carpets. Looking back on it, I don't think anyone in the family worked as hard as my mother or got less credit for it.”³

A recent study of more than 200,000 respondents found that women start their careers with just as much ambition as men or more.⁴ That ambition, however, is either encouraged or sabotaged by the opportunities and workplace interactions women face over time. Add to this that women’s ambition has historically been viewed as abnormal, unfeminine, or socially unpopular in previous decades, and it’s a perfect storm for blaming women for being underachievers.

Reality: Women start their careers with just as much ambition as men or more.

While the list of acceptable careers for women has been shattered long ago (thank goodness!), a nagging stereotype still chases women who, even today, choose to be trailblazers in a particular industry: Some jobs weren’t meant for women.

This is usually a result of three categories:

Pat herself was definitely a minority in her field during the peak of her career.

When women are underrepresented, they find themselves in a minority landscape. When they exist in a field but hold subordinate positions, it perpetuates the stereotype that male employees are better leaders/decision-makers.

When women are overrepresented in a field, stereotypes are formed as to what’s woman’s work and what’s man’s work (such as the incorrect but long-held jokes about male nurses or female truck drivers). This is what makes overall workplace equality so important!

The truth is, bias around women lacking corporate qualities isn’t backed by data inasmuch as dogma. As mentioned in previous lessons, when women are graded on leadership qualities, the women generally poll equal in positive feedback to the men. Adding to this is another layer we’ll call the perception gap. There is discussion over whether women genuinely inherently lack certain traits in the workplace, or if it’s the old case of natural vs. learned.

For example, the phrase, “women don’t speak up as much as men,” must mean they are shy, timid, and insecure, right? Well, it depends.

You could also look at it this way:

Pat certainly wasn’t. In short, it’s getting harder and harder to differentiate the chicken from the egg when it comes to decades of workplace stereotypes.

Understanding how we got here helps us understand the landscape of the modern workplace and some of the obstacles female leaders face when navigating the terrain. How do these stereotypes and biases play out in the workplace exactly? Usually in the area of microaggressions, which we’ll discuss in an upcoming lesson.

Women are underrepresented in a field, such as the math and sciences, transportation industry, or roles involving manual labor.
Women do exist in a field, but hold subordinate positions (e.g., a dentist’s office where two dentists are male and 10 dental hygienists are women, or the two airline pilots are male and the flight attendants are mostly women).
Women are overrepresented in a field (e.g., teaching, speech pathology, etc.), sometimes casually dubbed the “pink collar” fields.

Women are more scrutinized in meetings. People who are more scrutinized speak up less.
Women are more likely to have their authority challenged and are frequently talked over in meetings. People who are more likely to be questioned or talked over may naturally hold back for a fear of not getting it just right.
Women are soft-spoken. But anytime women display a so-called unfeminine characteristic, they typically pay the price for it, whether directly or indirectly, raising the question of whether women are any less forthright than men in their natural state.
