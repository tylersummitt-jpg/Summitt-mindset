Lesson 10: Powerful Conversations  
 
Alexis Hornbuckle 
When it comes to communication, there are two areas that can make or break your career: 
how well you communicate with your leader (or upper management), and how well you 
handle conflict – that is to say, how you handle difficult conversations with a colleague or 
team member. 
Ever been around someone who didn’t handle difficult conversations well? Anything that 
had the slightest bit of emotion around it, and within five minutes it was like a bomb went 
off in the conversation? Maybe this is you – does just the thought of having a difficult 
conversation make your heart race? 
How about those you know who couldn’t communicate well with their leaders? Their leader 
never knew what they were working on, or they felt conversations with this person were 
always negative, a never-ending string of problems.  
How far do any of these people go in their careers?  
Not as far as those who master the art of powerful conversations! 
Let’s start with your leader. Think about your current leader. On a scale of 1-10, how well do 
you feel you communicate with them currently? Do you know your current leader’s 
communication style? 
There are three keys to remember when communicating with your leader: right channel, 
right timing, right message. 
In addition, there are a few best practices to keep in mind with your leader. 
Be positive! This one is important. Leaders quickly learn which team members are solution-
oriented who are looking for the positive in each situation, as opposed to those who are a 
Negative Nancy. 
Be prepared. Always be prepared when you take your leader’s time. Do you know what you 
plan to discuss? Is there something important you need to show them? Plan this out in 
advance, so your meetings are well-organized and productive. 
Bring solutions. Problems are real, but whenever bringing an issue to your leader, also 
present some options for solving the problem if you’re able. Your leader may have thoughts 
of her/his own, but they will appreciate you bringing options to the table.

Be open to feedback. Be coachable! This one is a must for any growth in your career. 
Sound doable? Let’s learn!

## cw_mp_11_st_002 — Communicating with Your Leader

Lesson 2 of 6
Communicating with Your Leader

Sometimes great communication with your leader may come naturally (if it does, great!). Other times it may be a challenge, especially if your leader has a different personality type or communication style than you do. As a female leader, there are a few things you can do to optimize interactions with your leader.

Know your leader's communication style. This can be summed up in three parts:

Right channel

What medium do they prefer most (email, phone, text, Teams or Slack chats, etc.)?

Right timing

How often do they prefer communications? Leaders are busy, and some managers prefer frequent updates, while some prefer consolidated updates. Some prefer to receive news/information as it happens in real time, while some prefer to hold items to be discussed in a one-on-one or meeting. Take a minute to think about your leader’s overall preferences. (When in doubt, just ask them!)

Right message

Make sure your communication is professional, concise, and free from frustration. More on this later.

Optimizing & Sustaining Communication

Let's review some ways to optimize your communication with your leader.

In Conclusion

In review, here are some of the ways we've suggested to sustain an open and communicative environment with your leader:
Optimize Your 1:1s
Be positive
Be prepared
Bring solutions
Be open to feedback

Optimize your 1:1s with your leader.

Don’t overlook the rapport that can be formed in regular one-on-ones with your leader. Many times this is the foundation for trust that will help your career sink or swim.

Be Positive

This should be a given, but it’s not. Employees who bring a sense of positivity to work are rarer than you think, and in the world of leadership it can be a breath of fresh air. It doesn’t matter how talented you are—if your attitude is negative (or perceived as negative), your relationship with your leader will eventually suffer. Make positivity a part of your personal brand. Not all conversations will be positive in themselves, but when faced with something negative to communicate, try your best to frame it in a positive way.
“No one ever got anywhere, accomplished anything, or survived any amount of ill luck by being negative… With attitude you can determine your own performance.”
– Pat Summitt

Be Prepared

A little preparation goes a long way. Whenever you take up your leader’s time, even in a standing meeting or one-on-one, come prepared with a list of items to discuss or an agenda for the meeting, in addition to any information or data that may be needed.

Bring Solutions

This one is important! Leaders often feel like their office is a revolving door of problems to solve. Whenever bringing an issue to your leader, also present some options for solving the problem. Your leader may have thoughts of their own on the matter, but the fact that you took the time to think through solutions will go a long way in displaying your own leadership abilities. Depending on the issue and vantage point, sometimes you may be even more qualified to solve the problem than your leader.

Be Open to Feedback

Finally, be open to feedback and take any constructive criticism with an open mind. Remember, there’s usually a grain of truth in every bit of feedback, even if the feedback seems severe at the time.
Giving feedback is a sign of respect and care. Your leader cares enough to offer it to you and believes you have the maturity to receive it. Try not to get defensive about critiques or criticism, but instead use it to propel your best work forward.

## cw_mp_11_st_004 — Having Hard Conversations

Lesson 4 of 6
Having Hard Conversations

Deep Dive: Having Hard Conversations

As a female leader, there are going to be times when hard conversations are necessary, whether with a leader, colleague, or other business associates on the team. As humans, we all tend to naturally shy away from hard conversations, which can be harder for females due to fear of stereotypes or biases and how they might be perceived.

Have the conversation privately!

When defenses are high, they will be even higher in a public setting. It can be intimidating to have a private conversation when conflict needs to be resolved or negative feedback given, but hard conversations are almost always best relayed in private.

Pick the right time.

As Pat noted, timing is everything in communication, especially when having sensitive conversations. Is first thing in the morning or the very first day someone is back from PTO the best time to have a difficult talk? How about late on a Friday, when everyone is tired and 20 minutes away from the weekend? Pick a time that is not only best for you, but also conducive to your message being received.

Is this a repeat occurrence or a one-time offense?

Whether it’s happened once (e.g., an insensitive comment) or two or three times, it will still need to be addressed. But acknowledging the frequency of occurrence will be key in how you address it. Was someone having a bad day and speaking thoughtlessly, or is this a repeat behavior?

Begin by asking the person how they’re doing and if anything is going on outside of work.

Here’s where emotional intelligence comes into play for savvy leaders. You think you know your colleagues, but you never really know what may be going on, and it helps to know the context for the conversation you’re about to have. If someone just had a miscarriage a few days earlier or is upset because a close friend passed away, it’s probably not the best time to scold them for parking in the wrong parking space, even if it does need to be addressed eventually. And you won’t know unless you ask. Context is key.

Try to keep it objective and leave out other co-workers.

Instead of saying something like, “People have noticed that you’re late…” or “Your tone in the last meeting made people feel uncomfortable,” stick to the facts. “You’ve been running behind to the last four team meetings,” or “In the last meeting, you sounded defensive and frustrated” will suffice.
Don’t quote other people who haven’t agreed to be quoted in the conversation, unless you get their permission. (“Javier said your tardiness was affecting his meetings as well.”) It would be up to them to have a conversation with that person.

Be mindful of your tone; keep it low and slow.

Do your best to keep frustration and anger out of your voice, even if these are emotions you may be feeling at the time.

Don’t beat a dead horse.

Once the point has been made, leave it. Emphasizing someone’s shortcomings over and over will start to erode the conversation and make them defensive

Finally, listen to what the other person has to say.

Nothing is more frustrating than being criticized and being kept from being able to say anything. Give them a chance to process what has been said, and listen thoroughly to their response.
With a little intentionality, you can become the master of having hard conversations when needed.

Deep Dive: How to Have Difficult Conversations 
 
Christina Reckard 
The second part of communicating at work is knowing how to have difficult conversations. 
That’s right – conversations that make your heart race a little faster, the ones that you might 
be dreading. You might be thinking of some examples right now.  
I know in my own career, I have had to have some very difficult conversations. At one point, 
I even had to dismiss an underperforming team member that had helped to hire and train 
me, and who I considered a friend. Talk about a difficult conversation.  
As a woman in leadership or a future leader, difficult conversations come with the territory. 
The key is to learn how to master them, or at least not be afraid of them. Because when we 
put off the thing that needs to be said, or the conversation that should have been had with a 
team member four weeks ago, everyone loses. So here are some things to keep in mind. 
First, don’t put it off. The harder the conversation is, the less you want to put it off. Hard 
things only get more difficult with time, not easier. Pat once said “When something 
unpleasant happens between two members of our team, I don’t believe in burying it. I ask 
the two players to confront each other and settle it. ” 
Before initiating the conversation, make sure you can clearly articulate the main issue. 
There may be several details or general things to address, but what are the 2-3 main things, 
“We can’t leave this room until this is squared away. ”  
You also want to keep the discussion rooted in the facts. Instead of “You’re 
underperforming, ” which could be seen as subjective, try “Our revenue numbers are down 
due to lack of sales, ” or “The new product isn’t doing as well as we need it to, ” instead of 
“You rolled this product out too quickly. ”  
Facts help ground the conversation in reality and reduce emotional responses. If you’re 
having a conversation with your leader about a new role with new job duties, for example, 
what are the 2-3 main things you need to make sure they understand? 
 
Before having the conversation, make sure you pick the right time and place. This usually 
means in private, not public, and at a time when it’s convenient for both parties. Ever had 
someone who gave you some difficult news at 8 a.m. in the morning and you had to walk 
back to your desk and pretend nothing had happened? How about a Friday afternoon at 5 
p.m., right when you were about to walk out the door for the weekend? Having a

conversation with the finance team about hiring more people on the last week of the month 
when they’re trying to reconcile the numbers probably isn’t the best timing. Nor is having an 
important conversation in the middle of a crowded place, or within earshot of others. 
 
You also want to approach the conversation with a constructive mindset. Aim for a 
collaborative discussion, not a confrontational one. If there is a desired outcome or change 
that needs to happen from the conversation, you want to disarm the other person as much 
as possible from putting up their defenses or thinking their beliefs are under attack. 
That being said, be specific and direct. There’s an old saying “To be unclear is to be unkind. ” 
You want to clearly articulate the issue without beating around the bush. Don’t retreat to 
the safety of generalizations. 
Remember, this is a conversation. You want to make sure that you listen and leave time for 
the other person to share their perspective. Listen without interrupting and try to 
acknowledge their feelings and viewpoints. “I know you really like Sheila, but the fact is, her 
work is below the standard of what we usually accept. ” Or “I can see where you feel the 
new department merger is unfair. But we’re counting on you to help make this work. ” 
 
Do your best to stay calm and composed throughout the conversation. If emotions run 
high, take a break. Try to avoid escalating any emotions that may be present. 
 
After everything has been discussed and you’ve listened to the other person’s perspective, 
you want to shift the focus from the problem to finding solutions. Collaborate with the 
other person to identify steps to address the issue. At the end of the conversation, 
summarize the key points discussed and the agreed-upon action items. This ensures that 
both parties have a clear understanding of the outcomes and next steps. 
Now, those are some principles to consider in general for difficult conversations. If you’re 
going to be delivering feedback as part this conversation, maybe as in a 1:1 with a team 
member, here are some things to keep in mind. 
Slow down. Let them know your heart. Provide feedback in context of your belief in that 
person. “I know you’re capable of more than this, ” or “I’ve seen you perform better than this 
in the past. ” Frame your concerns from your own perspective using “I” statements. For 
example, say “I’ve noticed that deadlines are often missed, and I’m concerned about the 
impact on our projects” instead of “You always miss deadlines. ”  
Don’t speak for people who aren’t in the conversation, unless you have their permission. 
That isn’t fair to the other person and robs them of the opportunity to communicate in their

own way, in addition to making the person on the other end of the conversation feel like 
they are being ganged up on. You’re sharing other people’s opinions and feelings, but the 
only person they have to discuss them with is you.  
Finally, go slow, and give them time to process. Have them repeat back to you what they’ve 
heard, in order to make sure both of you are on the same page with what the next steps 
need to be. 
Now, I know that’s a lot, but with some time and practice, difficult conversations won’t be 
so difficult. As a leader or future leader, you want to know how to communicate in the best 
way, even in the worst of times or circumstances.
