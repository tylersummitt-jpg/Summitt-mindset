Lesson 1 of 6
The Power of Unapologetic Authenticity

Heading

With lingering gender bias in the workplace, it can be tempting for women to curb their behavior or personal brand at work to fit in. This can manifest itself in a lot of ways:

Not speaking up when you have a solution.

Not developing your skills in a particular area that could be perceived as “unladylike.”

Not asking for a promotion, for fear of looking too bold or greedy.

Underplaying your accomplishments or achievements for fear it might come across as bragging.

Volunteering to plan an office lunch or outing you really don’t have time to plan.

Downplaying your personal brand to look “nicer” or “friendlier.”

Pretending to be interested in sporting events or other traditionally male events in order to fit in.

The truth is, the workplace is filled with complex and unique personalities. No one has every skill or talent—we fill in the gaps for each other to form completeness as a team.

Lesson 7: Owning Your Game 
 
Christina Gradillas 
Take a moment to stop and think. Is there any area this past month in which you’ve haven’t 
been true to your authentic self at work? C’mon…you know what I’m talking about! That 
time when you knew a solution but didn’t want to look rude. Maybe you downplayed a skill 
you have in order to fit in with the workgroup you were on. Or that time you know you should 
have spoken up in a meeting…but didn’t and the opportunity passed. 
Whatever it was, you didn’t “own it. ”  
It’s OK – we all do this! We’re wired to “fit in” and “go with the flow” and for women 
sometimes that means accidentally fitting into stereotypes other people have created for 
our gender. 
But as strong, powerful women, we can’t afford to think like this anymore. The workplace 
needs your contribution. I’ll say it again – Your workplace needs you! 
As with any team, the corporate workplace is filled with unique personalities. No one has it 
all – we fill in the gaps for each other and all work together towards a common goal. 
There are several parts to “owning your own game, ” beginning with:  
#1 – Knowing yourself. You’ve got to know your own strengths. What are you good at? What 
are you great at? What can you help your team or your leadership team with right now? 
#2 – Knowing your brand. What image do you currently project, and what image do you 
want to project. What do you want people to think about when your name comes to mind? 
Reliable? Sharp? Focused? Collaborative? Innovative? You get the idea. 
#3 – Knowing your boundaries. An important part of owning your game is knowing your 
boundaries at work. Where are you prepared to go beyond for the team, and where are 
areas you need to rein it in to guard your priorities? 
Finally, knowing when to speak up. The art of knowing when to speak up and when to be 
silent is truly an art. And many women simply default to not speaking up in order to 
“maintain an even strain. ” This doesn’t mean you need to speak up on or challenge 
everything. It means knowing the right time to weigh in, and not putting yourself on 
autopilot. 
And it goes without saying that you want to be a “go-to player” in your field.

I actually know of a woman who was very quiet, very reserved, always sat in the back of the 
room, and she actually didn’t do very well in her position. It was the moment she started to 
speak up. It was the moment that she stood up for herself, created her own boundaries, 
and stayed in her own lane. When you can stay in your own lane, there’s no one else who 
can compete with you. She ended up being one of the top producers in our company once 
she broke her own mold.  
We’ll go over all of this in the upcoming lesson.

## cw_mp_08_st_002 — Be Your Authentic Self

Type: video_and_reading · Source sequence: 2

Lesson 2 of 6
Be Your Authentic Self

If Pat were here, she might say, “Own your own game.” Don’t hold back who you are and what you can contribute to your team or as a leader in your workplace. In other words, be your authentic self. So what does it mean to be your authentic self?

Click on the buttons below to expand the full text.

Know Yourself

Know your strengths and don’t hide them. What are you good at? What are you great at? How can you help your team or your leadership team right now? What problems are you uniquely positioned to solve? Whose permission are you waiting for? Step into your own shoes, and be comfortable in your own skin! Know your weaknesses and work on them… but don’t apologize for them.

Know Your Brand

Your personal style is an extension of your authenticity. While it’s important to always be professional, authenticity is often expressed in attire or personal brand. When you see someone wearing pearls and heels, you have one perception of that person. When you see someone wearing slacks and sneakers, you make another perception about their personality. What do you want to project?
Aside from people’s outward perceptions, dressing authentically allows you to feel confident and comfortable in your professional skin. Do you prefer heels and tights? Do blazers make you feel like a rock star? Maybe you prefer sweaters and slacks. Think about how you want to be perceived and craft your professional image from there.
On an internal note, reflect on what aspects of your personal life you want to include as part of your image or personal brand and what you want to keep private.
Overall, a good question to ask yourself is: What words do I want people to associate with me when they think of my personal brand?

Set Boundaries

Similar to knowing your nonnegotiables, setting boundaries means knowing lines you will and won’t cross—here you’re willing to fake it till you make it and where you won’t (although no one is suggesting anyone be fake!). Think about what you’ll speak up about and what you won’t, when you’ll stay late and when you won’t.
Consider these your core values. Nothing is less authentic than allowing yourself to be pushed around outside of your boundaries at work.

Speak Up

Finally, express your voice. You don’t have to speak up on everything, but offer your opinions and expertise on the things that matter. No one will ever know the authentic you if you don’t let them! Sometimes we are our own worst censor.

Deep Dive: Finding the Right Fit

When we show up to the present moment with all of our senses, we invite the world to fill us with joy. The pains of the past are behind us. The future has yet to unfold. But the now is full of beauty simply waiting for our attention.

Finding the right fit at work is a must for both personal satisfaction and career satisfaction. When it comes to being unapologetically authentic, the concept of "fit" goes beyond just matching your skills with the job description; it involves aligning your talents, values, and work style with the broader team and company culture.

The first step is ensuring that your unique talents complement the needs of the overall team and ultimately, the organization. Every work team is a unique blend of individuals with diverse strengths, and the most effective teams are those where members’ skills are complementary.

Beyond the technical skills, cultural fit is equally important. Every organization and team has its own culture, which is shaped by shared behaviors, norms, and values. Finding a workplace where your personal values align with the company’s culture is essential for long-term success and happiness. For example, if you thrive in a collaborative environment but find yourself in a company that values competition over cooperation, or a remote, independent producer type of career, you may struggle to feel comfortable and perform at your best. Or if you have a more informal approach to your work and your company has a highly corporate, more formal environment (or vice versa), you’ll spend a large part of your time simply trying to “fit in,” rather than playing to your strengths.

Heading

To sum it up, finding the right fit at work is important for your long-term career development! When your talents are aligned with your role and the organizational culture, you’re more likely to receive opportunities for growth, whether through promotions or new responsibilities. A good fit means that you’re not just performing a job, but actively contributing to and growing with the organization.

Deep Dive: Finding the Right Fit 
 
Sue Nokes  
 
So, Pat always said to own your game. And that's very, very true, and I don't disagree with 
her, obviously. But your game has to fit in the broader team.  
 
Charles Barkley taught me about “fit.” Charles, when I worked at T Mobile, Charles and I 
had an opportunity to work several times together and he was harassing me one day, and I 
looked at him and I said, well, what about you and all that money you spent to get a title 
before you left the NBA? 
 
And Charles had given up like 10 million dollars of his own money to go get the final piece 
because they were trying to get him an NBA title before he hung up his sneakers. And he 
did, and he went and got a big-name guy who I won't mention, but I said, so how did that 
spin go for you, buddy? Where's that ring? 
 
And he looked at me and he said, I didn't know. He couldn't play without so-and-so. And 
the deal is, do you fit in the system? You see it all the time in sports that one team will 
purchase a great reliever pitcher and send them to another team and they don't turn out to 
do as well in their new home. It's about fit, and fit is important for you to be and own your 
own game. 
 
When you go look for jobs and you're assessing where you want to go or where your next job 
is going to be or what your next move is going to be, what's very important is – yep -- you 
need to know the official rules and regulations and what are their aspirations and what are 
their values and how do they say they want to run the joint. But there's always an unofficial 
set of rules. 
 
And what you have to figure out is, “Is it a good fit for me?” Because if it's not a good fit for 
me, I will never be all I can be in that environment, and I will never give them the best I can 
be in their environment. This was made very clear to me early on in my career when I was 
offered a COO job and a major healthcare company. 
 
And at the time I was an SVP , and I thought, “Oh my.” You know, women, glass ceilings. And 
for me, it was always about making a difference. Could I go someplace and make a 
difference? Would they know that I've been there? And so, I thought, “Well, healthcare. 
Who loves their healthcare provider?” So, here I go, and I go for the interview. 
 
I interviewed with the CEO. And the CEO was new to that role. And I came back, got my 
feedback, and my feedback was, “They love you. They like you, et cetera, et cetera. When 
can you go?”

And my answer was, “I can't.” Because I knew that that CEO and my values were not in 
harmony. That we would cause each other to stroke within the next 12-18 months. 
 
Because my focus was more on the customer and the frontline employee and theirs was 
not. Doesn't make that CEO wrong and me right, but it does alter where you will be more 
effective. So, I always tell people, don't chase the title. Don't try to take a job to just get your 
foot in the door. Really look around and say, “Do I fit?” 
 
One of the things that I found is most effective -- the most effective tool for a leader and 
your career is a mirror, because when things aren't going well, you need to pick it up and 
kind of look at it and say, “What is it that I know about myself that I'm not using, or what is it 
that I know about myself that I'm suppressing?” 
 
Because it's not everybody else. It's about 80/20 proposition. 80 percent of your career is 
your responsibility. What job is it you want? Have you told anybody you want it? But at first, 
when you'd ask women what they want to be when they grow up, they may sometimes say, 
“Well, you know, I might be able to be... ” and it would be the next level in the organization, 
because you didn't want to seem too aggressive and all those other negative words that 
come along with knowing what you want. 
 
Today, I have those conversations with women that say, “Well, I'm going to be a COO and if 
it's not here, it'll be someplace else.”  
 
I like it. Because it's okay to know what you want. And what are you willing to do that's not 
illegal, immoral, or indecent? And what are your values? And how are you going to use and 
maximize the skills you have and build a team that gets you where you want, and everybody 
can come with you. 
 
If you build the right team and you give them the proper training and tools and information, 
they need to do their job, and you are aligned around a goal...There is no better example 
than Pat Summit.  
 
So, when you go into that organization, again, profit, for-profit, non-profit, academia. What 
is it that you're aspiring to? And have you communicated that vision and are you all aligned 
on the goals? And this is what, what we're going to achieve together. And here are the rules 
by which we're going to play together. And when you're not, you hold each other 
accountable to that and amazing things can happen. But you got to know where you're 
going. 
 
Because good old Alice in Wonderland says, “If you don't know where you're going, any 
road will take you there.”

It's about, do you fit? And can you be you as a part of that team and be your true self and 
give it all you got.

## cw_mp_08_st_004 — The Inner Struggle

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 6
The Inner Struggle

What are some of the ways women suppress their authentic self? (Click on the icons to expand the text.)

Fear of Judgement

The fear of judgment, especially in male-dominated industries, can stifle authenticity. Women may hesitate to express their true opinions, showcase their skills, or even take on a leadership role to begin with out of fear of being judged or not taken seriously.

Conforming to Gender Norms

Many of us grew up with gender norms we tried to fit into and it’s hard to erase decades of learned behavior, and it can be doubly hard to put that aside when it comes to expectations in the workplace.

An Endless Balancing Act

Juggling multiple roles and responsibilities, particularly for working mothers, can lead to the pressure of a constant balancing act while striving to appear composed and unflustered, resulting in a polished facade.

Avoiding Conflict

Conditioned to prioritize harmony and avoid confrontation, women often suppress their authentic views to maintain a semblance of peace.

Over-apologizing

Women often have a tendency to over-apologize, even when not at fault. This habit may stem from a desire to avoid being perceived as demanding, leading to a less assertive presence in the workplace.

Downplaying Achievements

A reluctance to highlight personal achievements is a common phenomenon among women. The fear of being perceived as boastful or arrogant may result in downplaying accomplishments and suppressing the authentic acknowledgment of their contributions.

Of the examples listed above, which do you find yourself guilty of, and why?

Conclusion

In short, stay true to your vision. Take calculated risks. No one benefits when you curb your talents and skills, especially to fit inside a preconceived mold that was created decades ago. Let the best parts of yourself shine. Know how to navigate office etiquette, but also how to be real and valuable to your team.

Remembering Pat: Going into Labor on a Recruiting Visit 
 
 
Michelle Marciniak 
 
So, in 1991, I was a high school senior, and I was the National High School Player of the  
Year, heavily recruited by all the schools in the country. Pat Summit and the University of 
Tennessee was on their way to a home visit in Allentown, Pennsylvania, which is where I 
went to high school, where I lived. 
 
I was coming home a little bit late. Once I got home, I just noticed that Pat was just a little 
bit hurried, kind of in an odd, sort of in a rushed way. We sat down and Pat was nice, but 
she was preoccupied and a little bit distracted. Everything seemed rushed. Pat kept getting 
up, going to the bathroom, coming back, getting up, going to the bathroom, coming back. 
 
Finally, on Pat's like fourth or fifth trip to the bathroom, she came back and said, “Michelle, 
I'm afraid I'm going to have to cut this visit short.”  
 
She said, “My water broke getting off the plane. The baby is on its way.”  
 
And I just looked at her, and I said, “Like...now?”  
 
And she was like, “Uh, yeah, so we have to go.” 
 
My dad knew about this. He said “OK, let's get you to the hospital.” It was about three 
minutes away. She could go there, have a baby. Tyler would have been born in Allentown, 
Pennsylvania.  
 
But she said, no. In true Pat Summit fashion, she said, “I want to go home, I want to have 
this baby in Knoxville.” 
 
My brother and I hopped in the car, we got there, we got to the plane, all we could do was 
get them out of their car and try to get Pat and Mickie on the right planes.  
 
Well, they went through the gates, but the pilots wanted to make an emergency landing in 
Charlottesville, Virginia, where the University of Virginia is.  
 
Pat would not let them land the plane because Tennessee had just lost to Virginia when 
Knoxville was hosting the Final Four. 
 
So, Tennessee didn't get to the final four because they lost to Virginia. Pat said, “I'm not 
having this baby in Virginia. We're going to keep flying.”

So, they’re in the air, in the midst of, I think Mickie trying to take a whiskey shot because she 
had to calm her nerves. Pat was probably the calmer one to be really honest. But they flew 
the plane into Knoxville to an ambulance who took her into the airport, and they had a baby, 
Tyler. So that's how Tyler was born.
