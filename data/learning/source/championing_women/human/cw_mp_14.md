Supporting Female Leaders at Your Organization

Summarizing what we've covered...

Now knowing the hidden challenges and stereotypes women face, how can an organization intentionally support the women they manage and employ? Here are eight approaches and principles organizations can use to support female leaders at their own organization.

Summary

Be mindful of microaggressions and stereotypes, and address them when they occur.
Eliminate gender bias in the hiring process.
Make sure performance reviews are wholistic and don’t reward gender-biased attributes.
Offer flexibility and predictability with hours and workspace.
Avoid team-building activities or performance awards that favor one gender.
Ensure benefits are generous and conducive to families.
Have a zero-tolerance sexual harassment policy.
Include both genders on workplace committees.

Be mindful of microaggressions and stereotypes, and address them when they occur.

This is an everyday challenge, but doesn’t have to be a particularly disruptive one. The first step is making sure all employees are educated in common biases and stereotypes and aware that inclusivity of both genders is a priority. The next step is having a policy to address missteps quickly and succinctly. It could be as simple as stopping someone who is a chronic interrupter: “Let’s let Tamara finish her thought.” Or asking a male to take notes instead of volunteering the only female in the room: “Cindy did the recap for us last time. Juan, will you do the honors this time?”
Be especially mindful of microaggressions and stereotypes in meetings. Why? Because workplace culture is inherently learned, and nothing subconsciously sets the tone for interactions quite like team interactions in meetings. Stop both genders from comfortably falling into gender stereotyped roles.
Make sure all employees are educated in common biases and stereotypes and aware that inclusivity of both genders is a priority.

Eliminate gender bias in the hiring process.

Keeping gender bias out of the hiring process can be done in several ways.
Avoiding Gender-Biased Language
The language used in job descriptions can unintentionally discourage women from applying for certain positions, and research has shown that the choice of words and phrases can influence whether women perceive themselves as qualified for a role. To avoid this, organizations should develop clear and gender-neutral job descriptions that focus on qualifications, responsibilities, and skills rather than emphasizing traditionally masculine traits:
Avoid defaulting to one-sided pronouns, such as, “The ideal candidate will have strong organization skills. He will be able to decipher complex situations and make decisions quickly.”
Stay away from using male-oriented words like hunter mentality, maverick, and looking for an alpha-male mentality or someone who can bring home the bacon.
Avoid similarly female-biased descriptions like, “the ideal candidate is cheerful and friendly,” or “sensitive to other’s needs.”
Develop clear and gender-neutral job descriptions that focus on qualifications, responsibilities, and skills rather than emphasizing traditionally masculine traits.
Keeping Gender Out of Applicant Screenings
Blind hiring is an increasingly popular practice that removes identifiable information from resumes and job applications to minimize bias during the initial screening stages. This includes redacting names and other personal details that may indicate the candidate's gender. By evaluating candidates based solely on their skills, experience, and qualifications, it can help ensure that gender is not a factor in the early stages of the hiring process.
Conducting Structured Interviews
Unstructured interviews are more susceptible to bias, as they allow for inconsistencies and personal judgments. Structured interviews, on the other hand, involve asking all candidates the same set of questions in the same order and evaluating their responses against predefined criteria. This ensures each applicant is evaluated fairly, and avoids pitfalls like asking male candidates performance-driven questions while spending the majority of the time asking female applicants about their interpersonal skills, or vice versa.
Diversifying the Hiring Team
Include both sexes in interviews for open roles. This doesn’t always lend itself naturally if the two or three hiring managers are men only (or women only). But if possible, always have someone from the opposite sex sit in the interviews for perspective, even if they aren’t the decision-maker. Why? Women tend to notice things men don’t notice, and vice versa. Men tend to ask different questions and vet employees from a different perspective than women do.
Encouraging Equal Sexes to Apply
Did you interview any men for the open administrative assistant position? Did you interview any women for the open developer or accounting position? Specifically look for ways to add to the diversity of some of your non-diverse work teams, assuming qualifications are met.

Make sure performance reviews are wholistic and don’t reward gender-biased attributes.

Ensure performance reviews take into account a diversity of strengths and don’t only reward traditionally male characteristics. For example, grading someone on the following attributes:

Competitive
Confident
Decisive

Collaboration

Offer flexibility and predictability with hours and workspace.

Flexibility can come in a variety of forms, but specifically, this includes:
Flexible hours: When possible, offer flexible hours that allow individuals with caregiving responsibilities to work around them as needed.
Predictable schedules: Make sure work schedules are predictable and not subject to wild variations on short notice.
Flexible workspace: If the role permits, flexibility in offering remote, hybrid, or on-site options.

Avoid team-building activities or performance awards that favor one gender.

This can be tricky, and may take an offline examination of your current organization’s activities. (When in doubt, ask a committee of both genders.) For example, does your company plan traditionally masculine events like a retreat at a fly-fishing camp? This is not to say women don’t enjoy fishing—some do—and you can’t make generalizations about all women. But female employees often view masculine-leaning corporate outings, like cigar nights, poker tournaments, or rock-wall climbing, the same way male employees might view a company-sponsored spa getaway, musical, or art class.
Similarly, avoid gender-biased incentives, prizes, and rewards such as box tickets to a professional sporting event, or custom business suit for hitting a sales goal. It may be “tradition” at your organization, but creating a gender-equal workplace may require some new traditions. Consider more gender-neutral activities like an escape room, boating cruise, movie night, outdoor picnic, local tour, or food tasting. In addition, keep in mind team-building events or happy hours after work may be disadvantageous to those with families or to nondrinkers.

Ensure benefits are generous and conducive to families.

Benefits are important and their availability can often shape the careers of female caregivers.  This includes:
Paid maternity leave and spousal leave
Adoption benefits
Ample sick time/time off for caregiving responsibilities
Health insurance
Counseling to combat stress and burnout
Inclusion of on-site facilities such as nursing rooms, childcare, fitness areas etc.
Company events conducive to caregivers
Take, for example, a company with below average health insurance. A female leader was unable to qualify for other independent insurance options and pools because, technically, her employer offered coverage, so she was unable to see herself supporting a family long term on her company’s subpar insurance. A leader at another organization found that the insurance coverage was good, but insurance premiums were too high. She could cover herself, but the premiums weren’t conducive to supporting a family.

Have a zero-tolerance sexual harassment policy.

Anti-harassment policies are now mandatory and sexual harassment is illegal, but make sure employees know it won’t be tolerated on any level. This includes locker-room talk, derogatory slurs, or jokes even without females present. It doesn’t have to become a full-blown incident, but needs to be addressed. Handle any matters with confidentiality and minimal disruption.
This is important: Ensure female employees know that there won’t be social or career implications for bringing sexual harassment issues to an organization’s attention. The last thing any woman wants is to have to speak up because she was propositioned or harassed, then become the center of weeks of unwanted attention while the incident is investigated.

Include both genders on workplace committees.

For example, avoid having six women and two token males on the committee responsible for planning office events, outings, or culture. Aside from stereotyping women into gender roles, this eliminates valuable perspectives from both sides. It also sends the unintentional messages that women are better suited for nonbusiness, extracurricular work, and that the men have more valuable things to do with their time.

In summary, when it comes to creating an environment that supports female leaders, start where you are and do what you can.
While implementing the steps in this lesson may take time and intentionality, the best part is, these policies are good for everyone, not just women. We’ll say it again. Men enjoy flexible hours too! They want to be valued for the variety of skills they bring to the table too, and not just the traditionally masculine ones. And not every male would consider professional football tickets or a mountain bike a sufficiently motivating reward.

Conclusion: How to Create an Environment that Supports 
Female Leaders  
 
Michelle Marciniak 
OK, hopefully you’ve made it to the end with us and you’ve started to get a much clearer 
picture on how to create an environment that supports strong female leaders. 
To wrap it all up, here are a few basic principles to keep in mind.  
We talked about eliminating gender bias in the hiring process. The first step to supporting 
women is making sure they have an equal opportunity to work at your organization and that 
the metrics used to screen employees are balanced. When in doubt, include a mix of 
genders on your hiring committees – a good practice for any hires, not just female team 
members! 
Next, be mindful of microaggressions and gender stereotypes at your organization and 
begin to think of ways these can be eliminated at your organization. It may be as simple as 
better employee education. It may mean changing some traditions or committees, such as 
asking the women to bring food to the monthly potluck or being in charge of showers and 
other extracurriculars for employees. 
Make sure performance reviews don’t reward gender-biased attributes, such as 
“outspoken, ” “assertive, ” or “takes charge, ” without including qualities such as 
“interpersonal skills, ” “collaboration, ” “dependability,” or “attention-to-detail. ”   
To the best of your ability, offer flexibility with schedule and workspace. You also want to 
make sure you are offering benefits that are flexible and conducive to families. This goes 
without saying. 
Avoid team-building activities or “awards” that favor one gender, such as boxed tickets to a 
sporting event or mountain retreat to a fly-fishing resort. This one is huge! These activities 
and perks weren’t created with women in mind, any more than a night at the symphony or 
spa package as a team building-event would be attractive to most males. There are plenty 
of other gender-neutral activities and rewards to choose from, like escape rooms, game 
nights, vacation credits, or online gift cards.  
Finally, have a zero-tolerance policy on sexual harassment. Most organizations have 
embraced this fully, but make sure employees know it won’t be tolerated on any level. This 
includes locker-room talk, slurs, or jokes even without the opposite sex present. Such

actions contribute to the overall culture and can be insidious whether the actual female is 
present or not. Handle any matters quickly and confidentially. 
These policies are good for everyone, not just for women. And when everyone benefits, the 
team wins! 
So, as you complete this course, take a minute and think about where your organization 
falls. What steps can you take to get better? Which steps can you alone influence, and 
which might you need to suggest to others at your organization? 
Supporting women in leadership is a team effort. And Pat knew how to create winning 
teams.  
So, without further ado, let’s take a closer look.
