Lesson 8: In It for the Long-Haul  
 
Michelle Marciniak 
How many times have you heard it said before, “It’s a marathon, not a sprint!” or “Slow and 
steady wins the race?”  
Too many?  
Well, in the case of the workplace it couldn’t be more true. When it comes to your career, 
you have to pace yourself for “the long haul, ” as they say. 
A lot of people don’t think of it this way – they dive in straight out of college – or maybe 
you’ve just started a new career and a new chapter and you’re rearing to go. Long nights, 
stolen weekends, a little here and a little there. No detail is too small, no work trip 
unimportant. 
This works for a while. Maybe it even works for years. But it does catch up with you. It 
catches up to all of us! 
The fact is, there are a few strategic principles to staying in it for the long haul and making 
your career go the distance. This involves picking your battles and avoiding burnout. 
First, picking your battles. You have to know which battles to fight, and which to leave to 
someone else. Which projects need 100% of your time and energy, and how to move on or 
delegate. There is an art to this – it does not come naturally. 
Second, avoiding burnout. According to research, women are statistically more likely to 
report burnout than men – nearly half of all women surveyed by some reports. And 
according to the American Psychological Association, 76% of adults said they have 
experienced health impacts due to stress in the prior month.  
76 percent! 
Women are at a higher risk than men for several reasons – one is the headwinds they face 
in their careers, which we’ve already outlined here. The second is the fact that women are 
more likely to take on “hidden work” – work that is prep work or behind the scenes, work 
that contributes to the office culture but not the bottom line. And the third is the simple fact 
that women face more caregiving responsibilities at home than men – traditionally, that is. 
When you go home and your work doesn’t “stop” and you keep running until bedtime, it’s 
no wonder you’re at a higher risk.

So, what can you do to avoid burnout and stay in it for the long haul? An ounce of 
prevention is worth a pound of cure here. 
The first is being aware of the stages of burnout and know that it has the tendency to creep 
up. Stress manifests itself in three categories: 
Acute stress is short-term, like a stressful month or two buying a house or working on a 
one-time project. 
Episodic stress is short term, but frequent, like a tax accountant during busy season or 
realtor during the peak summer months. 
Finally, chronic stress is long-term or ongoing stress that lasts longer than 6 months.  
You do not want to live in the chronic stress zone, especially as mother, wife, or simply as a 
human being prioritizing their health and well-being. 
So, what can you do to avoid burnout? 
Be mindful of stress and how it manifests itself in your life and your workday. All types of 
stress are not the same, some are good, some are bad, but too much of any of it will affect 
your health and ultimately your career. 
Next, identify triggers. The farther you go in your career, the better you’ll be able to 
recognize your own personal triggers and how to avoid them. 
Speak up about the stress. Know when to speak up and when to power through. Don’t be 
afraid to stand up for the boundaries you need to keep things on the rails in your career. 
Remember – marathon, not a sprint! 
And finally, make de-stressing an intentional part of your workday and work week. You 
heard me – it’s not going to come naturally! Our human instinct is to either be “on, ” running 
around, multi-tasking, trying to do all the things, or “off, ” where we are in front of the TV at a 
long hard day, making poor diet choices, or worse – too tired to leave the house or engage 
in time with family.  
De-stressing can look like several different things to different people, and we won’t get in to 
them all in this video – sometimes it’s intentional breaks during the day. Sometimes it’s a 
20 min walk after work to reset your mind. Sometimes it’s a hot shower with some 
aromatherapy, time at the gym, happy hour with friends, spending time in the evening 
reading to your son or daughter. Whatever it is for you, these things are natural speed 
bumps in your day for a reason to slow things down and calm you down to keep you 
optimized and ready for the next workday.

So, with that in mind, let’s learn more about how we can win the marathon, or in Pat’s case, 
the championship, without sacrificing the things we enjoy and the unique things that make 
you, YOU. 
You’ve got this! Let’s take a closer look.

## cw_mp_09_st_002 — Pick Your Battles

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 2

Lesson 2 of 6
Pick Your Battles

Heading

You can’t fight every battle. You just cannot. When it comes to women in the workplace overall, and your career specifically, know which battles to fight and which to leave alone. Grow your long-term perspective. This has several advantages as you progress as a female leader.

Strategic Decision-Making

Knowing when to pick your battles involves assessing the importance of an issue, understanding the potential impact on your career, and considering the long-term consequences of engaging in a particular issue.

Preserving Energy and Focus

Engaging in every battle can be exhausting and detract from your overall focus and productivity. Knowing when to pick your battles allows you to preserve your energy for the issues that truly matter.

Demonstrating Emotional Intelligence

Emotional intelligence plays a big role in workplace dynamics. Choosing battles requires recognizing and managing emotions effectively, leading to more constructive interactions between you and your peers.

Distinguishing Between Major and Minor Issues

Not every workplace issue requires the same level of your attention. Period. Major issues that align with your core values or significantly impact your work should be prioritized.

Assessing the Long-Term Impact

Consider the long-term impact of a potential conflict. Will the issue at hand have lasting consequences on your career, relationships, or the overall work environment?

Evaluating Personal Investment

If the matter directly affects your responsibilities, well-being, or professional growth, it may be worth addressing. However, for issues that are more peripheral to your role, choosing to step back might be the wiser option.

## cw_mp_09_st_004 — Handling Burnout

Lesson 4 of 6
Handling Burnout

Deep Dive: Guardrails for Burnout

When we show up to the present moment with all of our senses, we invite the world to fill us with joy. The pains of the past are behind us. The future has yet to unfold. But the now is full of beauty simply waiting for our attention.

When it comes to being “in it for the long haul,” one classic challenge female leaders face is a greater susceptibility to burnout. Burnout can look quite different for each individual, and a lot of it can be prevented on the front end with a little intentionality. First, you want to stop and think about your overall career goals and where your career “guardrails” are, what you will and won’t do. This is important, because it’s easy to eagerly accept long hours, salary changes, or a change in commute in the wake of an exciting new promotion. But without an honest assessment of your personal guardrails, women often find themselves landing squarely in the burnout zone. Don’t let this be you!

Your guardrails may change over the course of your career, and that’s OK! The important thing is to stop and think about where you are now and where you want to be. If, 10 years from now, you’re doing exactly the same thing as you are now, is that OK? What are the “non-negotiables” in your career when it comes to salary, work hours, long weekends, travel, location, commute, flexibility, etc.?
Some things are OK for a season, but you want to make sure you understand where your personal boundaries are as your career progresses.

Deep Dive: Recognizing Burnout

Because of caregiving duties and other factors we mentioned earlier, women are often more susceptible to burnout than men. The signs are subtle and easy to miss, but if you learn to course-correct before hitting the ditch, you’ll go farther and be able to perform better in your career.

Heading

Burnout is more than just feeling stressed or tired. It's a state where you feel constantly emotionally and physically exhausted. You might become cynical, detached from work, and feel like you're not accomplishing anything. Potential causes include:

Excessive workload

Lack of control

Unclear expectations

Lack of recognition

Poor work-life balance

Inadequate resources

The best way to stop burnout is before it starts. This involves keeping a close eye on yourself and your emotions when you start feeling the first signs of stress.

Deep Dive: Guardrails For Burnout 
 
Patty Hoppenstedt 
Okay, we’ve touched on things you need to keep in mind in order to be “in it for the long 
haul. ” Now let’s take a closer look at burnout. 
When it comes to being in it for the long haul, first, begin with the end in mind. What do 
YOU need to “go the distance?” 
Stop and think about that for a second. Is it a certain salary level? Maybe it’s earning 
enough to afford childcare, or a job that offers remote options for work-life balance? I 
s it a position located within a certain area of your home, or are you willing to move? How 
much are you willing to commute? What about retirement options, like a 401K plan? 
Start by making a list of where you want to be in 10 years. What does that job look like?  
You may want to split things into two categories, what the “dream version” of that job looks 
like and what are non-negotiables for you to be working in 10 years. 
The dream version can include things like a certain title, or the goal of having a certain 
position in a certain industry. The non-negotiable side may include things like a minimum 
salary or how much travel the job involves. 
There are things we do when we’re starting off in our careers that we won’t do when we’re 
older. In your 20s you may not have kids, so working unconventional hours or traveling on a 
moment’s notice doesn’t matter. You may not be married or have a house so you might be 
willing to move for that big opportunity.  
 
Things usually change the further we get into our career. You learn what’s important to you, 
and what you will and don’t compromise on. Even if a spouse or kids aren’t a consideration, 
you usually have other obligations or interests that influence your deal-breakers in the 
workplace.  
Maybe you’re an adrenaline junkie who thrives in an unexpected, fast-paced environment 
and you know you like the element of excitement in your career. Maybe you’re the opposite 
– you prefer professional but predictable, with a steady course. 
When you know where you’re going – the end result – and you know your boundaries, 
navigating your career options as they present themselves becomes a little clearer.

This is important, because to avoid burnout you need to know where your boundaries are 
and how to safeguard them when you find yourself getting pushed close to the line.
