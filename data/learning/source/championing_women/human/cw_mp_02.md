Lesson 1: Women at Work: The Current Situation

Morgan Vance

When Pat started her career in the 1970s, less than half of women over the age of 16 were working outside the home. In today’s workforce, nearly 50 years later, however, women have emerged as a large part of the U.S. economy.

When it comes to women in the workforce, some may wonder, “What’s the big deal? We’re equal, aren’t we?”

Yes and no.

Women may be equal on paper in certain areas and by certain laws, but a multitude of obstacles still face women trying to succeed in the workforce and advance their career. This includes factors like:

Unequal pay: In her first season, Pat earned a little under $9,000 for her first position as head basketball coach at the University of Tennessee, during a time when many male counterparts were making twice as much.

Unequal representation in certain industries: In Pat’s own field of coaching, today only one-third of all head coaches are women, while two-thirds are men.

Caregiving responsibilities: It’s no secret that working mothers (like Pat) often face additional hurdles. The lack of affordable childcare in addition to domestic duties often forces women to choose between career advancement and family.

Underrepresentation in leadership and gender stereotypes and biases are just a few more ways women lag behind men in advancement opportunities and perceptions in the workforce.

So, what can we do about it? The first step is understanding the current situation and how to navigate the current climate.

What makes things challenge for many female leaders is that many of the complexities of the status quo facing women often feel difficult and hard to prove. After all, it isn’t a standard topic of conversation. Women have learned to persevere in spite of it all. At best…no one talks about it.

That’s what makes the upcoming lessons so valuable! You’ll learn about some of the pitfalls of the current workforce for women and how we got here, shining a light on some of the solutions for women and would-be leaders in their careers.

As a female leader and working mother, I’ve found it’s best to be up front and transparent about our needs so we can perform our roles to the best of our abilities. When one area falls out of balance, other parts of our life do as well, and it impacts our work. What I’ve found helpful during my career is having strong female mentors to help advise on difficult situations while supporting and encouraging my growth as a leader.

In this course you’ll learn to face challenges with strength and resilience like Pat did and specifically how to view some of the unique obstacles facing women in their fields.

## cw_mp_02_st_002 — But We're Equal, Aren't We?

But We're Equal, Aren't We?

But We're Equal, Aren't We?

Yes…and no. Many people look at three main statistics to prove how far women have come:

Women now make up roughly half the U.S. workforce.
Anti-sexual harassment and discrimination policies are now federal law.
Maternity leave is now required by law, even if unpaid.

“So, what’s the big deal?” you might ask. “Why are women still complaining that things are unequal, or that they face obstacles?”

The truth is, there are several well-documented inequities that continue to face female workers in the modern workplace.

Unequal Pay

The first is the pay gap, which hasn’t changed all that much in the last decade. While the Equal Pay Act of 1963 made it illegal to blatantly discriminate based on gender (up until then, employers could openly advertise “men’s positions” at a higher rate than “women’s positions” in the local advertisements), there is still a way to go to achieve true equity in compensation. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, women continue to earn 82 percent of what their male counterparts make in similar positions.²

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, women continue to earn 82 percent of what their male counterparts make in similar positions.

Unequal Representation in Certain Fields

The second is the underrepresentation of women in certain fields—mostly the more technical and often higher-paying STEM fields (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Math). This is coupled with an overrepresentation of women in traditionally lower-paying fields like teaching or administrative assistants.

Even among fields where women are firmly represented overall, like healthcare, disparities exist between men and women in higher-educated roles. For example, dental hygienists are 93 percent female,³ compared to only comprising a little over 37% of dentists. And 86 percent of nurses are female,⁴ compared to only making up 37% of the physician workforce. Even in Pat’s own field of coaching, today only one-third of all head coaches are women, while two-thirds are men.⁵ Similarly, men make up 83 percent of all sports announcers, and only 17 percent of these roles are filled by women.⁶

The imbalance of women across the workforce as a whole leads to its own set of obstacles and challenges. You could argue that it’s hard for women to feel equal when they’re always a minority in an occupation that is two-thirds male. And it’s tough to choose a male-dominated career when women aren’t encouraged to choose a career field that gender biases make unappealing at a young age (e.g., being in the math club is “unfeminine” or makes you “intimidating”).

The imbalance of women across the workforce as a whole leads to its own set of obstacles and challenges.

## cw_mp_02_st_003 — Gender Stereotypes and Biases

Type: reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 5
Gender Stereotypes and Biases

Caregiving Responsibilities

Next is the challenge women have traditionally faced around caregiving duties. First, balancing the time off women need for maternity and post-maternity, often with multiple children. The Pregnancy and Discrimination Act of 1978 made it illegal to discriminate against female employees due to pregnancy or childbirth, and the 1993 federal Family Medical Leave Act (FMLA) now offers women 12 weeks of maternity leave with job protection (although whether the leave is paid or not is still up to the employer). Women have historically been the main caregiver for said children as they grow through the elementary years, often making them the first responder for sick days, sleepless nights, field trips, evening meals, extracurriculars, etc.

In spite of 21st century efforts to balance this caregiving load, modern studies show women work 4 hours and 25 minutes each day domestically outside of their corporate careers, compared with men putting in 1 hour and 23 minutes.⁷ This makes women much more of a candidate for burnout than their male counterparts. All this is coupled with the fact that childcare in most states is expensive, making many women question whether returning to work is advantageous financially.

There’s also what’s known as the motherhood penalty. Studies by organizations such as the Harvard Business Review have shown as much as an $11,000 gap in what mothers were offered in starting salary, as opposed to their childless female counterparts, which just widens the 82 percent female pay gap.⁸

According to one study, women work 4 hours and 25 minutes each day domestically outside of their corporate careers, compared with men putting in 1 hour and 23 minutes.

Underrepresentation in Leadership

One of the more complex issues in today’s workforce is the overall underrepresentation of women in leadership positions. An annual study on women in the workforce by McKinsey and LeanIn found that among C-Suite leadership today, 75 percent are male, while only 25 percent are female. Factoring in racial minorities, only 1 out of 20 C-Suite leaders are women of color.⁹

The reasons for this leadership gap, which we’ll get to in upcoming lessons, are complex. Many have traditionally pointed to childbearing and caregiving as a reason women opt out of the workplace before they reach leadership levels, but 21st century research has started to add more variables to the mix.

Gender Stereotypes and Biases

This brings us to the world of gender biases. Unconscious gender bias still remains a considerable barrier to women’s career advancement, but can be difficult to pinpoint and address.

For example, studies have shown characteristics such as “assertiveness,” “confidence” and “out-spokenness” tended to be viewed positively in men, while carrying a negative connotation among women. Another example, might be a job posting for an administrative assistant requesting candidates be “always smiling and pleasant” or that a leadership position require someone who is “no-nonsense,” “leads with a firm hand,” or is “willing to speak up” (with women displaying such assertive attributes being negatively stereotyped at a higher rate than men).

Unconscious gender bias still remains a considerable barrier to women’s career advancement, but can be difficult to pinpoint and address.

Cracking the Glass and Closing the Gap

In short, we haven’t reached true equality in the modern workplace simply by throwing women into the mix and shuffling the card deck. And the points above haven’t gone unnoticed among top employers and organizations committed to championing women in the workforce and promoting strong female leaders.

So what can employers do specifically to encourage female leadership and support women on their own teams? We’ll discuss that and more in the upcoming lessons.
