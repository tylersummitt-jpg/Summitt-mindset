Lesson 12: Thriving Together: Women Supporting Women  
 
Petra Dunn 
What would the world be like if we all supported each other? If we saw our colleagues as 
comrades-in-arms and not competition or obstacles in our way? 
Maybe you’ve had these thoughts before too. How can women do a better job of 
championing and supporting other women, especially in leadership roles? 
Pat would be the first to tell us all to intentionally celebrate the victories of other women in 
the workplace and have an “abundance” mindset. Eliminate any trace of a “scarcity” 
mindset that might propel you to view other co-workers – male or female – as competition 
in your own organization. What a joy it is to celebrate others who are navigating the same 
twists and turns of leadership and doing it well. Adopt a “You Win, I Win, ” mindset! 
You also want to ensure that you include strong female leaders on your support team…and 
we all need a support team! This means intentionally creating your own championship 
team with its own set of players to help you in your own career journey.  
There are a few people you want to include: 
First, “Work BFFs” – these are the people who are with you in the daily grind, who bring a 
smile to your face and make work a pleasure. Or maybe they provide a much-needed 
comic relief when you need to unwind, reminding you everything is going to be OK. These 
types of team members are everywhere and may not share your exact title or status and 
that’s OK.  
Next are Comrades-in-Arms – these are colleagues who are “in the trenches” with you. 
They may share a similar job description or be in a partnership role. 
Coaches are those who may or may not work at your business but have worked in your 
industry or in the workplace for many years and can offer encouragement. 
Finally, mentors. Everyone needs mentors! A mentor isn’t a peer or a “coach. ” Their job isn’t 
to make you feel good, but rather to give you insight and pass on experience. Mentors can 
be male or female and are usually a little older than you are. They might have experience in 
an industry you are trying to break into or a skill you can learn to acquire.  
We all know what it feels like to have someone who believes in us, someone who is on your 
side.  I was fortunate to have some really strong mentors along my career path.  Women

that showed me by their own example that success is more than just my career, that I need 
to use my visibility to lead in the community beyond the workplace.  
You might ask a mentor certain questions like:  
How did you get to where you are today? 
Are there specific skills or experiences you recommend I focus on? or 
What strategies do you recommend for professional development? 
We’ll go over all this in the upcoming lesson.

## cw_mp_13_st_002 — Creating Your Championship Team

Type: video_and_reading · Source sequence: 2

Lesson 2 of 7
Creating Your Championship Team

There are several types of players you want on your support team, and we’ll go over a few below.

The Support Team

There are several types of players you want on your support team. Here's a few:
Work BFFs
Comrade-in-arms
Coaches
Mentors

Summary

These are just a few examples of the great foundations to build your Championing Team.

Work BFFs

Work BFFs, if you will, are the unsung heroes of the daily grind. They're the ones who understand your coffee addiction and can read your facial expressions during meetings. These people simply make you feel better! They can be a refreshing break in your weekly routine, giving you the energy to keep going. So don’t skip out on making friends or lunch buddies at work—just make sure they are the type of positive and professional friend who will keep you moving forward.

Comrade-in-arms

Comrade-in-arms are important peers who share similar professional goals or who are your partners on particular projects. Whether you're planning an ambitious marketing campaign, rolling out a new product, or doing something you’ve never done before, having this type of peer in your tribe makes the journey more enjoyable. Comrade-in-arms help keep you accountable and can offer much-needed support in the trenches. Plus, success is sweeter when celebrated with those who truly get the hustle.
Pat’s comrade-in-arms were undoubtedly two of her assistant coaches Mickie DeMoss and Holly Warlick, whom she considered as good friends. The three were inseparable, and Pat often relied upon them to keep her grounded and in check.

Coaches

Coaches are just that—those who may or may not work for your organization, but have a history in the workplace and can offer encouragement. A coach is there to offer support and point out potential potholes in the career road to avoid, which a work BFF or comrade-in-arms may not necessarily see from their vantage point. Think of them as an encourager or cheerleader with an eye for critical thinking.

Mentors

Last, but certainly not least, are mentors. A mentor isn’t a peer or a coach, they’re someone who has gone before you in your industry—maybe even at your own organization or one similar. They’re not here to be a cheerleader, but rather to pass on experience. Imagine you're facing a workplace decision that feels impossible. Or you’re wondering how to navigate a new technology rollout you haven’t used before, and the team isn’t on board. This is where mentors come in. They've been there, done that, and can help you navigate the twists and turns.
Mentors can be male or female, and balanced leaders usually have both on their support team. Cross-generational mentors in particular bring a unique perspective that younger generations might readily miss. A mentor might have prior experience in a role or a particular skill set to teach that will help move you forward in your career path. It's not about finding a friend, it's about finding someone who's been through the trenches and can offer valuable insight and advice.

Deep Dive: Finding a Mentor

A mentor is someone you trust, admire and respect who will give you honest and invaluable career advice.
Sample questions you might ask a mentor include:

What steps did you take to advance in your career, and are there specific skills or experiences you recommend I focus on?

How do you approach setting career goals, and are there specific milestones you recommend I aim for in my current role and beyond?

How did you build and leverage your professional network throughout your career? Any tips for effective networking?

As a successful professional, how have you managed to maintain a healthy work-life balance, and do you have any advice for finding that balance?

What strategies do you use for continuous professional development? Can you provide feedback on my current performance and suggest areas where I can improve?

Heading

These are just a few. The sky’s the limit as to how much gold you can mine for with a mentor. If they’re female, for example, you may ask them, "Have you faced gender-related challenges in your career, and if so, how did you overcome them? Any advice for navigating gender bias in the workplace?"
If they hold/held a position of senior leadership you might ask, "What leadership skills do you believe are essential for success in our industry today and how can I strengthen these skills?" If they are a self-help junkie, ask, “What strategies do you use for continuous professional development?" You get the picture. As one of the first in her industry to smash through the glass ceiling, many colleagues and team members considered Pat herself to be a mentor.

Deep Dive: Finding a Mentor 
 
 
Sue Nokes 
 
You talked to me about mentors and the importance of mentors. I think it is very important 
to have somebody that you can trust, that you admire and respect, that will give you the 
honest opinion and assessment of your performance.  
 
I've seen a couple of things happen. First of all, we often will ask someone to be our mentor 
because of their position within the organization that you're a part of, whether that's for 
profit in academia or in business. 
 
And often it's not a good choice, and the reason it's not a good choice is because they don't 
see you perform every day. So, I was always getting asked if I would be somebody's mentor. 
And it isn't that you don't want to, it's a matter of, “I really can't help you if I'm not in the 
meetings with you, and I can't see how you interact with others or if I'm not around when 
you're leading your people.” 
 
So, finding a mentor who can truly assess your performance and sees you in your day-to-
day operations is important. Number one. Number two. Finding somebody that will tell you 
when your baby's ugly. So, as you're thinking about your career. Remember, it is yours. You 
own it. 80/20 proposition. 80 percent is your responsibility to grow and develop your skills 
and deliver goods so that the organization provides an environment that's conducive for 
your growth and development. 
 
But don't look for people that are like you. Know yourself. Take an inventory. But understand 
what your gifts are and what your specialties are. And then look for mentors or people that 
you can grow and learn from that cover what I would call your deficits or flat sides of your 
tire so that they're not necessarily who you want to hang out with and then you want to go 
on vacation with, but they are someone that has a skill or a talent that you need to learn 
and develop from.  
 
So that's who you surround yourself with. Don't be intimidated by folks that are smarter 
than you. Learn from them. Surround yourself with excellent talent.

Lesson 12: How to Receive Feedback 
 
Christina Reckard 
 
OK, we’ve talked about mentors and coaches. Now let’s talk about another key component 
of thriving in the workplace – how to receive feedback. If you have a coach or mentor in 
your corner specifically, you want to get as much feedback from them as possible.  
 
Knowing how to receive feedback is a skill most people never master. It’s something too 
many people never seek out to begin with – some people run from feedback altogether! But 
if you’re going to thrive in your career, knowing how to be coachable, how to receive and 
implement feedback from your leader or peers is essential.  
 
First, we’re talking about solicited feedback, or feedback coming from a place you trust. 
We’re not talking about barbs or criticisms made in the heat of an argument or anything on 
social media. Even if it’s unexpected feedback from a colleague you otherwise trust, but 
who surprises you with the timing, if their heart is in the right place, it counts. Ask yourself, 
is this someone I like, trust or respect? If the answer to any of these three is yes, you want 
to be intentional about how you process the feedback.  
 
I remember a time when the executive team I was a part of was tasked with providing peer-
to-peer feedback in order to help us grow as a team. One by one, we each gave and 
received both positive feedback and areas for growth. I found providing positive feedback 
was easier for me than providing negative feedback. I tried to keep that in mind when it was 
my turn to receive feedback so that I could receive it in the constructive way it was 
intended. One key takeaway I received was that I need to be more inquisitive in meetings. 
That piece of feedback sticks with me to this day and helps to keep me mindful when 
participating in meetings.  
 
You may get feedback on a regular basis at your job or you may not. Some people go their 
entire careers without getting feedback, or only get it once a year in their annual review. So 
here are a few tips. 
 
The first step sounds obvious, but we need to listen to the person giving the feedback. 
Those who are practiced know how to accept feedback with grace. They don’t fall apart. 
They don’t lose their cool. Learn how to hold feedback with neutrality and open-
mindedness in the moment in which you receive it.  
 
Don’t interrupt. Don’t get defensive. Remember that feedback is often hard to give, and it 
might be your only chance to hear what they have to say. 
 
The person giving the feedback might deliver it in an emotional or clunky way, and that’s 
OK. They may have a string of faults and inconsistencies themselves, and that’s OK – save

it for another time. You’re not here to argue or talk about their shortcomings, you’re here to 
get feedback on you and how you can grow.  
 
Be mindful of your body language when receiving feedback. Are your arms crossed 
defensively? Are you scowling or slouching? Do you look afraid of what they’re going to 
say?  
 
Remember, you want to exude grace and poise, not defensiveness and hostility. We all 
know people who don’t take feedback well, or who no one wants to approach on a 
particular subject. Don’t let that person be you! 
 
Next, you want to clarify the feedback. Repeat back to them what you heard them say, just 
to make sure that you’re receiving the right message. 
 
Thank them for their feedback. This person is trusting you with the maturity to be 
coachable and learn from whatever is being said. 
 
Then you want to process the feedback. Here’s a tip – feedback can often be hard to 
process in the moment. Sometimes it can take days or even weeks to fully sink in. That’s 
OK! The point is that it sinks in, or whatever element of truth in the feedback can be 
received and help you to grow. Resist the urge to throw out feedback immediately if you 
don’t think it’s valid. 
 
If you don’t understand or aren’t sure quite how to process something, reach out to a friend 
or mentor you trust. Tell them of the feedback you received and that you aren’t sure what 
to make of it. This isn’t to gossip or debate the feedback, but to get more clarity on how you 
can learn from it. It should be a mentor or someone you trust who can give you an honest 
opinion on what was said and how you can learn something productive. 
 
Finally, you want seek out feedback on a regular basis. That’s right – seek it out! Don’t wait 
until your annual review once a year or for your leader to initiate the conversation. Ask for 
feedback on a regular basis. Not only from your leader, but also from other peers you trust.  
 
You want to learn to ask for targeted feedback – rather than saying “Do you have any 
feedback for me?” try asking things like “Do you have any feedback on my communication 
style?” or “Do you have any feedback from me on my design elements?” You’re more likely 
to get feedback on a particular subject if you invite someone to speak up in that specific 
area. 
 
The truth is, how coachable you are – how well you receive feedback overall and can learn 
from it – goes a long way in how others perceive your professionalism and can even affect 
the rise and fall of your career.

## cw_mp_13_st_005 — You Win, I Win

Type: video_and_reading · Source sequence: 5

Lesson 5 of 7
You Win, I Win

Heading

Finally, but not to be missed, learn to intentionally celebrate the victories of other women in the workplace and have an abundance mindset. Eliminate the scarcity mentality that might propel you to view other women as rivals for a limited number of positions. Other women aren’t competition at work; they can be allies, if you let them. There are several ways to rally around your fellow sisters in the workplace:

Do they know of a good mixer or networking opportunity in your area? Where do they meet other people in your industry? Did you just have a great experience at a recent conference you’d recommend? Share the knowledge!

Share networking strategies

Are you a whiz at design or excel spreadsheets? Know someone who is struggling in an area you can help with, or vice versa? Consider a monthly lunch or skill swap to help educate fellow women in the workplace.

Promote skill development

Did someone just hit a huge milestone or work anniversary? How about a major promotion? Take a moment to help them celebrate! Victories at work are more meaningful when they’re shared.

Celebrate achievements

Maybe someone you know just went through a hard season at work. Or maybe you’re facing a new software rollout or department reorganization and know someone a floor over who went through the same thing. Ask for advice, and also pass along your own wisdom.

Share similar experiences

Look for ways to encourage your organization’s support for women in the workplace, from hiring to mentorship programs or peer group committees.

Champion equal opportunities

Know of a job posting at your organization that’s the perfect fit for a woman you know? Refer her! How about someone who would be a great candidate for a particular committee? Offer the recommendation!

Look for growth opportunities

Give Back

In summary, as you build your tribe and support system at work, be on the lookout for ways to give back. There are people who have helped you get to where you are today, or are mentoring you as a female leader. Take a minute to think about how you can pay it forward with your own talents and skills.

Be the cheerleader someone else needs. Be the resource someone else can count on. Who can you be a reference for, or who needs an introduction? Pat would be the first to reinforce the power of the team in achieving any goal, as opposed to being a lone ranger or individual performer in your career. Remember the old African proverb, “If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together.”

Deep Dive: Supporting other Women: An Abundance Mindset 
 
 
Sue Nokes 
 
We tend to want “good news” feedback. We like a lot of feedback as long as they're telling 
me how wonderful I am. And I have found that to be more true of women than men. And 
part of that comes from the way we were cultured. And it is changing generationally, thank 
heavens, but that starts with a time when I was coming up in my career that women weren't 
cultured in the same way. 
 
We did not learn to play on teams like guys did. Little boys had t-ball teams and you would 
put them on a team and you'd prop that little ball up before they could hit a moving target. 
And they learned that it's OK if Johnny hits a home run today and Timmy hits it next week 
because we're all going for ice cream, and we're all going to win and it's a great thing. 
 
Women didn't have that. So, when you went to school, it was about: “How did you look? 
And were you pretty? And were the boys going to like you? Who's going to be on the 
cheerleading squad where you're competing with other women? Who's going to be the 
homecoming queen?” When there's typically one. Then you get into a business 
environment where there aren't that many women. 
 
And if you look at the numbers, and particularly as you get into director level and above 
positions, again, numbers are increasing and it's looking good. And I'm not suggesting we 
put anyone in a position who's not qualified for the position, but there are fewer examples 
and role models to follow. That's part of the issue -- who is around for you to emulate and 
learn from and apply to your leadership style and goals. This lack of opportunities to be 
cultured or to understand the rules of the game, if you will, in a business or an organization 
manifests itself in various ways.  
 
I've watched and studied women in meetings and men in meetings. Men will argue and 
debate and at the end of the debate, smack each other on the back and say, let's go for a 
burger and a beverage. Women leave, and we tend to take it more personally and we take it 
as a personal attack and then won't talk to that person or we will say terrible things about 
that person to others for six months to a year or longer because we haven't learned to 
separate and attack issues and not individuals. It's about, “We're solving a problem. We're 
dealing with an issue. We leave the individual their respect.”  
 
In organizations, it's sometimes tough to not see other women as competition because we 
feel like there are fewer opportunities and that women are only going to get a certain 
percentage of those opportunities. Instead of looking at it as: “What can I do? How do I 
equip myself? How do I give myself the skills and the reputation? And how do I deliver the 
goods -- in other words, get results, so that people see me in that role.”

So, we tend to look throughout organizations, again, in academia, in business, in non-
profits, and we see another person, and we think there's only a handful of us that are going 
to get to the next level, and we tend to then not work together, but we often don't optimize 
each other in our strengths because we see each other as competition.

Remembering Pat: Being “Coachable” as a Coach 
 
 
Joan Cronan 
 
You know, when I think of mentorship, I think of Pat and how she helped others and how 
others helped her. But one of the things to be mentored or be received mentorship, you 
have to want it and you have to want to keep learning.  
 
One year we were playing in a regional finals and we lost to a team that we never thought 
we would lose to. I was disappointed. The team was disappointed. I got home before 
sunrise the next morning my phone rang, and it was Pat Summit. And she said, “Joan, it's 
my fault. We lost that game.”  
 
I said, “No Pat, it wasn't your fault. They played really well.”  
 
She said, “No, they ran an offense that I didn't understand.”  
 
Now, this is a coach who has won many championships. This is a coach that's considered 
one of the best coaches in the world. And she's saying, “I didn't understand.”  
 
And you know what she said? “I'm going to learn that offense and I'm never going to have 
somebody beat me with it again.” And she called a male counterpart at that stage, and he 
came in and spent about a week with us. 
 
You know, Pat had to be willing to admit she was wrong and didn't know something. This is 
such a winning coach, but she did it. She was willing to listen to other people and keep 
learning.  
 
And you know what? We never lost to that offense again.
