Introduction

In the world of sports, there are many trailblazers who leave a personal mark on the game—with Pat Summitt at the very top of that list. Her name became synonymous with excellence in women's sports and leadership. Summitt's journey also intersects with a pivotal moment in history known as Title IX—a federal law that transformed the landscape of women's extracurriculars in the U.S.

The impact of Title IX resulted in a cultural shift that exceeded the realm of sports and ushered in a new philosophy of gender equity across the country, just as more and more women began to enter the workforce in the 1980s and 1990s.

Robin Roberts 
 
All of us know strong, successful women who have made us who we are today. Whether it

Pat wouldn’t have considered herself a feminist – she considered herself a “subversive. ” 
That is, someone immersed in a man’s world at the time, who was slowly re-defining the

she smashed the glass ceiling over and over again, paving the way for other women to

she be paid equally with the other male coaches in her profession.  
Pat was also the first person to be an Olympic winner both as a player and a coach. She 
was “Coach of the 20th Century, ” in addition to “NCAA Coach of the Year” seven times. In 
2012, she was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the country’s highest civilian 
honor, alongside other recipients such as Bill and Melinda Gates, John Glenn, and Stephen 
Hawking. 
The road to gender equality in the workplace hasn’t been an easy one. There are still many 
obstacles to women in leadership and making the workplace a more level playing field for 
their bright, talented contributions. 
In this course, you’ll learn more about how we all got here, including some of the specific 
roadblocks for women in leadership. You’ll learn about microaggressions and how they can 
be damaging – to any gender – and about some of the specific strengths of female leaders.

You’ll learn how to “own your game” and be a master of your own destiny, specifically as it 
relates to communication, key conversations and negotiation. 
In Pat’s own words, “Belief in yourself is what happens when you know you’ve done the 
thing things that entitle you to success. ” 
Pat believed women everywhere could be – and already were leaders.  
And you can be too.

## cw_mp_01_st_002 — From the Farm to the Basketball Court to Women’s Rights

Lesson 2 of 4
From the Farm to the Basketball Court to Women’s Rights

Pat’s own background was unlikely, in a way— she wasn’t a lawmaker or politician. Pat grew up in the small town of Henrietta, Tennessee, the first girl in the family, with three older brothers. When Pat was born, the family lived in a two-room log cabin, but the family farm eventually grew to a 1,000-acre farm with more than 60 milk cows, in addition to a general store, hardware store, feed mill, gas pump, laundromat, and construction business. Everyone worked on the farm, including Pat, and they lived from crop to crop.

“The best, most valuable thing my father gave me was an equal opportunity,” Pat later recalled of her childhood. “Nobody in the family seemed to regard me as a girl when it came to work or playing basketball. I fought hard and played hard and was expected to hold my own with my brothers, whether we were in the fields or in the hayloft. In most ways I was raised like a fourth son.”¹

“The best, most valuable thing my father gave me was an equal opportunity.” — Pat Summitt

Pat credited her upbringing on the farm with a sense of responsibility and independence at an early age.

“I’ve had more experience with physical pain than most men,” Pat said. “I’ve been paddled and whipped so hard it lifted me off the ground. I’ve had my jaw dislocated, and I’ve blown out my knee and I’ve given birth … Only recently have sports audiences become accustomed to watching women explore their physical limits in competitive arenas, and deal with issues like playing with pain. A tremendous cultural shift has taken place in just the last 25 years, allowing us to run, jump, and throw without being accused of being unfeminine. So don’t tell me what I can and can’t do.”

When Pat neared her high school graduation in 1970, she realized that while her brothers each had athletic scholarships, she wasn’t going to get one, because they weren’t offered to women at the time.

“I was a good enough player that the Cheatham County High School in Ashland City would eventually name the school gym after me,” she said. “But my parents had to scrape to pay my way to the University of Tennessee-Martin. And I thought, this needs to change.”

Three events in 1972 did change everything. Title IX legislation was passed by United States President Nixon. The International Olympic Committee (IOC) announced women’s basketball would be played for the first time at the 1976 Olympic games, and the Association of Intercollegiate Athletics for Women (AIAW) began to officially oversee women’s sports.

When it was announced that women’s basketball would be played for the first time at the 1976 Olympics, Pat got an invitation to try out for the national team.

“Sports, it struck me, could be a vital avenue to self-worth for women,” she recalls.

Pat would also go on to be a staunch advocate of the new Title IX legislation—Prohibition of Sex Discrimination. Title IX states: “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.”²

In the meantime, Pat became the head coach for the University of Tennessee Lady Vols in 1974. During her first two years as head coach she taught PE, coached basketball, attended graduate classes, and trained for the Olympics.

While college athletes were allowed to play full court, at the time, the Tennessee Secondary School Athletic Association (TSSAA) felt high school girls were not physically able to play full-court basketball. Girls were limited to a six-player half-court game, with each team comprised of three forwards and three guards. Forwards handled the ball and shot on the other team’s basket on one half court, while guards played defense on the other half court. There were no fast breaks, as players were not allowed to pass the half court line.

Then, in 1976, athlete Victoria Cape sued the TSSAA on grounds that the half-court game constituted discrimination under Title IX and the 14th Amendment. Pat defended Cape’s claim and also argued that the half-court arrangement stifled her college recruiting efforts. The court originally ruled in Cape’s favor, but the decision was later overturned. Frustrated, Pat announced that she would no longer recruit girls from the state of Tennessee to play for the Lady Vols until the TSSAA changed its rule and let Tennessee girls play the full-court game. A month later the TSSAA voted to let the girls play full-court.

Pat’s presence was felt, but a hefty double standard still existed for women and their coaches in the sport. She went on to coach the U.S. women’s basketball team at the 1984 Olympics. The women’s team won the gold medal and hoisted Pat on their shoulders in victory. Legendary Indiana Head Coach Bobby Knight remarked on Pat’s win saying, “You need to retire and start your family.”

Pat’s father agreed with Knight.

“Dad, I can have a family and still coach,” Pat remembers telling him.³

And the rest is history.

“Sports, it struck me, could be a vital avenue to self-worth for women.” — Pat Summitt

Pat went on to be the winningest college basketball coach in American history (men or women) at the time of her retirement. She coached the Lady Vols until 2012, and amassed more than 1,098 wins. During her career, Pat won eight national championships and boasted a 100 percent graduation rate for her student-athletes.

## cw_mp_01_st_003 — Cutting the Net … and Smashing the Glass

Type: reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 4
Cutting the Net … and Smashing the Glass

When once asked if she was a feminist, Pat joked, “No, I’m not a sign carrier.” The sign she carried was that of her character, her accomplishments, and an unflinching commitment to equality, which she let stand for itself. Pat’s achievements extend beyond Title IX—and to overall equity for women. As a woman in a male-dominated industry, she smashed the glass ceiling over and over again, paving the way for other women to succeed.

In 1998, Summitt was the first female coach to be on the cover of Sports Illustrated. She was the first female coach to negotiate a salary of $1 million and insist that she be paid equally with men in her field. She was the first person to be an Olympic winner both as a player and a coach (silver medal as a player in 1976 and gold as a coach in 1984). Two college basketball courts were named in her honor—one at UT Martin and one at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville—in addition to two streets. She was the Coach of the 20th Century, in addition to NCAA Coach of the Year seven times. In 2012, she was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the country’s highest civilian honor, alongside other recipients such as Bill and Melinda Gates, John Glenn, and Stephen Hawking.

## cw_mp_01_st_004 — Understanding the Playing Field for Female Leaders

Type: reading_download · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 4
Understanding the Playing Field for Female Leaders

While Title IX was a start, it was only a forerunner of the many gender equality changes that would happen in the U.S. over the next two decades. Discrimination against female employees has taken many forms over the years—most notably, being paid less than male counterparts and/or being subjected to gender stereotypes. Women now make up roughly half the U.S. workforce, but the road to overall equality has been a long one.

This course provides a closer look at the modern obstacles facing women in the workplace, specifically women in leadership. If women start out as half of the workforce, what is impeding their ability to reach the echelons of leadership, bringing gender equality to the decision-making and market success of organizations and businesses? As many researchers point out, the answer may be a “pipeline” problem—the number of qualified women available in all industries is limited, and many opt out of the workforce before leadership is attainable.

So why are women disproportionately spread throughout the workforce, and why are they leaving in higher numbers than men? What are the modern barriers, overt or hidden, that are keeping them from being seen as equal to their male counterparts?

Over the next few lessons, we’ll answer that question and more, including:

The current landscape of women in leadership
The evolution of women in the workforce
The unique obstacles facing women
Gender stereotypes
How to support strong female leaders
The power of female authenticity in the workplace
How to avoid burnout
Communication and negotiation for women
And more...

A Few Important Notes

Before we get into the above, a few notes on what this course is and isn’t:

This course is not an exploration on whether women should be in the workplace or not, because ultimately, the choice is up to the individual female. It solely seeks to explain the obstacles facing women who have decided to have a career, whether by choice or necessity.

It focuses on individuals who identify as a woman. It doesn’t attempt to explore non-binary gender issues in the workplace, as that would be a separate and complex discussion.

It isn’t meant to implicate modern-day men, who aren’t personally responsible for 100 years of U.S. corporate history and economic development and any lingering gender stereotypes. On the contrary, most males we’ve encountered are genuinely curious about how to make the workplace more equal for their female counterparts—they’re just not sure how or even what the exact issues might be. For that reason, we prefer to avoid language like “patriarchy,” “male privilege,” or “man-splaining.”

Finally, this course isn’t meant to imply that men don’t face their own biases or stereotypes in the workplace. This course simply focuses on women, especially as it relates to female leadership development.

As Pat would point out, the men aren’t villains, and women aren’t victims in the story of workplace equality. In her 40-year pursuit for gender equity in her field, she would simply say, “We don’t want special treatment. We just want to have an equal playing field.”

With all that in mind, let’s dive in!
