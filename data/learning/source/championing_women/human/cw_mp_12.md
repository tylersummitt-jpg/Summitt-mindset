Negotiation for Women

Negotiation is a life skill. Whether you are in business, sales, or any other profession, you will need negotiation at some point in your life. Maybe you already use it regularly. The point is, don’t be afraid of it, and don’t take it personally.
Unfortunately, gender expectations can impact how women in particular approach negotiation. Many women feel intimidated by the very thought of having to negotiate, or being labeled as "difficult" or "pushy."

## cw_mp_12_st_002 — Negotiation Tips & Techniques

Lesson 2 of 6
Negotiation Tips & Techniques

Negotiation Tips and Techniques

Maybe the thought of negotiation intimidates you, and you see yourself in one of the four examples in the previous lesson. Or maybe you work in sales, event planning, or use negotiation regularly in your role. Either way, here are a few negotiation practices and strategies for women specifically so you can take your skills to the next level.

In Conclusion

Utilize these tips & techniques to elevate your negotiation skills.

Silence is key in a negotiation.

Only talk as much as you need to! Pause. Don’t rush. Give the other person time to process your offer. People don’t think at lightning speed, especially when they’re trying to negotiate. It’s fine for there to be silences and long pauses while they process what’s being said. Don’t assume silence is disapproval and cut yourself off trying to fill in the gaps with a better offer.

Don’t underestimate the art of congeniality.

Even if things are tense with the other party or you feel like you hold most of the cards, you never want to walk into a negotiation with a closed perception, or like The Godfather: “I’m going to make him an offer he can’t refuse.”The more likable you are, the more likely the other person will be disarmed and come around to your side. Or if you are negotiating for three to five weeks of PTO in a new position instead of two, don’t say, “I was hoping for at least three weeks.” Explain your reason for needing more PTO in the package and listen to what the other side says. They may surprise you with more than what you are asking.

Know the range you are willing to negotiate in, but don’t give the other person your range.

For example, if you are negotiating salary or cost, don’t say “I’d be willing to accept $7,000 to $10,000.” All the other person will hear is $7,000. Say $10,000, and go from there. Give yourself room! Don’t start negotiating at $7,500 if $7,000 is the lowest you’ll take, even if the initial offer seems high to you. That’s why it’s called a negotiation.

Use specific numbers, if possible.

When it comes to business negotiations or even personal negotiations like the price of a used car, saying that it’s valued at $4,588 sounds more precise and leaves less room for grandiose negotiation than round numbers like $5,000 or $7,000. Similarly, $1,852,245 sounds more precise with less wiggle room than simply tossing out $1.8 million.

Listen for their “no.”

Listen for their no. In a negotiation, the no is the starting point. Specifically, why are they saying no? Is it the cost? Is it the timeline? Is it convenience? Many times we assume they don’t like one part of the bargain. They may even pretend they don’t like one part of the bargain, but upon listening further you realize the hang-up is actually another factor entirely.

Avoid black-and-white “yes” or “no” questions.

For example, rather than saying, “Is this the best you can do?” or “Is this the highest you can go?” leave room for the other side to be creative by saying things like, “What if I told you I just can’t make that number work with my manager?” or, “If we had to come up with another option, what would you suggest?” Then listen to what they say. Again, they just may surprise you.

Give (concise) reasons for your offers.

You don’t want to give too much information, but in general, giving the other person a reason for your proposition shows you care enough to treat them like a person and provide context, rather than simply stonewalling or saying, “That’s the best I can do,” or “That’s just what I need.” The key word here is concise. Simply saying, “We don’t have enough floor space at my facility to bring on any more people,” will suffice, rather than “It’s not happening” or “That’s a nonstarter.”

Don’t expect the other person to be won over by your logic.

Ever have the most logical argument in the world but still couldn’t get the other side to come to an agreement? Negotiations aren’t 100 percent logical. They’re emotional, just like any other form of communication. You can have the most rational argument in the negotiation, but that doesn’t mean the other side is going to automatically agree. You have to keep the mood light and emotions managed enough for the negotiation to continue in a productive way, and reach a settlement that both parties agree on.

Clarify what you’re hearing from the other side at each step of the negotiation.

“It sounds like I’m hearing you say you want more travel days worked into the package, right?” or “It seems like you are OK with the venue, but have concerns about the entertainment being offered, is that right?” Doing this frequently eliminates the chance for misunderstandings, and makes it less likely the other side will do an about-face later and try to renege on their deal.

Avoid tossing a “bomb” or ultimatum into the negotiation.

If things are going slow, resist the urge to shake things up or get their attention. The minute one party says, “That’s it, I’m out,” or “I’m going to get management on the phone,” the conversation ceases to be a negotiation.

Get the terms of the arrangement in writing.

Once an agreement is reached, even if the situation doesn’t warrant a contract, do your best to get the outcome in writing, even if it’s an email recap of what was discussed. Having it in writing eliminates the chance that one party can renege on what was decided upon later.

Deep Dive: Negotiating Salary

Negotiating Salary

When it comes to negotiating, salary is one of the most common negotiations women leaders face. Negotiating your salary is more than just a financial discussion; it's a reflection of your value, skills, and contributions to the organization, which makes it feel very personal.

Review

In short, with a little practice, negotiation won’t seem so foreign. If you’re able, practice some of these guidelines on smaller negotiations where the stakes aren’t so high. Every female leader needs negotiation at some point in her career (if not regularly) and practice makes perfect!

Know your worth.

The first step in successful salary negotiation is understanding your market value. Studies show that women routinely dismiss or undervalue their own skills in the workplace, which is problematic when advocating for pay increases. Arm yourself with data by researching industry standards. Consider your qualifications, experience, and the responsibilities of the role. Many times the first number in a salary negotiation is based on what the previous person was paid or what the department can afford for the role, and may not be based on market value or your skills specifically. Confidence stems from preparation.

Be armed with a range, not a finite number.

What’s the lowest you would accept, and what is your ideal range? Your dream number? The three might be very different, but it’s important to think about where the bottom is and what you’re working toward in advance of the negotiation. As stated earlier, don’t give out your range.

Be open and flexible.

Negotiation is a two-way street. Be open to discussion and compromise. If the employer can't meet your salary expectations, explore other areas such as additional benefits, flexible work arrangements, or performance-based bonuses. You don’t have to automatically accept, but it’s important to be open-minded.

Maintain a positive tone.

To keep the negotiation positive, emphasize your enthusiasm for the role and the company while discussing compensation. Let them know that you’re coming from a place of excitement about the role, not implying that you need to be compensated for the grunt work, stress, or long hours involved. Frame your negotiation in positive terms. Instead of saying, "I can't accept this offer," try, "I’m excited about the opportunity and my role here, and I believe a slight adjustment in the salary would better align with my skills and experience."

Quantify your achievements.

Provide concrete examples of your accomplishments and their impact on the company (or for previous employers). Salary shouldn’t be a hypothetical number. Use metrics and specific examples to demonstrate the value you bring to the table.

Don’t expect an answer on the spot.

Don’t be too frustrated if the other party needs to review your salary offer. There may be other implications to consider, such as salary increases given within 90 days of your professional review or salary constraints for a particular position. Many times your direct leader or supervisor isn’t the only person who has to sign off, so be prepared for a slight delay. But be prepared with an acceptable time frame: “I know my annual review isn’t for eight months, but I’ve taken on a new role with increased work. What can you do in the next 90 days?”
In short, with a little practice, negotiation won’t seem so foreign. If you’re able, practice some of these guidelines on smaller negotiations where the stakes aren’t so high. Every female leader needs negotiation at some point in her career (if not regularly) and practice makes perfect!

Lesson 11: Negotiating Salary  
 
Nikki Caldwell Fargas 
One of the most common negotiations you’ll face in your career is how to successfully 
negotiate your salary. Whether it’s negotiating at the onset of a job or during your position in 
a certain role, how you negotiate your salary is key.  
For women, mastering this skill is especially important in addressing the gender pay gap 
and ensuring fair compensation. By doing thorough research, knowing your worth, and 
approaching the negotiation with confidence and professionalism, YOU can make sure you 
get paid the salary you deserve.  
We’ll go over a few things to Do and Don’t here. 
First off, a few things to Do:  
Research your market value. The first step in salary negotiation is understanding your 
worth. Consider your qualifications, experience, and the responsibilities of the role. 
Studies show that women routinely dismiss or undervalue their skills, which can be an 
issue when negotiating salary. Arm yourself with data by researching industry standards.   
Be armed with a range, not a number. When accepting a new position, know your range. 
What is your ideal salary? The lowest you would accept? Think about what you’re working 
toward before the negotiation. Don’t give out your range – that’s just for your own guardrails. 
Pick the right timing. Timing can significantly influence the outcome of a salary negotiation. 
Ideally, try to negotiate after a successful project or a performance review when your 
contributions are fresh in your employer’s mind. Avoid negotiating during stressful periods 
for your company or immediately after a budget cut.  
Get everything in writing. Sometimes a salary increase is promised after the first 90 days, or 
they may communicate that you just had a raise and need to wait six months before they 
can get another approved. That’s fine, but ask if you can have that in writing from your 
supervisor. Tell them you understand, but want to make sure that the promise is 
documented, so there won’t be confusion later. Remember the saying, “If it’s not in writing, 
it’s not real.” 
Keep emotions out of it. OK, this is easier said than done. We’re talking about your salary 
after all – your bread and butter! – but try to stay focused on the facts and avoid getting 
emotional during the discussion. Remember, you are a highly talented, competent

individual, and this is not your only option in the workforce (even if it’s a job you love and 
don’t want to leave). Putting too much pressure on the discussion will only make you 
emotional, which can come off as desperate. Do your best to keep emotions out of it and 
pick a time that you can calmly and rationally make your pitch – not immediately following 
a downsizing to a single-household income or right after bad financial news. That in itself 
shouldn’t be the motivating factor for a raise.  
All this being said, the art of negotiating salary is as much about knowing what not to do as 
it is what to do. 
Here are some things you want to avoid: 
Don’t focus on the negative: “I need to be paid more for what I put up with. ”  or “I’ve taken 
on more work in the last year and my salary hasn’t gone up. ” A salary increase should be 
based on the value you provide the company, not simply the hours you work. It may be true, 
but it’s not helpful in a salary negotiation. Don’t mention grunt work, stress or long hours. 
You want to focus on the positive – how you are adding value to the company. You want to 
show so much ROI that you make them think they are practically saving money by keeping 
you on and giving you a raise! 
Don’t compare yourself to others: “I don’t get paid nearly as much as [fill-in-the-blank.]” 
Leave other people out of it! Their salary is their business and your salary is yours. Plus, it 
won’t help your case. It will only make your employer defensive. 
Don’t wait until you are actually in your annual review with your supervisor to talk about 
salary. Most salary increases have to be approved months in advance. Go on the offense 
here – 90 days out is a good window. If you need a salary increase, let your supervisor know 
a few months in advance of your annual review: “My annual review is coming up in January. 
I’d like to sit down before then to go over some of my recent contributions and discuss 
some future topics. ” This gives you an opportunity to go on the offense outlining the value 
you provide, backed up by any data, and let your supervisor know that you think a raise of X 
amount would be fair. It then gives them time to work on the request prior to your review. 
Don’t be inflexible. Negotiation is a two-way street. Be open to discussion and 
compromise. Be prepared for counter offers. If the employer can't meet your salary 
expectations, explore other areas such as additional benefits, flexible work arrangements, 
or performance-based bonuses. You don’t have to automatically accept, but it’s important 
to be open-minded. 
Don’t expect an answer on the spot. Don’t be too frustrated if the other party needs to 
review your salary offer. Many times your direct leader isn’t the only person who has to sign 
off, so be prepared for a slight delay. But be prepared with an acceptable time frame: “I

know my annual review isn’t for eight months, but I’ve taken on a new role and added a lot 
of new value to the company. What can you do in the next 90 days?”      
Finally, don’t throw down an ultimatum: “If I’m going to stay here long term, I need a salary 
increase. ” This starts the negotiation out on the wrong foot. This should only be used if you 
are truly prepared to walk out the door that moment, as in, you have another job lined up. 
Don’t let it get to this point! With successful salary negotiating, you can head off the need 
for any ultimatums at the pass. 
In a nutshell, negotiating salary is a skill that can significantly impact your career trajectory. 
Just like any skill, it has to be developed – and it can be learned!  
Remember, negotiation is not just about the money – it’s about recognizing your value and 
taking control of your career. 
You’ve got this!

**process_1**

Negotiating Salary | When it comes to negotiating, salary is one of the most common negotiations women leaders face. Negotiating your salary is more than just a financial discussion; it's a reflection of your value, skills, and contributions to the organization, which makes it feel very personal.

**process_2**

Review | In short, with a little practice, negotiation won’t seem so foreign. If you’re able, practice some of these guidelines on smaller negotiations where the stakes aren’t so high. Every female leader needs negotiation at some point in her career (if not regularly) and practice makes perfect!

**process_3**

Know your worth. | The first step in successful salary negotiation is understanding your market value. Studies show that women routinely dismiss or undervalue their own skills in the workplace, which is problematic when advocating for pay increases. Arm yourself with data by researching industry standards. Consider your qualifications, experience, and the responsibilities of the role. Many times the first number in a salary negotiation is based on what the previous person was paid or what the department can afford for the role, and may not be based on market value or your skills specifically. Confidence stems from preparation.

**process_4**

Be armed with a range, not a finite number. | What’s the lowest you would accept, and what is your ideal range? Your dream number? The three might be very different, but it’s important to think about where the bottom is and what you’re working toward in advance of the negotiation. As stated earlier, don’t give out your range.

**process_5**

Be open and flexible. | Negotiation is a two-way street. Be open to discussion and compromise. If the employer can't meet your salary expectations, explore other areas such as additional benefits, flexible work arrangements, or performance-based bonuses. You don’t have to automatically accept, but it’s important to be open-minded.

**process_6**

Maintain a positive tone. | To keep the negotiation positive, emphasize your enthusiasm for the role and the company while discussing compensation. Let them know that you’re coming from a place of excitement about the role, not implying that you need to be compensated for the grunt work, stress, or long hours involved. Frame your negotiation in positive terms. Instead of saying, "I can't accept this offer," try, "I’m excited about the opportunity and my role here, and I believe a slight adjustment in the salary would better align with my skills and experience."

**process_7**

Quantify your achievements. | Provide concrete examples of your accomplishments and their impact on the company (or for previous employers). Salary shouldn’t be a hypothetical number. Use metrics and specific examples to demonstrate the value you bring to the table.

**process_8**

Don’t expect an answer on the spot. | Don’t be too frustrated if the other party needs to review your salary offer. There may be other implications to consider, such as salary increases given within 90 days of your professional review or salary constraints for a particular position. Many times your direct leader or supervisor isn’t the only person who has to sign off, so be prepared for a slight delay. But be prepared with an acceptable time frame: “I know my annual review isn’t for eight months, but I’ve taken on a new role with increased work. What can you do in the next 90 days?”
In short, with a little practice, negotiation won’t seem so foreign. If you’re able, practice some of these guidelines on smaller negotiations where the stakes aren’t so high. Every female leader needs negotiation at some point in her career (if not regularly) and practice makes perfect!
