## cw_mp_10_st_002 — Communicating for Success

Lesson 2 of 7
Communicating for Success

Tone

Pat was famous for her authoritative presence on the sidelines, but would be the first to clarify that it wasn’t the only communication style she employed as a leader:

"I employ three completely different modes of communication as a coach,” she once said. “Off the court, I am a confidante and substitute mother. In that situation, my chief role is to listen, advise, and comfort. In practice, I speak as a teacher who sometimes needs to employ severe methods in order to maintain the attention of the students.
In a game, I issue blunt commands and motivate our players to endure adverse situations. It’s a competitive situation with no time for politeness or misunderstanding. My different tones, taken out of context, would be counterproductive or even downright offensive in any other situation."

Knowing how and when to use different tones appropriately can be the difference between coming off as authoritative in a situation or weak, sensitive or insensitive, excited or unenthusiastic.

Situation

The second component of personal communication is being able to appropriately assess each unique situation to determine the right level of communication.
“Frequently, knowing what to say to someone is all about time and location,” Pat once said. “You don’t always make the right call. There’s a judgment involved. You have to ask yourself, ‘What does this person need today?’ And you have to watch his or her body language.”
As Pat used to say, part of knowing how to communicate successfully is knowing what each person needs at the moment: “Do they need a pat on the back, or a kick in the rear?”
Context, she believed, was important. “Circumstances dictate how you need to speak or whether you need to just shut up and listen,” she said. “You don’t holler at a golf tournament. You don’t whisper in a forest fire.”

Nonverbal Communication

Nonverbal communication plays a significant role in conveying messages, feelings, and intentions. Mastering the art of nonverbal communication can enhance your professional and personal interactions. Here are a few tips for doing just that:

Summary

To summarize, nonverbal communication plays a significant role in conveying messages, feelings, and intentions. Mastering the art of nonverbal communication can enhance your professional and personal interactions.

Maintain Eye Contact

Eye contact is a large part of nonverbal communication. It signifies confidence, attentiveness, and sincerity. However, it's essential to strike a balance. Too much eye contact may come across as intimidating, while too little might suggest disinterest. A powerful gaze like Pat Summitt’s “Summitt Stare,” for example, communicated strength and assertiveness as part of her personal brand.

Confident Posture

A strong and confident posture can significantly impact how others perceive you. A straight posture exudes self-assurance and professionalism. Stand tall with your shoulders back and head held high. Avoid slouching or crossing your arms, as these gestures may convey insecurity. It can be helpful in this area to film yourself or get feedback from friends. Plus, there are all types of devices on the market nowadays to help you work on your posture.

Gesture with Purpose

Gestures can enhance verbal communication, but they should be purposeful and not distracting. Use gestures that complement your speech, emphasizing key points. Avoid excessive or erratic movements, as they can be distracting or detract from your message.
Use your hands purposefully to enhance your verbal communication. Some individuals have nervous habits that can detract from their nonverbal communication. Whether it's playing with hair, tapping fingers, or fidgeting, being aware of and minimizing these habits is crucial for professionalism.

Facial Expressions

Pay attention to your facial expressions, ensuring they align with the context of the conversation. A genuine smile or look of interested attention in what is happening at the moment can instantly create a positive atmosphere.

Active Listening

Nonverbal cues are equally essential in demonstrating active listening. Nodding, maintaining eye contact, and mirroring the speaker's expressions show that you are fully engaged. Avoid distractions and give your full attention to the person speaking.

## cw_mp_10_st_005 — Brevity

Type: video_and_reading · Source sequence: 5

Lesson 5 of 7
Brevity

Heading

Finally, one of the last components to stellar communications as a leader is brevity. Men typically use fewer words than women, but brevity is an underrated skill almost everyone can benefit from.
Brevity is most essential in your day-to-day communications for voice mails, emails, texts, or phone calls. Here are a few simple recommendations:

Voicemails

Keep voicemails 30 seconds or less. The point of a voicemail should be to leave one or two sentences with enough information to solicit a callback, not leave an entire monologue with all the details in someone’s voice inbox.

Texts

Because texts are already supposed to be short and sweet, anything over two or three sentences can come across as hitting your point too hard or warranting a phone call instead.

Emails

For emails, two to three short paragraphs should suffice. More than three paragraphs may come across differently than intended and may warrant a phone call or meeting to clear up the issue instead.
Be direct—clearly state the purpose of the email in the opening lines, and once you’ve typed a draft, review your emails to eliminate redundant phrases and information. Resist the urge to provide excessive background information in the email, and use special punctuation sparingly, such as exclamation points or too many question marks.

In the spirit of brevity, we’ll stop there, but to determine where you fall on the scale of brevity, consider getting some peer feedback on your communications. Ask a colleague or trusted friend to show you where you can improve if this area of communication is a challenge for you.

Deep Dive: Tone and Non-Verbals

Tone of voice and body language are often overlooked parts of effective communication, especially when you're in a leadership role. Think about how many times a situation has gone from “eh” to “Oh no!” just because someone’s tone was off. We’ve all been there—raising our voice when we’re excited or letting emotions like frustration or anger sneak into our words. But here’s the thing: mastering your tone can make a world of difference.

Similarly, body language plays an underrecognized role in communicating in the workplace. The way you carry yourself can scream confidence—or not—even before you say a word. Ever noticed how some people just command the room? It’s not magic; it’s body language. Good posture, eye contact, and not jiggling your leg like you’ve had too much coffee can make a huge impact in how you’re perceived.

And then there’s your face. Yep, that expressive mug of yours can either be your best friend or your worst enemy in communication. Ever been on a Zoom call and realized your facial expressions were betraying your inner thoughts? Maybe you have the kind of face that shows every emotion, or maybe you’ve got a poker face that makes you look like you’re plotting something. Either way, it’s worth getting some feedback—ask a trusted colleague to let you know if your face is sending mixed signals.

Sure, nobody’s perfect—we all have our quirks—but with a little practice, you can take your communication skills from good to great. And who knows? You might just surprise yourself with how much you can grow.

Conclusion

In summary, communication is a skill you practice continually. Great leaders never arrive, and are frequently revisiting the components above to see where they can improve. Make it a part of your own leadership toolkit to get regular feedback in these areas so you’re continually taking your game to the next level.

There’s a reason they say the eyes are the window to the soul. You’ll find that in the process of 
trying to read people’s eyes, you’re working two skills – holding eye contact and learning to read 
other people’s body language, which is a key part of emotional intelligence. 
 
So that’s several things to work on, but it’s worth it. And even the greats still have to work on their 
body language and overall communication. In her memoir, Pat once wrote that she didn’t 
consider herself starting off to be a very good communicator. “I was pretty hopeless. It’s a change 
I’m proud to have made, and it’s the way in which I’ve grown the most as a coach.” 
 
With all that in mind, how will you grow?
