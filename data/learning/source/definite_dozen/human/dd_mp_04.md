Develop and Demonstrate Loyalty: Introduction, Featuring Greg Hiebert 
 
Greg Hiebert, Leadership Coach & Managing Partner 
By far the greatest influence and source of purpose in Pat Summitt’s life was family. As much as 
she loved basketball, competition, and, of course, winning, family always came first. So it is not 
surprising that she created and nurtured the family model within Tennessee basketball. Her 
assistant coaches stayed with her for decades, through triumphs and travails, through every up 
and every down, because she treated them as so much more than just paid employees. To Pat 
Summitt, they were family, and her loyalty to family was absolute ... a non-negotiable. 
In fact, today, years after the untimely passing of Coach Summitt, hundreds of former players, 
assistant coaches, and colleagues consider themselves all part of Coach Pat Summitt’s family. 
Coach Summitt said, “To build a sound organization, you must surround yourself with people 
who share the same basic values. People who are constant and who will be true to your 
organization. In pressure situations, you don’t wonder how the person next to you will react. 
You know.” 
Isn’t it better to work with someone you have a regard for … in a relationship with … someone 
you have shared good and bad with … and have developed some trust in and loyalty to? 
The single most common reason organizations self-destruct is disloyalty, especially when they 
are made up of young people who have a tendency to talk behind each other’s back. When 
Coach Summitt talked about loyalty, it is powerfully revealing to notice that the conversation 
always started with her focus of being loyal, not on receiving loyalty, as well as expressing her 
appreciation for those who were deeply loyal to her.  
Coach Summitt was asked how she kept her core staff intact for so long. She replied, “How do 
you keep your family? I’ll tell you. You take care of your family, and you protect your family.” 
And the next obvious question is: How do you do that? Paying people well is a start, but you 
must find a way to show that you appreciate them, even with a simple gesture like buying them 
lunch. The joy that comes from giving without worrying about what you’ll receive in return is 
the unsolicited bonus that comes when you demonstrate genuine loyalty, for only when you 
give it away will it come back to you.

**back_2**

The second important ingredient a leader must consider in developing loyalty is to start with themselves, or with things they can control. In other words, instead of operating as a leader, waiting for others to demonstrate their loyalty, a great way to build that family environment is for the leader to demonstrate that loyalty themselves through how they interact with others.

If you want to develop loyalty, the first thing you have to do is demonstrate it. My staff and my family are one and the same. My staff is my family.

Pat Summitt

In most families, it is the aspiration of the elders that younger members of the family will learn what they need to someday create and lead their own family. For that to happen, the family needs to give younger members a chance to learn what it means to establish and maintain a family. The notion of building knowledge within the family to allow for more families is at the heart of why the world continues to grow and prosper. This concept of living like a family and gradually giving responsibilities that allow others to lead is true for teams and businesses as well. A successful family is one that is closely knit and loyal. The same should be true of teams and your organization. Loyalty and respect are both a two-way street and have to be given consistently if anyone in the family or in the organization expects reciprocation.

The Loyalty List

Finally, one very practical way that loyalty can be rehearsed and promoted on the team or within an organization is to establish what Pat Summitt called loyalty lists. Here’s how it works:

1. Identify three people who are important to you.
2. Identify what you think would happen to your relationship if you were disloyal to them.
3. Identify what you think would happen to your relationship if you show consistently how loyal you could be.
4. Identify three things you can do right away to demonstrate your loyalty to these very important people in your life.
5. Take action and monitor what happens when you live out loyalty, in both your personal and professional life.

Remember, "It’s all a family."

Develop and Demonstrate Loyalty: Loyalty – A Family Model, Featuring Joan 
Cronan 
 
Joan Cronan, Women’s Athletic Director Emeritus, University of Tennessee 
Pat’s philosophy was ... “If you want to develop loyalty, the first thing you have to do is 
demonstrate it. My staff and my family are one and the same. My staff is my family.” 
What would it take to be considered one of the most loyal leaders in your company, where 
your team is always willing to go the extra mile for you and you are a leader the entire team 
looks to for guidance and direction?  
What would that mean to you? Is that even possible? 
Pat (Summitt) was fiercely loyal. Pat was also very clear about what loyalty was and what it 
wasn’t. Loyalty was not a one-way street where people blindly followed others. It was very 
much a give-and-take where loyalty has to be given in order to be received.  
Pat never wanted to be surrounded by “yes people” who appeared loyal and would never 
challenge her when needed or push her to get better. The best way to acknowledge talent is to 
let people do the job you hired them to do and trust them to do it. 
This is often difficult for leaders – letting go and trusting.  
Pat’s humility allowed her to see that if she surrounded herself with “yes people” who simply 
pandered to her every decision, Tennessee basketball would not go very far.  
She wanted coaches who she believed were better in areas which she knew were not her 
greatest strengths. She wanted coaches who would challenge her and call her out when they 
felt her actions or emotions were becoming counterproductive.  
When she found those coaches, she created an environment where her coaches were free to 
tell her what she needed to hear, not just want she wanted to hear. She gave them both 
responsibility and authority and created loyalty by treating them with trust, gratitude, and 
ultimate respect. 
Remember, you build loyalty first by being loyal. So write out a loyalty list today. 
List three important people in your life and then define three ways you commit to demonstrate 
your loyalty to them. 
Post this list on your mirror or another prominent place where you will see it each day and 
condition your focus on being loyal.

Develop and Demonstrate Loyalty: The EQ Wacky Stack, Featuring Joan Cronan 
 
Joan Cronan, Women’s Athletic Director Emeritus, University of Tennessee 
There is no doubt you have a high IQ. If you didn’t, you wouldn’t be watching these videos. But 
IQ doesn’t equal EQ – your emotional quotient, or emotional intelligence. 
Emotional intelligence plays an enormous role in these dynamics, and simply put, emotional 
intelligence is being aware of how you come across to others and your willingness and ability to 
adjust. 
Now, there is a right way to build emotional intelligence and there is a wrong way.  
Let’s start with the right way. There are four building blocks to emotional intelligence, and they 
need to be stacked in a particular order.  
It starts with you understanding yourself. Pat (Summitt) started by taking a personality profile

**step_1_own_the_conversation**

When you own the conversation when dealing with conflict, you don’t begin the discussion by saying, ‘Do you know what your problem is?’ It’s important to enter a conflict conversation without the burden of initial blame. The leader should immediately own, without accusation or assumption, their desire to address the issue and their desire first and foremost to understand, to listen, and to learn. It’s important when doing this, however, to be honest about the situation. For example, if a leader is addressing a circumstance where someone they work with didn’t take responsibility for a mistake they made, they would start by benignly communicating what they heard, and describing how it made them feel, while once again clarifying the desire to learn about the other person’s perspective or experience.

**step_2_disarm_the_tension**

Even if a leader assumes that they have a reputation for being a good listener and giving people a chance, it’s important in a situation like this to reiterate these facts. Remind those involved that they are important to the organization; their feelings matter; and even if this conversation is challenging, the goal is to learn and for everyone (including the leader) to continue to grow and get better. This type of disarming will free the participants from being defensive, preparing them instead for personal and career growth.

**step_3_describe_the_situation_factually_without_emotion**

As a leader, when you bring the situation to the attention of those involved, it’s important to describe that situation as benignly as possible. This isn’t always easy, but it also is important to discuss these issues without accusation. For example, if the leader had observed a team member acting in a cruel and thoughtless way towards another, it would be important in that discussion to describe what was observed in careful detail without qualifying statements (‘And then, you RUDELY said...’). Notice, with that statement, you’ve already judged the action and intention. This can be difficult, because in some cases, maybe the rude behavior is obvious to everyone. Remember that the goal of dealing with conflict isn’t to assert the leader's observation. The goal is to bring behaviors to light and learn what those behaviors mean. A much better outcome is when those involved recognize on their own that they were being rude rather than a supervisor declaring the obvious. If leaders want excellence in others, they have to be consistently engaged in showing them how to grow. In this case, pointing out an opportunity to grow and letting them absorb it and declare it will result in more growth.

**step_4_describe_the_impact**

After a factual recitation of what was observed, the leader then needs to once again benignly describe the impact, without accusation. In the previous example, let’s assume the team member was rude in a public way to another team member. It would be a mistake to say, ‘And after you said that awful thing, you got your wish. Your team member was humiliated.’ The mistake here is that you’ve already assumed intention. After all, maybe the person was trying to be funny or was just clueless and truly missed the mark. To make that person feel like they would intentionally hurt another person could be enormously hurtful to them, even after an egregious mistake. Though you don’t want to assume intent, it is important to describe the impact as thoroughly as possible. If feelings were hurt, or people were discouraged, it’s important to share the absolute truth about that impact. Remember, you can be truthful without being accusatory.

**step_5_ask_for_a_response**

Early in the conversation, it’s helpful to remind the individual being addressed that they will be given a chance to share their version of the facts and to respond. After sharing the facts and the impact above, it’s important to allow the person to gather their thoughts and respond. Remember, if you are initiating a difficult conversation, you have had the advantage of preparing in advance for the discussion. The person at the center of the conversation may not have had that luxury, so slow down and give them time to reflect. This might keep them from overreacting or acting out further.

**step_6_conclude_and_state_your_request**

Making sure things are clear at the end is important. The leader may decide to provide a summarized overview of what they just heard, in terms of what was presented, what other perspectives were shared, and what the next steps are. If the leader is forced to make some recommendations at this point, factually and thoughtfully lay them out. Put these requests in gentle terms such as, ‘It would really help me if...,’ allowing the conversation to conclude in a less confrontational way.

**summary**

While dealing with conflict isn't easy, having a plan or process to effectively deal with it can be very helpful: 1. Own the conversation. 2. Disarm the tension. 3. Describe the situation factually, without emotion. 4. Describe the impact. 5. Ask for a response. 6. Conclude and state your request.
