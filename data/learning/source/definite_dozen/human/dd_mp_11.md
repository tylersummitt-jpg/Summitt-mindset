Are You Ready to Compete? Do You Believe Your Own Answer?
In the video, Coach Summitt talked about a rather famous moment with a team of her All-American players where she challenged them to communicate their readiness to compete.

There is a great exercise that you can do, too, to achieve more, develop a stronger mindset, stay focused on your goals, believe in your abilities, and improve mental readiness and determination to compete as a leader or as an individual performer.

Competitiveness Affirmation Mirror Technique

Set the Competition Goal
Express your competition goal as an affirmative, declarative statement. For example, "I will lead my state in life insurance policy sales next year."
Say Your Goal Out Loud in Front of the Mirror
Stand in front of a mirror and make the statement out loud as if you were greeting yourself and sharing the news.
Repeat the Affirmation Until You Firmly Believe It
As you state your goal you want to achieve, look into your own eyes and see the expression on your face. Do you believe your own answer? Do you believe that the person in the mirror is capable of what you are saying you're going to achieve? Continue to double down on the certainty of that statement. Apply this technique to many things you are trying to achieve.

Be a Competitor: Introduction

You can't always be the most talented person in the room. But you can be the most competitive.

Be a Competitor: Introduction, featuring Roberto Fernandez

Competition Is Coming
Competition is one of the key elements in outstanding work. No one stays on top forever, and opportunities are always up for grabs. Your competitor is always either rising or will be rising soon. It comes down to how much you are willing to embrace competition to reach the most exalted level of performance.

While sports may only crown one winner a year, in business and other elements of our lives, we often have more than one winner. A burning sense of "competition" encourages organizations to create outstanding products and services. The message here is clear. Striving to be the best is important. And the best way to ignite that pursuit is to ignite the sense of competition.

How Competitive Are You and Your Team?
This is where leadership truly matters. When a coach inspires a team at halftime, challenging them to ignite their energies and actions, they are pressing the buttons of competition. When a longstanding champion sees a rival rising up and feels threatened, that sense of competition comes alive. The question is, how competitive are you as a leader? Do you feel that same sense of competitive juice when you observe a competitor gaining on you? Do you feel yourself wanting to overtake the champion? These are honest questions that you must reflect upon.

Part of what can help you learn to compete more often and with enthusiasm is to recognize that competition is an important part of what it means to lead. If you read these words and feel like you "don't like to compete," you may want to ask yourself, how bad do you want to win?

Wanting to win, and wanting to beat the competition, is at the heart of what it takes to improve your performance. If you want to nurture your sense of competition, the best thing to do is to treat it like a muscle and use it. This is why so many coaches are so good at creating competitions in practice. They want to ignite that spirit and get their team members to think consistently about the presence of competition in their practices, and then in games.

In your team or organization, how can the leader ignite a sense of competitiveness?

You have to love your adversaries. They make you better. They force you to improve, to stretch your capacities.
Pat Summitt

Harnessing the Power of Competitiveness
Don't Seek Out Weak Competition. Compete Against the Best
Coach Pat Summitt said, "If you want to be the best, you have to compete against the best, even if it means risking a loss. Why seek out weak competition?" Rigorous competition makes competitors stronger and brings the winners more valuable rewards. Hoping for the competitors' failure or weakness is a sign of weakness. Rather than praying for weakness within a competitor, develop your own strength and skill that is capable of beating even the most formidable competition.
Identify the Rival
The rival, or the competition, is absolutely key in this formula. Can you identify a rival in your business? We all have rivals in our lives. Maybe you are in line for a promotion at work. Is there a rival for that position? Maybe you and your team are trying to break a sales record, and the rival company just opened a new office in your area. Recognizing the presence of a rival — small or large — is key to igniting that sense of competition.
Keep Your Rival in Sight to Motivate You
One of the most motivating things you can do to help maintain your discipline and focus is to picture your rival and imagine that they are one step ahead of you. When businesses are run poorly or teams are poorly led, there is a habit of forgetting about the rival. For example, if you own a restaurant and your staff members are embroiled in a number of internal battles, those problems could negatively impact the service to your diners. In this context, instead of focusing on your business and staying ahead of your rival, you might forget about your rival, allowing it to go after your most important asset...your diners. If you're a competitive person, what are you going to do? Keep your rival in sight.
Appreciate Your Rival
Our rivals make us better. The most competitive leaders and peak performers have come to realize that appreciating your rival is one of the healthiest, most forward-thinking, competitive things you can do. Rather than being driven crazy by the achievements of your rival, appreciate their grace and strength, admire what they are doing to stay competitive, and then recognize that they are there in your life to, indeed, make you better. The presence of your rival will force you to raise your standard and improve your game.

Let's Summarize
Don't seek out weak competition. Compete against the best.
Identify the rival.
Keep your rival in sight to motivate you.
Appreciate your rival.

In fact, all of these factors contribute to the peak performer's success, through an enhanced commitment to reaching a winning goal.

Don't Be Afraid to Say You Want to Win
When you declare that you want to win, you put yourself in a position of either winning or publicly failing to do so. It is, however, that public declaration that often drives peak athletic performers to practice and compete at a higher level. The same is true in business. Some of the best business leaders are famous for making extraordinarily intense promises that require a significant commitment in order to achieve. This public declaration drives unusual levels of success through an enhanced commitment to rising up and meeting the publicly declared standard.

The reason so many people underachieve, instead of overachieve, is simply because they are afraid to make a mistake, or to fail, or to be wrong. They are afraid to find out what's inside of them. Competitiveness is the opposite of complacency. It's disquieting and uncomfortable. It requires commitment, and risk, and soul-searching. When you choose to compete, you take a huge gamble.
Pat Summitt

You Find Out What's Inside
Another reason that public declaration of a goal is so terrifying is that it reveals an opportunity to expose what's inside. In other words, if on the inside beats "the heart of a champion," capable of rising up and delivering, even if winning eludes the competitor, we find out that the potential to win is there. Or you may find out on the inside that the person can't compete at all. After all, failure happens every day, and true competition will reveal it. Peak performers and the very best leaders are always on a relentless search for what's inside. They have an optimistic view of who they are and what they can become under the weight of competition. They believe that the best version of themselves will emerge under the scrutiny of the opportunity to compete and win.

Timidity or Tenacity?
In the roar of the fight, there will always be those who retreat for the quiet shade. They will let somebody else fight the fight and claim the ring. The best leaders understand that they must commit to remaining enormously tenacious in the face of competition to advance their skills.

Unfortunately, you can't always tell immediately whether a person is going to step up in the face of competition. How would you find out a new employee's capability to be tenacious under the weight of competition?

Influence Your Opponent

How Do You Influence Your Opponent?
You influence your opponent by studying them, and then playing to your strengths and against their weaknesses. As a player, Pat Summitt fought for a seat in the Olympic squad against a superior player. She won the contest by forcing her opponent — a strong offensive player — to play a defensive game. In the world of business, a new competitor can apply similar tactics of influencing their opponents.

Getting More Competitive by Competing More Often
Once again, the key to getting better in this space is to compete more often. None of us are born in a perfect, competitive condition until we learn how to compete and get used to it. This is why participating in sports at a young age plays such an important role in the lives of youth. They learn what it's like to face competition at an early age and what to do in those high-pressure situations. The opportunity to grow never ends, and the best leaders understand that the best way to get their team to be more competitive is to compete, adjust, reflect, and compete again.

Two Strategies for a Call to Compete
To be competitive, leaders and peak performers must get good at answering a call to compete. Here are two strategies to consider.
Posting the Bulletin-Board Material
One strategy is the presence of "bulletin-board" material. You don't have to wait for a competitor to say something negative about you or your work to create that material. If you are a software designer trying to compete with another designer, and you post articles in your workspace reminding you of their latest innovation, it may motivate you to rise up, compete, and win. Seeing an accolade your competitor won within view of your workspace may inspire you to keep working when fatigue sets in.
Identifying a Rival
In some cases, a rival may not be as easy to identify. For example, if you work in a large corporation doing quality control, you may not have the day-to-day "wins and losses vs. the competitor" that other departments may experience. On this note, leaders may need to get creative and find existing metrics that would allow for competition. In some cases, companies are big enough for internal departments to compete with each other for some level of excellence. Who is your rival? Identify it and keep it in front of you.

What Does Competition Do to Us?
There is an old adage that competition doesn't build character, it reveals it. It's likely that competition does both. By consistently competing, you learn what it means to compete and how to improve your readiness to match wits and energy with whomever you're up against. On the other hand, competition indeed reveals our character. As we continue to face competition, we ultimately unlock an ancient potential within us. The reason you are here reading these words at this moment is because countless ancestors who came before you had the agility to survive. Human beings have been very successful at meeting challenges, rising to the competition, and evolving. Our bodies have literally learned to grow and change in order to compete.

In your life and career, who are you competing with? How do you identify your rivals? And how can you influence them?

Wrap-Up

When you choose to compete, you make the conscious decision to find out what your real limits are, not just what you think they are. Competition trains you to accept risk and to endure setbacks. By embracing it, you can enhance your life. But it will also pull you through those painful, frightening, everyday battles we all have to face at one time or another.
Pat Summitt

Be a Competitor: Wrap-Up, featuring Roberto Fernandez

Competition Can Create a “Whatever It Takes” Mentality
Have you ever noticed how hard it is to beat a sitting champion? They already know how to win, and they have at their disposal a number of tools to hold off the competition. While this is probably more easily visualized in sports, it's true of virtually any type of challenge. When a team has worked hard to reach a goal and understands the challenges of success, team members are far more likely to commit to whatever it takes to win! As a leader, this "whatever it takes mentality" will show up more often when competition becomes a regular element of practice and preparation, and resilient leaders within the organization are consistently encouraged and supported.

The Urgency of Failure
Failure, or the threat thereof, can create a great deal of urgency. Teams who have been working a long time to accomplish a goal will feel the emergency of impending failure, perhaps brought on by the emergence of competition, and will use that fuel to redouble their efforts. Leaders need to be aware that the urgency of failure can show up at every turn.

Raising the Standards in the Face of Urgency
Urgency brings out our very best at a cellular level. Athletes have actually reported that at times they have performed at peak performance while on the verge of getting sick, likely because their brains and bodies felt a sense of urgency due to the impending illness, causing an even greater level of focus. Wilt Chamberlain was reportedly sick the night before he scored 100 points in a single NBA game. The key takeaway for leaders is this: make sure you respond to a sense of urgency by raising standards, not inducing panic. Some groups don't respond as well in cases of urgency or emergency. They may collapse under stress and refrain from doing their very best. This panic can come due to a lack of experience on how to perform under a sense of urgency. Coach Summitt famously had her team practice for hours with simulated end-of-game situations, forcing players to take the last shot or to make split-second decisions under a variety of circumstances. By helping the players get used to a sense of urgency and training them to raise their standards when it happened, there was a much greater chance that game-winning performance was going to happen.

Bringing the Battle, or Waiting for War
We’ve learned in this unit that competition is going to emerge no matter what we do. To this end, leaders have two choices. They can respond to the competition by simply waiting for it to show up, or preemptively bring the best of themselves to battle, anticipating competition. A seasoned, peak-performing athlete may know that a competitor is likely to emerge at any time and attempt to dethrone them in new ways. By preemptively being ready for battle, it's far more likely that the competition will be a fierce one when it arrives.

Raising the Standard, and Leaving Others Behind
Don’t be surprised if the act of raising the standard makes others feel uncomfortable. If your peer group tends to be out of shape, and you decide to commit to a rigorous workout routine, your new standard may not necessarily be met with open arms. While it may not be your intention, the very act of raising the standard for yourself or for your team may send a message that they are being "left behind." The desire to raise your standard and do whatever it takes to remain competitive will attract others who are hungry for competition, but you will face opposition from those who are uncomfortable with the same. The harder you push to be competitive, the more the divide will increase. Don't be surprised if you leave behind some folks who just weren't ready for the challenge.

Competition Ignites Both Success and Failure. Leadership Matters!
Throughout this module, you've been exposed to the many merits of competition. However, jumping into a competition doesn't mean that you or your team will automatically achieve or experience all the amazing benefits. One of the best things that a leader can do, however, is to test the mettle and see where they stand. A coach may look at their team and think they know who the real competitors are, only to discover that, when tested, different leaders and intense competitors rise and fall. Competition ignites both success and failure, and to be an outstanding leader, the presence of competition must ignite in you a sense of innovation, and the willingness to raise your own standards. Without question, competition and the ability to manage this dynamic is an absolute must for high-performing leaders and individual performers to achieve the highest results.

Let's Summarize
Make competition a regular element in your practice.
Be prepared to face the urgency of failure at any time.
Respond to a sense of urgency by raising standards.
Anticipate and prepare to compete at any time.
Accept that not everyone will accept raised standards.
Accept the risk of failures.
Be willing to innovate and raise your own standards.
