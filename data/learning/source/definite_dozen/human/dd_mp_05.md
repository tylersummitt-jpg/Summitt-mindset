Why Communication Is So Important for Leaders

Becoming adept at good communication is at the heart of what good leaders must do to be effective.

One could argue that the skill to communicate is perhaps the most important skill any leader can develop. Day in and day out, leaders spend their time communicating. They communicate with customers, clients, colleagues, direct reports, and outside partners who can have a huge impact on their success. With every artery of communication emerges an opportunity to influence and improve the trajectory of your organization. If a leader is a good communicator, literally every moment of their day is spent infusing good energy and possibility in the organization or team. If they are a bad communicator, things can spiral in the wrong direction very quickly.

While Talkers Talk, Communicators Listen and Empathize

The best leaders understand that good verbal skills are helpful. But they are only part of the puzzle. Good communication involves the ability to be a good listener. While listening, it’s also important to be able to absorb the feelings attached to those providing the message. Listening to an irate colleague may be a signal that the system is broken, the individual is struggling with their job, or there was a significant (or maybe only episodic) breakdown leading to this outburst. This understanding can't happen without carefully observing the surroundings, listening to the message, feeling the intention, and being able to use discernment to reflect on next best steps.

There’s NO Small Stuff!

When attempting to communicate, the little things matter! If a leader has a difficult conversation to execute, it’s helpful for them to consider the location of the meeting, the leader’s body language, the facial expressions, and any other potential contextual factors that could impact of the message is received. The more careful the delivery of the message, the more likely the successful receipt of that message.

Timing, and Other Factors, Are Everything

It is said that "timing is everything." Communicating to a person or a group at a point in which they’re ready to hear it makes for a much more dynamic message. As a coach, speaking to a team after losing in triple overtime versus a speech after winning by 30 points represents very different timing. And it isn’t just about timing – it’s about wanting to pick the right message, at the right time, delivered to the right people, at a moment that will provide the greatest opportunity for growth.

Three Keys to Great Communication

In reflecting on the keys to great communication, three essentials stand out.

KEY 1 — Authenticity

There is currently a lot of talk in business and politics about the importance of "transparency." One of the key elements to transparency and believability is whether the leader communicates with authenticity. Is the leader believable? If a leader is seen as suspicious or insincere, they aren’t seen as authentic. One notorious example of how important authenticity is came during the infamous presidential election between Richard Nixon and John F. Kennedy. This was the first nationally televised debate, and Nixon was observed to be sweating and fidgety. Those who watched the debate on TV indicated that they didn’t think Nixon was authentic or trustworthy. Conversely, of those listening to the debate on the radio, some actually favored Nixon in the discussion because they only heard the words and weren’t privy to the nervous behavior in front of the TV audience.

KEY 2 — Relationships

When you have strong relationships, there are greater opportunities to communicate quickly and with precision. As a leader, if you have established a strong relationship with your team, in a moment of stress you can be somewhat demanding without worrying whether or not you have a good enough relationship with them to withstand it.

KEY 3 — Nonverbal Communication

How can you tell that the two persons in the image are having a positive, engaging conversation?

The nonverbal element of communication can sometimes have a greater impact than the exact words used. The debate between Kennedy and Nixon is a great example. Another example might include a leader who needs to call in an employee and remind them to report to work on time. If the leader calls in the employee and stands up during the meeting with arms crossed, scowling, it’s likely that the employee will get the message that there is displeasure in that moment. Or, a leader who might physically stand close to somebody could create an intimidating environment that changes what the meeting feels like. Imagine if you were in a meeting and you had a boss that was 6’8”, and he chose to confront you about a topic, while leaning into your personal space! That could be intimidating.

The problem with these non-verbal miscues is that they can send a message that isn’t exactly intended. The well-intended, tall boss may not like the idea of intimidating those around them, but that could be the perception if he doesn’t use his nonverbal communications correctly. It’s true of every leader, and being conscious of it is half the battle.

Summary

We will review these three communication strategies more closely further in this module.

Good leaders remain acutely aware that every interaction they have with other people affords an opportunity to enhance or diminish their message. No matter how much or how little a leader pays attention to their ability to communicate, they continue to send out a myriad of messages every day. This is why it is incredibly helpful for leaders to invest energy in learning how to communicate and deliver a message that hits the intended target. Whether a leader likes it or not, their ability to communicate is the wellspring of their effectiveness.

What does it mean to be authentic in communication?

1. Be honest and transparent.
2. Be the true you. Don't pretend to be something you are not.
3. Speak with candor.
4. Move aside your ego and be open to have your ideas challenged.
5. Strategically balance the amount of compliments and criticism.
6. Don't compromise your principles. Stay true to your principles, values, and ethics.

Now let's delve deeper into each of these qualities.

1. Be Honest and Transparent

When a leader is seen as honest, there’s greater trust. With greater trust comes the ability to grow as a team and take chances. Be honest when you become a leader!

2. Be Authentic: Be You!

Have you ever seen a politician, in an attempt to find favor with local voters, find themselves in situations that don’t at all seem authentic? Maybe they are trying to act comfortable on the back of a horse, or in a factory, or bowling, when in reality, this isn’t who they are. Although some may give them points for trying, in most cases these attempts go awry because leaders were seen as inauthentic. This goes back to the idea of honesty and transparency. Leaders should, by-and-large, be themselves. If they are going to put themselves in the position where they are trying something they haven’t tried before, they should admit it! Don’t be afraid to claim to be an outsider. Former President Barack Obama had a long history of being comfortable around the game of basketball. However, when he went into a bowling alley he openly admitted he had no game! In that situation, he didn’t pretend to be anything he wasn’t, and the bowlers in the alley had fun showing him the ropes.

3. Speak with Candor, Even When It’s Uncomfortable

This can be difficult, because it can’t always be premeditated. Individuals within an organization may suddenly spring a question on a leader that requires uncomfortable candor. For example, if a leader is asked by an employee about a promotion they just aren't ready for, telling someone the truth can be hurtful, but that’s part of what being a leader is all about. A leader is better off sticking with the truth and trying to deliver the message with kindness than to be inauthentic and insincere. In general, speaking with candor always improves the organization and enhances everyone's opportunity to grow.

4. Move Aside Your Ego

Once again, ego can stand in the way of thoughtful communication. If a leader isn’t honest, open, willing, or interested in listening, the ability to communicate successfully disappears. In many cases, a leader will fail in this area because they lack the confidence in themselves to take the risk of being open. In order to be open to communication, you have to be open to having your ideas challenged...and in some cases even replaced.

You can simply stick to whatever script you’ve imagined, but leaders like this are rarely successful. In a world that requires flexibility, creativity, and spontaneous pivots in the face of challenges of competition, it’s impossible to maintain a large ego and communicate well.

5. Don’t Overuse Compliments or Criticisms

Pat Summitt always believed you need to be strategic with compliments and criticisms, and refrain from overusing either one. If Coach Summitt were effusive in her compliments for the team after a hard-fought victory, the words probably would mean something and stick with the players for quite some time. On the other hand, if she praised her players on how quickly they got back on the court after taking a water break in practice, the compliments would begin to seem meaningless and ultimately become less impactful.

6. Don’t Compromise Your Principles

Don’t go there if your heart, head, and gut tell you not to go. As a leader, you’re going to hear a lot of feedback from a lot of people. Through it all, you must stay true to your principles, values, and ethics. Take in the information you receive in stride, but don’t let anyone talk you into compromising those principles, values, and ethics. If someone tries to get you to go against your beliefs and it won’t work for you and your organization's core values, you must have the courage to just say "No."

Throughout this course, we’ve discussed situational leadership. It suggests that the particular situation a leader finds themselves in should dictate the strategy and approach they take. For a leader, arguably what's most important is the communication dynamic in private spaces. A good leader understands that a kind word said in front of the entire work group or team can send a powerful message that the effort it took to receive that compliment is to be duplicated. Furthermore, the decision to admonish or embarrass somebody in public can be one that leads to long-term pain and even resentment. The decision to call out a player or a colleague in a public space where others are able to observe is a decision that can have multiple ramifications. Maintaining awareness of situational variables in effective communication is a skill that good leaders must continue to work on throughout their careers.

Do They Know Who You Are?

While leaders need a personal life away from the scrutiny of the workplace, it's clear that one of the fastest ways to build relationships with others is to let them into your life. Leaders who are willing to share stories about their childhood struggles, continued imperfections, and other examples that reveal they are just like everyone else, arguably help a leader get closer to their colleagues. A wise strategy to this end is to identify parts of your life that you're willing to share.

Consider What to Share

Sharing family anecdotes and certain hobbies and pursuits represent safe and identifiable reveals within a larger work group.

Sharing your continued attempts to disrupt the efforts of an opposing political party, or a deep dive into a faith journey that others may not share perhaps should be saved for a closer set of friends.

Great Ways to Create Connections

Connect with High Touch! — Manage by Walking Around

Leaders must get connected with their teams and peers. Many times, leaders who start new jobs spend a huge chunk of their day simply walking around the organization and talking to people, getting to know who they are, and learning about their tasks. It would be very easy for a new leader to get caught up in the myriad of office-bound meetings that are arguably necessary. But, there's a great likelihood that the leader’s overall effectiveness and chance of success within the organization will be greater by making those initial connections and allowing those perceptions to drive understanding. Successful leaders understand the importance of establishing and maintaining high-touch. Some of the best leaders on the planet establish within their calendar time to simply walk around, observing the work environment and looking to connect with those who are on the front lines of doing the work, looking for clues to success and are seeking understanding, solving problems on the spot, and resolving conflicts before they emerge.

Connect with High Tech — Manage by Effectively Using Technology

So, can you manage by walking around online? Can you do high touch as a leader with high tech? The answer is yes. However, you shouldn't use technology to the exclusion of opportunities to connect with face-to-face. To put it in perspective, probably the best thing that a leader can do today with all the access points of technology is to simply see these tools as another mechanism for making connection.

For example, if a leader recognizes that there is an individual in the organization with whom they haven't spoken in a while and for whatever reason they still haven't been able to see them face-to-face, a warm text message indicating that they were thinking about them and checking in can mean the world to the person receiving the message.

Another advantage of these high-tech tools is that they are very scalable. Certainly a leader could send a group text and literally connect with everybody in the organization in a heartbeat. However, deeper levels of personalization emerge when the messages themselves are more personalized. So, as a point of practice, technology can be used to enhance a steady diet of face-to-face, heartfelt connection.

In an era of technology that can ebb and flow in the blink of an eye, the permanence of a handwritten card or note makes a big difference to those who receive them. Some of the best leaders have maintained a habit of writing a personal note every day to different people within the organization, expressing gratitude or the observance of something positive within the organization. For example, some leaders will maintain a stack of these notes. After they manage by walking around, observing noteworthy attitudes, behaviors, and accomplishments, they will follow up with the permanence of a written note. The good news and the good feelings within are hard to deny.

The Stare

Much has been written over the years about the famous Pat Summit stare. Her very expressive eyes and face had a stirring emotional impact on her players, the fans, the referees, and all levels of her opponents. None of this was by accident. She understood at a very high level the power of nonverbal communications. She understood that how she carried herself could have a significant impact on her message. She understood that words alone weren’t enough. In order to have a significant impact on other people (the true goal of leadership), a leader must take advantage of all the tools at their disposal. In this case, nonverbal tools, when used thoughtfully, can become an expert leverage point in reinforcing your message.

A Deeper Understanding of Nonverbal Communication

The goal of communication is to create impact or understanding. If you were delivering a college commencement speech, you may want to send the message of hope or inspiration to those in attendance. Or, if you were trying to mend a broken relationship, you may convey the right words of apology to move the relationship forward. In both examples, we search for tools to help carry our message, hoping that what’s in our hearts and in our heads can be transferred to our audience. When you think about it, it’s a painfully difficult translation. Our brains are enormously complicated, and we still have a lot to learn about mental processing. If you are trying to share a feeling with someone who hasn’t lived your life experience, hasn’t grown up in your world, or may be a very different age, all those differentiating factors can make communicating even basic ideas more difficult.

The Impact of Three Communication Elements on Messages of Feelings and Attitudes

Pie chart: Words: 7; How words are said: 38; Facial expression/nonverbal: 55.

Albert Mehrabian. 1981. Silent Messages: Implicit Communication of Emotions and Attitudes

To help better break this down, we need to reflect on how we currently take in the signals during communication and which signals we tend to pay the most attention to. Although scientific research doesn’t represent a comprehensive understanding of the art of communication, it does give us a starting point. When communicating with someone face-to-face, extensive research concluded that 7% of the impact came from the words being used, while 38% was conveyed by the way the words were said. This means somebody could use the words "I love you" in a sentence. But if this is said sarcastically or with laughter, the message is significantly altered. Perhaps, more importantly, the same research revealed that the message received was 55% impacted by the use of a person's facial expressions and body language.

Why Read a Face Over Listening to Words?

So, why would our brains work this way? It likely comes down to a basic survival instinct. There are hundreds of muscles in our face, and the evidence of joy, frustration, and fatigue all show up in our bodies — arguably a lot more authentically than in our words. How many times, for example, has a friend told you “I’m fine,” when you knew they weren’t? How about when someone you love says, “I’m not tired,” when you can see they are exhausted? Our brains have learned to study others and know the signs of what’s really going on with them. It’s an instinct that has helped us protect each other and survive.

So How Can I Apply This?

Study yourself on video

If facial expressions and body language mean so much, you need to spend some time in the mirror or on video. Without taking the time to really study ourselves, we can't begin to observe how others are likely perceiving our behaviors. How many times have you seen yourself on video and disliked what you saw? Does this person look strong and confident? Would you believe what they were saying if you didn't know them? It is important to be really honest with yourself and look at your facial expressions.

Do you need to slow down or pick up the pace?
Do you need to speak with more confidence?
Do you need more humor?
Do you appear in control?

Take a good look at your nonverbal communication and think about how others may be receiving the messages you are sending.

every referee who ever blew a whistle against Coach Summitt’s Lady Vols. 
  
In fact, former Lady Vols player Kara Lawson said, “Here’s the thing about the stare, about how 
powerful it was. You knew when it was on you, when you couldn’t even see it. There were 
times we would be in the huddle, and I would feel that she was staring at me. And I would say 
to one of my teammates, ‘She’s looking at me right now, isn’t she?’ And they’d say, ‘Yes, she is.’ 
You felt it. You felt like wherever you were.” 
  
Albert Mehrabian is professor emeritus of psychology at UCLA. In his foundational work, Dr. 
Mehrabian and his colleagues studied nonverbal communication in an effort to understand the 
relative impact of facial expressions and spoken words. Mehrabian’s research provided the 
basis for a widely quoted and unfortunately, often much oversimplified statistic on the 
effectiveness of spoken communications. Mehrabian found that 7 percent of a message 
pertaining to feelings and attitudes is in the words that are spoken. Thirty-eight percent of a 
message pertaining to feelings and attitudes is paralinguistic, or the way the words are said. 
And 55 percent of a message pertaining to feelings and attitudes is in facial expression. 
  
The important points of Mehrabian’s work are these: it’s not just the words.  
 
A lot of communication comes through nonverbal communication. Without seeing and hearing 
nonverbal communication, it’s easier to misunderstand the words. When we’re unsure about 
what the words mean, we pay more attention to the nonverbal communication.  
 
Pat Summitt found that listening and being aware of her own nonverbal communication made 
her a better coach. Are you effectively using nonverbal cues and expressions in your own daily 
communication? As you go about your day for the next few days, be cognizant of how your 
nonverbal cues help or hurt your communication as a leader.

It’s Not About Your Great Speech, It’s About Their Great Performance

Once again, it’s important for leaders to recognize that their success will not be driven by their ability to give speeches. While strong verbal skills are always an advantage, leaders are measured by the degree to which they empower and ultimately super-empower other people to perform at high levels. The very best leaders create conditions where their teams blossom and contribute in a way that’s rarely observed in other similar organizations. These leaders are able to get other people to give more and try harder when others are exhausted.

Let's review the keys to effective communication: Authenticity; Relationships; Nonverbal communication.

Key 1 — Authenticity

You can only play a role for so long. What you are, who you are, what you value, and where you invest your emotional energy will become obvious to everyone around you in time. This is why it’s so important to know yourself and to be authentic with others about who you are. When you meet highly successful leaders they are often acutely aware of the values and beliefs that drive their behaviors. They can describe them with certainty because they know that living these values and beliefs is inherent to the journey of successful leader.

Key 2 — Relationships

Relationships are always under construction. If you are just getting to know someone new to your organization or social circle, your investment in getting to know them will pay big dividends in enhancing the relationship. Additionally, the strength and quality of your relationships will influence the quality of your outcomes. By investing in relationships, you are investing in the very fabric of success of every organization or team.

Key 3 — Nonverbal Communication

Many people think they have a good poker face. But, in reality, many of us give away our feelings through our body language. When people go through periods of depression, their posture is rarely in peak form. When people fall in love, their eyes are bright and their steps are light. What’s important to remember here is that it’s very difficult to fake body language. Being your authentic self and investing in your health and well-being make good nonverbals less of a chore. Video technology makes it easy to see ourselves, and when you do, ask yourself whether you would follow the person that you see. Remember, one of the most compelling elements of a leader is the expression of confidence. Confidence is almost impossible to ignore, and in many cases, it is the invisible, quiet, determining factor of success.

Nonverbal Communication (cont.)

As an exercise, you may want to consider visualizing something you’re confident about and examine your face and body when you’re thinking about that element. How do you look when you are feeling confident? Remember that look and hold onto it! As often as you possibly can, try to express that look on your face, or hold your body in that place of confidence. Finally, what is really exciting about this exercise is that it actually becomes predictive of your emotional state. In other words, even if you aren’t feeling particularly confident or calm, successful facial expressions or body language can take you there. Try it!

Practical Thoughts on Communication

In ending this section, it’s helpful to reflect on and revisit some very simple yet profound truths about communication:

1. Shorter messages make a deeper impact

Have you ever opened your phone to a really long text or email? Unless you are in love with the sender, you may feel a sense of dread at having to wade through it all. Don’t feel guilty — it's not about your unwillingness to read a long message. The amount of brainpower it takes to understand a long message, and then reflect on its meaning and intention, represents one of the more complex brain function activities in processing communication. The best leaders today edit themselves to make sure they aren’t using too many words.

2. Simple is better than complex

The most popular website on the planet is Google. Have you ever noticed the simplicity of the homepage? Think of how many gadgets could be attached to the Google homepage. Instead, the aspiration is to make it look simple and accessible. The same is true when it comes to learning. Simplify the game plan and make it accessible to everyone. Boil it down to simple and easily digested steps. When we do this, we increase the opportunity for those learning the ropes to master the fundamentals...and ultimately become masters of what they do.

3. The truth is better than anything

Sticking with the truth makes life so much easier as a leader. When you tell the truth, you don’t have to keep track of your story or manage perception. You can tell the truth and do the best you can to support those around you in dealing with that truth.

Leadership Reflections: Let’s Talk…About Nonverbals

Why is a picture worth 1,000 words? Because in many cases, words can be imprecise. You can describe in a beautifully written essay the majesty of the Swiss Alps, but it doesn't compare to actually standing there and taking it all in. To that end, your nonverbals work the same way. You can tell somebody you love them, but if your facial expression looks like you just chomped on a lemon, they may be less likely to believe your message!

While this may sound like overly simple advice, let's get a little more technical and practical. First, have you ever seen yourself in a video or in a picture and been able to think about what others might see in your face when you look at them?

Think about the facial expressions that you have when you're sitting in a meeting, issuing a directive, or encouraging somebody. If at all possible, take a picture of yourself looking in the mirror and imagining yourself in those positions. While it may not be perfect, it can at least give you some perspective on the facial expressions that you make when you are in these moments. If you can take a picture of these images, it can give you something to reflect on.

As you think about these images, what do you have to say about your own facial expressions?

Do you look interested?
Do you look engaged?

Some people give off facial expressions that don't exactly inspire confidence, or even trust. Good facial expressions sometimes need to be practiced. Take the time to actually reflect on the expressions you see and what you think others may be perceiving of your nonverbal communications.
