Make Winning an Attitude: Introduction, featuring Sue Nokes

Winning Must Become an Attitude
Winning doesn't always look the same, right? Think about it. Sometimes a team may win even when members haven't had the best habits or put forth the best performance. On the other hand, sometimes a team can lose even when members have performed at their very best. Thus, it isn't enough to simply pay attention to the record of "wins and losses." The same is true in business with profit statements. Although profits and corporate financial success are very important to every business, they can't be the only measures of success.

Results are what you get at the end. They are summative. Your attitude, your practice habits, and your preparation all represent formative elements that get you ready for winning and losing. The formative nature of your attitude, then, means it can set you up for success or failure. Developing a winning attitude can have a long-term impact on success.

Note: Your response to the following question is for self-reflection only and will not be saved or graded.

What do you think could happen if a team is made up of members who have superior talent, but negative attitudes? Do any examples come to mind?

That team's negativity will likely result in the team being ill-prepared for tougher times.

Collective Attitudes That Can Make or Break the Team
The attitudes of the individuals who make up teams and organizations compound to form an overall attitude within the team or organization. That collective attitude might be:

positive; thankless; grateful; apathetic; urgent; losing; winning; negative

The attitude a group consistently maintains is arguably its most important predictor of success. Think about it. People with incredibly resilient attitudes usually find ways to make good fortune great and to get through bad fortune without completely derailing their progress. Therefore, leaders must keep in mind that attitude is everything. A negative attitude can consistently lead a team or organization to underperform more easily. A positive attitude will likely do the opposite. A leader must remember that the attitude or emotional state of the team will be a key driving factor of their success.

Winning is a comprehensive attitude that a leader must first build for themselves, and then for everyone around them.

It Takes Practice to Develop a Winning Attitude
You might try to help that person understand the very real impact their negative attitude has on their daily life, right? Maybe instead of focusing on what's wrong, you would hope to help this person start focusing on how they can do things right more often to lead to winning.

This same advice is important for yourself as well. If you find yourself frequently asking "What's wrong?" or "Why am I always losing?," your brain will get really good at constantly seeking answers to these questions in your life, in business, and otherwise. This is why winning people always seem to surround themselves with amazing outcomes. A simple shift can train your brain to consistently look for solutions, better opportunities, happier outcomes, and more relaxing points of view. This simple shift in context amounts to a huge shift in mental and emotional work.

Think, Feel, Say, Act!
We have to get ourselves into the right headspace to visualize winning. In other words, we need to consistently think and feel like winners. We also need to say it — that is, declare what winning looks like and declare that we plan to achieve it. No one ever broke an Olympic weight-lifting record by going to the gym and telling themselves they are weak, they are a loser, and they cannot break the world record. It simply will not happen with that kind of internal monologue.

But it's not enough to just think, feel, and say it. It also takes consistent action. Breaking that weight-lifting record takes more than talking a good talk and watching others put in the work at the gym. It takes getting in there, working hard, and giving it everything. Even then, success is no guarantee — but the only chance of becoming a champion is to live that winning attitude. As a leader, the closer you get to living out that winning attitude daily, the more successful you will be.

Your Outlook Is the Most Predictable Element of Your Potential Success
Your outlook and attitude are extremely strong predictors of your potential success. An individual of average intelligence, skill, and support can become an industry leader if they have an overwhelmingly positive, winning attitude driving them. Their energy and ambition — expressed in their daily outlook and evident to everyone around them — will create a positive climate that's impossible to deny. If you want to change your life and make a powerful impact on your team or organization, adopt a winning attitude that will shine on everyone around you.

Practice Your Belief

Make Winning an Attitude: Practice Your Belief, featuring Timothy Alexander

I Believe. Therefore, I Am.
Everything you have achieved and become in your life is because, at some point, you believed it was possible. Let that sink in. If you graduated from college, at some point in high school (or even earlier), you decided to attend college, truly believing that if you attended and worked hard, you would graduate. If you are married and have a family, you believed at some point that someone would love you, share their life with you, and join you to form a family. That all happened first in your head and heart. Then your belief system allowed you to develop the courage to take action, which you knew would be worth it, because you truly believed it all could happen.

How to Create Belief in Self
Leaders of successful teams and high-performing organizations believe in themselves, and their teams are powered by high performers who likewise believe in their talents and skills. But an individual doesn't simply develop belief after, say, hearing a motivational speaker or reading an encouraging greeting card. Belief takes more than words. Belief requires action. Whether you are a team's leader or a peak performer, you are the first person you must teach the power of belief.

It Takes Consistent Action
We've established that the road to success starts with a dream at the individual level, followed by heart-felt belief that it can happen. Now, we add a third step to make the process look something like this:

Envision the dream of what you want to accomplish.
In your heart, believe you are capable of making that dream a reality.
Take action to make it happen.

Gina, an award-winning track star, thinks she might want to compete in the Olympics.
At first just a passing thought or fanciful idea, Gina recalls a coach or two mentioning they could see her as an Olympic competitor. The idea has sparked.
After some careful reflection, study, and analysis, Gina decides that, with the right work, she could see herself achieving what has become this dream of competing in the Olympics.
She knows the dream is nothing more than wishful thinking if she doesn't put in the work to make it happen. Her next step, obviously, is action.
She may have an enormous amount of work ahead of her to physically and mentally prepare for the rigors of an Olympic tryout.
Gina plans her fitness training. She decides to create a plan to gain sponsors to help pay for coaching to prepare her for this enormous challenge.
Clearly, Gina has a dream, believes she can achieve it, and now faces decisive, consistent, and intentional action to get there.

What About Groups or Teams?
The good news is, the formula is exactly the same for groups or teams:

Dream
Believe
Act

Being part of a team has the added advantage of teammates who can potentially push us to dream bigger and to work even harder than we perhaps would do alone.

Leadership within a major tech company believes they have a team capable of creating a game-changing sales app that could revolutionize their industry.
Leaders and team members chat about the idea and reflect on how it might look to compete with the most dominant apps in their space.
They talk about it enough and analyze the possibilities to the point they confidently believe they can do it.
The idea morphs from casual conversation to a group belief that they can achieve this major accomplishment.
Team members remind each other of their commitment and hold one another accountable for striving toward it.
They agree on the tasks needed to make it happen and they take action on those tasks, pursuing their dream with passion.

The formula of establishing the belief, making the belief a real pursuit, and then taking action will always lead to progress. It may not always lead to game-changing innovation or a guaranteed win, but it will always get the ball rolling in the right direction when pursued by leaders who care about their people and are faithful to the formula.

The Presence of Adversity
Adversity is inevitable, but it's also one of the most powerful forces capable of stimulating innovation. Adversity can come in the form of an unexpected loss or the emergence of a formidable competitor that begins to gobble up market share and challenge the organization's viability. Adversity can emerge after an unforeseen setback, putting the organization in a perilous position where the choice between winning and losing is hanging in the balance. The best companies led by the most forward-thinking leaders, however, understand that it is within these moments of adversity where some of the best outcomes can emerge.

Facing and Beating the Forces of Adversity
There are three steps you could take as a leader or individual performer when facing the negative forces of adversity. Remember, resiliency is the key, and the best leaders understand that learning is a continuous value proposition.

Make it personal.
See every setback as an opportunity to learn.
Expect the extraordinary.

Make it personal
Michael Jordan is one of the most celebrated winners of all time. He exemplifies nearly every concept covered in this leadership training program, but this idea of "making it personal" is perhaps one of his best-known attributes. While some high-profile winners have famously acted poorly when they lost, this is not necessarily a sign of strength. Disappointment is very real, and whenever you work at something and don't achieve it, a passionate person will feel deep pangs of disappointment. However, leaders and top performers who consistently stay in the winners' circle will often find themselves determined to use losses or setbacks as opportunities to grow.

Rather than being angry or pouting about the loss or setback, a smart leader might use these moments to self-scout—that is, take a look at what's being done well and what's being done poorly and evaluate what can be learned from these moments of truth:

- “What can we come away with after losing?”
- “What can we learn from coming away with less than we'd hoped?”
- “What did we fail to do? What did we lack?”

These may sound like negative questions. Rather, they can be formative and strategic questions that will allow the team members to gather themselves and better prepare to win the next time.

Attitude Can Determine Performance

Don’t ever settle for being on "autopilot." Even if you are executing a task you have done several times, the attitude you bring to any moment of the day can influence your performance. For example, if you are exercising and lifting a weight you are accustomed to lifting, your experience can vary dramatically depending on your attitude. If you are optimistic, excited, and somewhat fired up, you might push that weight and make it feel light. If you are exhausted or distracted, the weight might feel heavy.

The same is true with nearly everything in your work. If a customer service agent comes to work after struggling with a child or a difficult marriage counseling session, these events will impact that employee's attitude. To this point, the most successful leaders understand that setting the emotional tenor of an organization can make a huge difference in the attitude team members bring to their work. Strong leaders work hard to build a specific cultural tone within their organizations to help create a winning "attitude" or environment through the system.

For example, creative companies that specialize in art and design often create beautiful, interesting, and stimulating workspaces to help inspire creativity among team members. As a leader, you must identify and employ mechanisms for creating an outstanding attitude for yourself and your team. Investing in culture makes all the difference.

When Positive People Enter a Room, Everything Changes

Numerous studies show that our body temperature, heart rate, and emotions can change when different people enter a room. Happy and engaging people, unsurprisingly, bring happy and engaging vibrations. Angry and toxic people bring negative feelings. Perplexingly, there's almost nothing you can do to avoid at least feeling the vibrations of these people when you're near them. No matter how positive you may be, your psychology will be affected if you are consistently shoulder-to-shoulder with negativity. It can take a lot of energy to fight that off, and it's something to consider when reflecting on those with whom you regularly surround yourself.

As a leader, think about the energy that you bring to every team meeting. Whether it's a video meeting or face-to-face, evaluate your energy by asking yourself things like:

Do I tend to give or take more energy?
Do my colleagues and team members feel inspired and excited to engage with me? Or are they fearful they may get scolded?
Do my team members feel the need to pursue growth? Or do they feel they need to avoid me because I might call them out on things they aren't prepared to discuss yet?

The way you make people feel when you enter the room is really important, and you need to think about the vibrations you hope to put out. Input from trusted colleagues and 360 evaluations (discussed earlier in the course) can give valuable insights into this.

It's really helpful to try to unpack exactly what people think when they see you or the energy you give off. You may imagine yourself as a powerful, positive energy-giver. Attitude Is a Choice, Followed by Action

It may seem cliché, but attitude is a choice. If you're not feeling optimistic and your friend says, "You can feel better if you just decide to," you may not necessarily feel that advice is very helpful. The simple truth is, however, attitude does revolve around the concept of feelings or emotion. If your attitude is positive and affirming, it's because you feel that way. If you're feeling grateful and happy, chances are you will carry that into all you do. Yes, attitude is a choice, but it requires at least some action.

As part of the drug rehabilitation process, a counselor might ask a struggling addict to foster dogs and cats. At face value, it may seem odd to ask someone who is already overwhelmed with repercussions of their addiction to take on responsibilities of caring for animals. However, when the addict takes the actions of consistently caring for something other than themself, it also establishes that same condition or attitude of consistency and caring for themselves. Consistently being there and caring for the animals requires a change in attitude, which can then translate to a change in attitude and behavior for themselves as well.

The message here is simple. Actions matter. If someone with an ungrateful attitude is placed in a position to give things up for people who are less fortunate, it can completely change their perspective and open up their hearts and minds in appreciation of what they have.

The Specificity of the Dream
Most people allow themselves to dream on some level. The difference between casual dreamers and dreamers like Elon Musk is the specificity of the dream, the belief in its possibility, and the relentless pursuit of the end goals.

When certified financial planners sit down and talk to people about their future and ask them what they are saving for, a common answer is, “I want to be rich.” Unfortunately, richness isn't very specific. Wrap-Up

Make Winning an Attitude: Wrap-Up, featuring Sue Nokes

Expecting Loss
Individual and team losses are a natural part of any organization. The best leaders understand that even in championship seasons, losses will come in one form or another. How we respond to losses will have a significant impact on our overall success.

A team can have an undefeated season, yet individuals on the team can find themselves experiencing loss. They can lose a starting position, get injured, or lose focus and be unable to perform as well even in the face of continued winning within the program.

Benchmarking Losses
Obviously, it's easier to quantify success or failure in sports than in business, where benchmarking "wins or losses" isn't as straightforward. Consider these examples:

A company posts robust profits one quarter. Further examination reveals this "win" was due to selling off income-generating assets that could cost profitability in coming quarters.
A company has a less-productive quarter due to internal investments in preparation for gigantic steps forward.
A company launches a new product or service after months of market research, but the new offering doesn't resonate with customers.

Clearly, in business, wins and losses don't always tell the whole story. When it comes to losses, the key is to make sure teams or businesses take time to reflect on them. Losing is hard enough on its own — but then also missing out on the opportunity to grow is almost like losing twice.

What Is Your Story?
Stories are very powerful. Consider an example:

A team wins the national championship.
The very next season, the same team struggles just to get a regular season win.
A newspaper runs the story: "The championship must have been a fluke. The team had one really strong player and once that player graduated from the program, the magic was gone."
Another source says the team has lost its hunger and no longer has it in them to compete on this level.

Stories are also psychological. Everyone you know is living the story of their life. One individual's story might be that they are going to leave their home country, start a new business in a new land, and be wildly successful. Another person's story might be that they and their children are doomed to repeat the fate of their alcoholic parents. Some are living stories that suggest their best days are behind them. For others, their best days are still ahead.

Our stories are powerful because they set our expectations and put us on a pathway that's hard to deny. Remember, a story isn't a dream. A story is something that you live. While a dream might have gotten you to start living your story, a "story" is more linear.

Each of us lives the story we have established for ourselves. If you're looking to change your fortune as a leader, thinking deeply and honestly about the story you have told yourself about your life is perhaps one of the most powerful things you can do. If you've told yourself you lead a division within a company that won't let you be as effective as possible, you will likely continue with that exact path. Or, if you told yourself a very different story, you're likely to pursue that as well. The first step is acknowledging that your story has authentic elements that can drive your ultimate success. And if you want to change your life... change your story.

Are You Living Your Comeback Story?
Everyone loves the story of a comeback, rising from defeat or from the ashes of obscurity and achieving victory. The Rocky film franchise is based on the story of an athlete who rises up in the face of unlikely odds. Rocky lives a story on his own terms and doesn't allow the story others would write for him to manifest. He takes action and generates a different future for himself. As a leader, recognize that your business and every team member is living out their story. Their stories, at least partly crafted by your strong leadership, have a huge impact on how the story ends.

What story are you living? What about your story are you happy about? What would you like to change?

Thank you! You might also save this response in your workbook.

Convincing Others That They Are Writing This Story, Not Somebody Else
To help a team lead its comeback story, a strong leader must first convince team members that they are in charge of their own stories. This is harder than it sounds. In many cases, individuals tell themselves a story that someone else is in charge of the story of their lives — perhaps they would be more successful if somebody wasn't writing their story for them.

Once a leader convinces their followers that they are in charge of their story, and that by rewriting the story of their lives, they can have virtually any destiny, their results can be amazing. Small victories and achievable elements of their story will begin to convince them that, indeed, they are in charge. When you can empower them with this truth, as a leader, you will be giving them the "keys to the kingdom" to control their own destinies.

Leadership Reflections: What Do You Do with Your Losses?

Losses happen. You can lose out on an opportunity to sell a client your product. You can pursue a new job promotion and fail to achieve your objective. In this module, we learned that high-achieving leaders treat losses differently. They see them as setbacks that opened the door for new levels of growth. Put another way, they see a loss as only temporary and the pathway towards victory is just ahead.

Most of us don't handle losses perfectly in our personal and/or professional lives. For this reflection, think back on a time when you were unsuccessful.
