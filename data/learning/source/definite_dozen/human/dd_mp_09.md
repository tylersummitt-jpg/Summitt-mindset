Teamwork is really a form of trust.
Pat Summitt

Trust Falls Aren’t Enough

Some leaders, even with good intentions, may hold an annual meeting where certain activities are designed to build teamwork and improve the overall ethic of working together. These ceremonial activities or the equally popular enthusiasm of a motivational speaker won't be enough to truly build great teams. The construction of great teams comes from a strategic sense of purpose, the deliberate construction of teams, and the ongoing reinforcement of teams as family, every week and every day.

Let’s Get into the Details: What Does Our Team Do?

Getting into the details regularly is an important part of making sure teams have specific purposes and protocols. In business, for example, it's important to ensure teams are constructed in a way that every individual on the team has a purpose and a function, and that their purpose and function is demonstrated every time they get together.

What to Do with the Weakest Player? (aka, All of Us Could Be the Weakest Player)

It is said that any group is only as strong as its weakest link. While on the basketball court, that weak link may consistently be one person. In the world of business, it's often not that simple. In fact, when it comes to the weakest link, sometimes it can be entirely situational. There may be a season where a particular department within an organization simply has a difficult week or month. There could be a scenario where unique stress is placed on a department due to issues beyond their control. Suddenly, the organization is seen as weak in that particular area.

The adage that an organization is only as strong as its weakest link is certainly true, but there are two important things for leaders to keep in mind.

The weakest link, even if it's only situational, is only as painful and harmful to the organization as the team allows it to be. If a team works together consistently and effectively, those weak moments won't damage the overall progress of the group.
The weakest link could be any one of us at any given moment. Even the most consistent hero might need a great deal of support (and even saving) in an unforeseen circumstance. Furthermore, anyone within the organization is capable of stepping up and delivering in the face of certain moments of challenge. The best leaders create conditions where this is understood and consistently exemplified.

Put the Team Before Yourself: Introduction

Put the Team Before Yourself: Introduction, featuring Dr. Keith Gray

To me, teamwork is a lot like being part of a family. It comes with obligations, entanglements, headaches, and quarrels. But the rewards are worth the cost.
Pat Summitt

The Family You Choose
We are all born into a family. However, there's a second family in our lives. They are the people that we choose. Strong leaders understand that the instinct to build a family is very powerful, and taking advantage of it in terms of building strong teams can help lead to unprecedented success.

Your Family Has a Bond
With blood relatives, your bond is your genetic lineage and your family experiences. This can be the most powerful bond in your life. Your second family, which can be your team and/or your social circle, is likewise held together by a powerful bond. This bond is what makes all the difference in our lives. Chances are that bond has to do with some sort of collective experience that you shared together, a common moment or aspiration that you locked arms and worked together towards. Leaders need to remember that new families can be constructed when bonds are deliberately formed. This means that the best leaders bring groups together and give them those experiences that create the emotional bond.

Bonding Experiences
You and I have heard the term, "bonding experiences." You may have even been in a group activity involving some sort of bonding exercise designed to bring people together during the learning experience. When these are done well, they can work to bring a group together effectively. The best leaders and coaches understand that, when they bring a disparate group of people together with the collective aspiration of achieving a goal, a bonding experience or series of experiences is required to create the emotional tie that will hold them together. To that end, as a leader, you must remember that the degree to which your team will bond together and become a family will be driven directly by the quality, consistency, and sheer number of bonding experiences that pull them together and help them to perform as one. Without these collective experiences, the connectivity simply won't be as strong, and that family won't likely hold together during tough times. Build the bond, build the family, and enjoy the results.

Think about people in your life whom you consider family members by choice. What shared experiences led you to the emotional bond that holds you close to them? In your team, what experiences can you create to help the team develop emotional bond?

Families and Conflict

Now let's talk about conflicts happening in families and in teams. Which do you think is a sign of healthy team dynamics?

Team members never have conflicts.
Team members have occasional conflicts.
Team members always have conflicts.

Incorrect. Correct answer: Team members have occasional conflicts. Your answer: Team members never have conflicts.

Correct. Correct answer: Team members have occasional conflicts. Your answer: Team members have occasional conflicts.

Your chosen family will never be free of conflict. In fact, relationships that don't include conflict at all are not necessarily the healthiest ones. Without conflict, there's an implication that members of the family aren't close enough to be honest and get real with each other. As we all know, when you "get real," you run the risk of either offending or disrupting the status quo...and that disruption is where all the growth occurs. If you're not close enough to have a real conversation with someone, how close are you, really? Keeping everything polite and at a distance may be appropriate in certain social settings, but with teams that are trying to achieve at the highest level, that moment of raw honesty is essential. If you're truly going to take advantage of the team-building that's required to build a powerful family or team, you have to be willing to endure and embrace conflict.

High Regards and High Expectations
Your chosen family, when constructed in an optimal way, holds each member of the family or the team in high regard. First, team members know each other well enough to observe and appreciate the elements that make each person valuable to the team. They hold those assets or attributes close to their hearts, and they respect what they bring to the team. Second, the concept of high expectations is extremely important. When you care about somebody, you have high expectations for their future. The best leaders create conditions where the entire team or family pulls for each other after recognizing a skill or ability in an individual member. High regards and high expectations of each other within a team help it build that family bond and make it stronger.

Teamwork is really a form of trust. It's what happens when you realize that you wouldn't achieve your individual goals without the support of your colleagues.
Pat Summitt

Compromise and Collaboration
Compromise — A team member makes a sacrifice to support another team member at their own expense.
Collaboration — Team members successfully work together towards a common goal.

Shared Success

When teams learn to work together and operate like a family, their success becomes a shared success. The good thing about shared success isn't just that it creates a condition where celebrating good news is emotionally rewarded, it also creates a situation where team members get more actively involved in helping each other arrive at their destinations.

Checked vs. Unchecked Egos
Finally, one of the common denominators in making teams work within a family is observance of checked versus unchecked egos. An unchecked ego won't allow the appreciation of others to emerge for fear that they will somehow miss out on praise. The members of the team who have such fragile egos that they're unwilling to celebrate others see good news or compliments as limited commodities. An abundance of that commodity shining away from them creates nervousness and resentment.
Clearly, on effective teams, egos are checked at the door, and people are willing to set aside their own success for the success of the team. They don't worry about their personal praise as much as they worry about the collective success of the team, knowing that their moment will likely come, and to be patient until it does. Obviously, this isn't easily achieved. There are certain people who are so fragile from an ego standpoint that they will never allow their egos to go unchecked, and these individuals may never fit into a team or be part of a winning team culture.
However, there are others who simply have never been taught what it means to care for others and connect, and once they are given the opportunity to do so, they relish it. It forever changes their outlook and how they connect with people. Strong leaders help others learn to check their egos and appreciate other people...and everyone involved grows as a result.

Building a Great Team
Create regular opportunities for bringing the team together and giving team members quality experiences that create emotional bonds and a sense of unity.
Foster honesty and trust in team communication.
Do not leave team conflicts unresolved.
Teach team members to compromise and collaborate.
Create an environment where team members respect each other and recognize each other's skills and abilities.
Create an environment where team members help each other succeed and share each other's success.
Help team members learn to keep egos checked and to express appreciation for their teammates.

Make it clear from the outset that no individual on the team is more important than the other.
Reinforce the notion that no individual is as good as the collective power and capacity of the team.

All three actions will help you cultivate a team-oriented mindset among the team members.

Teams Achieve More Than Individuals
Successful teams are made up of highly talented individuals. As we have suggested before, a championship team or industry-leading group doesn't achieve this status without assembling a collection of talented contributors. However, we have all seen talented individuals who make terrible team members. Leaders must understand that it isn't enough to simply group individuals together and call them a team. A team is something that has to be thoughtfully created, and its foundation needs to be nurtured, revisited, and reinforced.

Testing the Theory
Basketball coaches have often run famous drills with the fastest players dribbling the basketball and establishing a contest where blazing dribblers race down the court and try to beat the speed of teammates who passed the ball. Suffice it to say, even the fastest Division 1 athlete can't outrun the speed of a ball moving from one teammate to another, carefully placed with knowledge of where they want to go. The goal of an assignment like this is obviously to learn to pass. But it's more than that. It's also to learn that the most powerful individual isn't as powerful as a well-crafted, strategic team. Leaders must take the time to reinforce this notion and show members of the team that, by working together, there's nothing they can't achieve; and no matter how powerful one individual member of the team may be, no individual is as good as the collective work or capacity of the team.

You can train yourself to become a team player — by keeping a disciplined eye on the larger goal and realizing that when you help each other, you help yourselves.
Pat Summitt

There Are Individual Benefits to Team Membership
Even the most selfless team member becomes more dedicated to the team family when they begin to enjoy the individual benefits of being part of a team. Some of those benefits include extra levels of support, emotional engagement, and the closeness and positive stimulation that comes from having team members who are there to encourage them. The combination of social support and technical engagement makes team membership unbelievably valuable to individual team members. Leaders should support these benefits and illustrate them on a regular basis.

In your organization, how do the individuals benefit from team membership? As a leader, how would you make them aware of these benefits?

Investing in Others

Every Family Needs Heroes
Heroes tend to get a lot of attention and, to some degree, they should. If your team doesn't have a few heroic figures who are willing to provide industry-leading performance and insights, it isn't likely that your business endeavor will be successful. Even with the numerous basketball examples provided, having a team full of unselfish players is wonderful, but if none of them can emerge as the hero, the chance of abiding success is weakened. Thus, leaders should understand that heroes are required. Indeed, every team who becomes a family needs to look for the heroes in their midst and work together to nurture those heroes so they are ready to perform when the moment arrives!

Some Days, Different Heroes Emerge
In the world of business, heroes can emerge at different times, from all directions. Approaching the end of the fiscal year, for example, the high-flying accounting department might wear the hero’s cape. During the middle of a logistics disaster, a dedicated set of administrative assistants may come through in the clutch and overcome what might have been a daunting technical issue. In healthy, well-led organizations, these heroes are always ready to emerge when needed. In other words, that heroic individual within the organization may play the role of bystander or support most of the time. However, they need to work in an organization that lets them know that when their time comes, it will be essential for them to perform. Time and again, the best leaders have found themselves in difficult situations, only to be bailed out by unexpected members of their team who showed up with heroic strength to remedy difficult situations.

Anytime Heroes
Without question, heroes can emerge anytime. In the best organizations, there is an understanding that heroism does not require permission. Someone can just step up and do their job in a way to deliver the desired outcome. Can people make mistakes? Of course. But which type of organization would you rather work in — one that consistently holds you back? Or, would you rather work in an organization where the leader sets the parameters and encourages everyone to own the journey and behave like heroes every day?

Every Family Needs Pacesetters
In this module, we discussed the emergence of Roger Bannister, breaking the previously unthinkable barrier of running a sub-four-minute mile. As the story goes, Bannister was able to set this pace throughout his record-breaking run by having dedicated pacesetters, running just ahead of him, creating a condition where he was more likely to push himself to keep up and ultimately break the record. The term pacesetter is important here. Leaders need to know that heroes can go farther and faster as long as those around them operate as pacesetters, helping them break records. The leader must once again be exceedingly clear with the team that, when the hero's time has arrived, it is important for everyone to not only graciously appreciate the effort, but do anything they can to accelerate the pace and maximize the possibility.

Roger Bannister reaches the finish line in a 1953 race. Source: Wikimedia.

So, how can you be a pacesetter? Simply support your heroes in their moment. For example, if some dedicated administrative assistants have rolled up their sleeves and are taping boxes and delivering packages due to the logistics nightmare, others in the organization may hear about their efforts and stop what they are doing, run down to the warehouse, and pitch in. The CEO and vice president may also contribute to the effort. Everyone can be a hero and everyone can be a pacesetter. Depending on the time of day and the challenge ahead, we all have to stand ready to deliver whatever is needed.

The Power of Caring
One of the amazing benefits of being a pacesetter for a hero is to demonstrate the power of caring. In the logistics example, above, imagine how the vice president of logistics felt when others jumped in to help? Imagine how administrative assistants felt when everyone stopped what they were doing, including the CEO, and helped deliver boxes so that everyone looked good in the process. This shows a genuine caring for one another and a willingness to do whatever it takes to get the job done. This is the stuff of high-performing groups and teams, and it builds family bonds that will last forever.

No one cares how much you know until they know how much you care.
Teddy Roosevelt

Put the Team Before Yourself: Carrots and Sticks, featuring Virginia Means

Carrots and Sticks
Longstanding managerial theory has reflected on the role of "carrots and sticks" in motivating people to perform at higher levels. It was understood under this theory that carrots served to lead and motivate people, and sticks were there to threaten, poke, and cajole others into behaving in a way that was expected or preferred.

When Carrots Work
Carrots tend to work in motivating people when the task is relatively simple, or when used as team-building motivation, as opposed to being the ultimate reward.

When Carrots Don't Work
Carrots have been seen as less effective over time because human beings tend to consume the carrot and then begin to be less motivated unless a bigger carrot is offered. When carrots are used to motivate people in their work, people may end up working for carrots rather than for each other. Ultimately, this keeps us from being as connected as possible to our purpose, and it doesn't lead to long-term growth that might otherwise be achieved.

When Sticks Work
Sticks, or the threat of punishment, exist in many forms in our lives. Does the fear of punishment keep us from driving too fast on the highway or in front of a school? Probably so. You might be genuinely concerned about the cost and the inconvenience of getting a ticket.

The Stick as Positional Power

In reflecting on this concept, think about the stick as positional power. If you are a leader in the organization, you likely have some positional authority over those you work with. You may have the right or authority to provide them with a raise or to even fire them from their position. You might control key elements of their working conditions that matter a lot to them. As a result, you have a "stick" to control them in terms of your positional power.

Because of this power, you could start making demands on those you work with by letting them know that by failing to comply with your expectations, they could run the risk of losing your favor and miss out on some of the opportunities that you hold over their heads. Some individuals will respond to this.

When Sticks Don't Work
In terms of motivation, sticks don't work. Why? Because the person with the stick is generally the "enemy" and one's performance is driven by the desire to avoid pain rather than trying to ascend towards something positive or affirming.

Does having a stick over your head motivate you to work at your most connected, heartfelt, team-based best? The answer is no. Avoiding the slings and arrows of punishment isn't as powerful as locking arms with a teammate and racing toward a better outcome.

The Stick Comes From Behind
Lastly, in thinking about the stick, remember it tends to come from behind. This means the leader tends to watch the progress, and if progress isn't demonstrated to their liking, the stick is either used or threatened in order to create the conditions the leader wants. When the stick is delivered from behind it is entirely transactional and reactionary. Of course, the best leaders understand that planning for higher performance is much better than waiting for the exception and threatening or delivering a punishment.

Authority
When a leader has authority, they have the ability to inspire or move those whom they work with. For example, if a new leader comes into an organization and immediately demonstrates a high degree of knowledge and understanding, individuals may begin to defer to that leader and work diligently to operate in alignment with the goals of the new leader, out of respect and understanding that this leader can be trusted.

Authority Is Earned, Power Is Wielded
As illustrated, authority is something that is earned within an organization. It can come to both formal and informal leaders. How many times have you worked in an organization where an individual without a formal title has a huge amount of authority simply because of how much others respect that individual's thoughts and opinions?

Power, however, isn't earned. It's wielded. In other words, an individual taking advantage of their power will demand compliance, or otherwise administer some sort of negative outcome, or a punishment delivered.

Authority is always better than power. This is not to say that there won't be times as a leader where you need to use your positional authority to make a decision or to move an individual. This can happen especially when the individual is working or behaving inconsistently with team or company standards. This can result in difficult confrontations, but indeed, there are circumstances where this degree of power is needed.

Authority Is Out Front
While the stick is reactionary and is delivered when the leader notices that performance isn't up to their prescribed standard, authority is more anticipatory. Authority is provided "out front," showing the pathway forward to better outcomes and achievements.

We vs. Me, or Selfless vs. Selfish
The best teams are made up of dedicated individuals who recognize that there is true power in "we." One of the first steps a team must take is to overcome selfishness.

The journey toward building a great team isn't easy. Every individual has needs, and the observance of those needs is important. Make Teaming an Integral Part of the Organization
Teaming is a strategic approach to running an organization that requires training, observance, evaluation, and the ability to overcome mistakes. Without a doubt, organizations that get serious about teaming often run into challenges. It takes time and effort to build successful teams.

Can you imagine what it would be like if you had a large family, always eating separately and never really communicating, suddenly being pulled together into family therapy? The results would be rather bumpy. However, most thoughtful leaders make a commitment to teaming and never look back. Teams Have Roles
Team success is largely driven by the ability of the team members to embrace and execute various roles. For example, there should be opportunities for each person to lead the group. To have only one team member in charge all the time doesn't give everyone the opportunity to be fully engaged. During meetings, roles can be assigned, such as note taker, facilitator, time keeper, and even "innovator."

This notion of high-functioning teams, rotating roles, and collaborative engagement has been at the heart of much of the software development in Silicon Valley. The ability of these organizations' team members to consistently work together, share leadership, push themselves to innovate, and collectively resolve conflicts and challenges is at the heart of what it means to be a great team. It's working in all sorts of organizations today, and the best leaders are at the center of it.

Teamwork Is Taught and Modeled by Leaders
Hopefully, this unit has given you a leader's perspective on the importance of teamwork. Teams must be constructed and teamwork must be taught. This cannot be taken for granted or left to chance. Holding teams accountable for what they have been taught makes all the difference. If a leader values teams, they obviously value the collective voices of those they work with. In some cases, a leader will join a team and insist that their role is that of a pacesetter or support role. They may "join the team" to serve the organization as the leader, but they are there to serve and encourage. This sends an incredibly powerful message to everyone involved that teamwork, role consideration, and learning together is the work of everyone. It's not hierarchical. It is collaborative and done together.

Teamwork is not created by like-mindedness. As in a family, you have to be generous enough to take pleasure in someone else's success, not just your own. You have to have the smarts to realize that no one succeeds alone. Pat Summitt
