On the Road to Success, You Never Arrive

It’s bittersweet to realize that on the road to success, you never reach your destination. But if a leader doesn't appreciate their progress or a benchmark that they've reached, those alongside them may begin to feel burned out. Reaching a winning milestone needs to be warmly celebrated and embraced. By never truly "arriving," you position yourself for continuous growth.

Failure and Success Can Bring Out the Best and Worst

Legendary UCLA basketball coach John Wooden famously said, “Sports don't build character; they reveal it.” This is very true. Have you ever heard of an athlete or elite leader who achieves success, only to burn out soon after because their character couldn't handle it? Enduring champions reveal something different about themselves in pinnacle moments, namely working just as hard to defend their crown as they did to earn it. All matters of success tend to follow this trend.

How do you evaluate your team members' abilities to handle success and failure? By being a careful observer. As a leader, create conditions where you can observe individuals on your team and see how they respond to reaching success or experiencing failure. For example:

- How does a high-potential employee respond when they don't get the promotion they want?
- How does a team member respond when suddenly given the opportunity to supervise a group or own a major project?

You can harvest these revealing behaviors when the time is right.

Learn to Welcome Both Success and Failure

Learning, growth, and evolution are always at the heart of great success. Next-level success happens when dedication and intelligence meet learning, followed by adjustment, and adjustment again. This lesson is all about taking advantage of salient learning moments — in this case, both successes and failures. For athletics, competition presents the opportunity to measure progress. Wins and losses represent key benchmarks. The very best leaders put both winning and losing moments under a microscope and use both outcomes as chances to reflect, grow, and ultimately evolve.

Losses: Tantrums for Testimony

No one with even the slightest competitive spirit likes to lose. In fact, if losing doesn't impact you emotionally, how invested are you in winning? The lack of emotional engagement will keep you from the training and preparation needed to bring the best of yourself to competition.

That being said, we've all seen examples, particularly in sports, of leaders who don't respond well to losing. The moments where temper tantrums are caught on video certainly help illustrate what happens when a passionate leader gets caught up in the moment and frustration takes over their bodies. It isn't pretty, and sometimes it can cost a career.

On a more positive note, a loss can give you the opportunity for reflections and heartfelt gut-checks, leading to improvement. If you study their biographies, you'll find that many of the most successful winners had to experience more than a little losing to better understand what it takes to grow. There are few champions in any walk of life who are able to get to the winners' circle unscathed, undefeated, and without a few painful scars. The trick, obviously, is to prepare to win, and win often, however you define "winning." Then, when you don't win, respond thoughtfully, gracefully, and redouble your efforts in getting where you need to go.

When you fail, you have a natural tendency to examine it more closely. You analyze it. You critique it. You look at every second of your performance, every possession, and every turnover. I'll tell you straight out, I've learned more from losing than from winning.
Pat Summitt

In this lesson, we will talk about the importance of reflecting on "what's next" after you win. What are the most successful practices that top performers and forward-thinking leaders embrace? They learn the process of continuously evaluating the merits of their winning ways, reflecting on what got them to the winners' circle.

Unfortunately, we don't automatically learn from winning, and it can actually be harder to learn from winning than from losing. When winning, we tend to take a breath and feel like simply repeating the steps, even if they aren't quite as intense as the previous time, will be enough. Furthermore, a bit of ego can also begin to emerge. Team members may have been laser-focused on the process of winning, but then the win itself made them become ego-driven or credit-seeking.

Leadership Challenges

Let's review three concepts that may present common leadership challenges and examine how leaders can address them. For each card below, think what the term might mean and then select the card to reveal the definition.

Fundamental attribution error
Here, attributing a person's success or failure to their personal traits, rather than to the situational factors.

Overconfidence bias
Here, belief that a moment of success will automatically lead to more success.

Failure to ask "Why" questions
Here, the tendency not to investigate the causes of good performance systematically.

Fundamental Attribution Error

When it comes to team or organizational success, it's important to avoid a fundamental attribution error — a human tendency to attribute our own and others' behaviors to personality traits and abilities while underestimating the role of situational factors. Let's take a basketball game win as an example.

Basketball Win: Let's Unpack the Process

If a player makes a game-winning shot, it's easy to get lost in the glorification of that shot and the shooter as the buzzer sounds. It's fun to put an almost mystical quality on the shooter, adding them to legend. In reality, in order for that shooter to hit that shot, a lot of things had to happen in the process.

- The shooter would have to work unusually hard to develop a shot that goes into the basket consistently. Additionally, that shot has to be released quickly so that defenders can't consistently knock it down. Then, the shooter has to have the ability to shake a defender, get open, and handle pressure, understanding that even if they are a great shooter, it's likely that four times out of 10, the shot is not going to go in.
- The shot isn't possible without the team running a play, setting screens, and expertly getting the shooter the ball at the right time to make the game-winning point. If it's at the college level, recruiters would have had to bring in the right people and the coach would have had to run the right play to get them all pulling together.
- The strength and conditioning coach would have to make sure that they weren't so tired at the end of the game that they couldn't hit the shot.
- The nutritionists would need to make sure that the pre-game meal was enough to power them through the game without bogging them down or making them sleepy or slow.

There are, of course, a myriad of other things that have to happen in order for that winning shot to drop. A successful coach would recognize all the things that went into hitting that shot and would do everything necessary to make sure that all those moving parts are either as good or better the next time around.

How to Avoid Fundamental Attribution Errors

Winning teams consistently observe the process it took to make a victory and study the process for the next time around. The same is true in business. Studying all the moving parts and focusing on the process makes the outcome more likely to become predictable and consistent. The way to avoid fundamental attribution errors lies in a deeper analysis of the formula for success. This requires honesty and research. There may have been a certain amount of luck or coincidence that created a unique moment in time for the victory. You can avoid fundamental attribution errors by truly studying the variables that led to your success.

Overconfidence Bias

Overconfidence bias emerges when an individual or a group experiences some form of victory, and that leads them to believe that they can maintain the same level of success in the future, without any extra effort. Winning at any level can be deceiving. We all have experienced some overconfidence bias somewhere along the way. We get a taste of success and assume it gets easy...and then we are corrected. This is human nature and happens to all of us. It's important to be aware of the overconfidence bias effect and be ready to respond to it.

I want our team to experience victory, and to develop some confidence. And yet, right about the time that they have the world by the tail, just when they have it all figured out, I don't mind if they get knocked down. It's the best lesson they can learn.
Pat Summitt

How to Address Overconfidence Bias

Strong leaders unpack these situations and can remedy them sooner. The quickest way to resolve an overconfidence bias is for that person to experience a little bit of a loss and then pull themselves together, build up their confidence, and get to the next level. Convince them that, as a result of this setback, they may need to reinforce the processes it took to get to that level before and to assume that it is reachable again.

Asking "Why Did We Win?" Questions

The best leaders and peak performers don't fall into the trap of taking their successes for granted. Instead, they take time to understand why they found success. Without answers to why, it is very difficult to repeat success or take it to the next level. Coach Summitt explains it like this:

A lot of people succeed once, but never understand why. They don't examine what they did right or wrong. It's important to define your method and know what you did right, so you can reemphasize it. It's amazing how much intensity comes from what you emphasize. If you pay fierce attention to a few important fundamentals, you can fight complacency. The most successful organizations tend to have a signature. They do two or three things extremely well. They don't try to be all things to all people. You can try to do fifty things not very well. Or you can emphasize a few things to perfection.
Pat Summitt

Handle Success Like You Handle Failure: Learning from Success and Failure, 
featuring Greg Hiebert 
 
Greg Hiebert, Leadership Coach & Managing Partner 
We know the importance of learning from failure, as Pat so eloquently pointed out. Yet surprisingly, 
learning from success can present even greater challenges. Pat also said, “It's much harder to handle 
success than it is to handle failure. You have to learn to handle them alike. Only by placing success and 
failure in their proper perspectives can you maintain the principles and priorities you arrived with.” 
That's why I want to examine a commonly made leadership mistake. Too often people get blinded by 
their success and start believing they are infallible due to their brilliance and overconfidence. 
The first challenge is what psychologists call fundamental attribution errors. When we succeed, we're 
likely to conclude that our talents and our current strategy are the reasons for our success. However, we 
tend to underestimate the role that external factors, situational influences, and random events may 
have played. 
The second challenge is overconfidence bias. Success increases our self-assurance and, while confidence 
in ourselves is a great thing, too much can make us believe we don't need to change. 
The third challenge is the Failure to Ask Why syndrome, the tendency not to investigate the causes of 
good performance systematically. When leaders suffer from this syndrome, they fail to ask the tough 
questions that would help them alter their assumptions and even worse, create the conditions where 
dissention is not allowed.  
Pat Summitt outright refused to engage in the Failure to Ask Why syndrome. In fact, not only did Pat ask, 
"Why did we lose” – she just as regularly asked, “Why did we win?” In an effort to understand the 
team's performance, Pat was known to relentlessly review film after both wins and losses to determine 
the source of the team's success or failure.  
There's nothing wrong with celebrating your success, but if you only revel in your wins, you've missed a 
huge opportunity to investigate what led to success with the same scrutiny, the same rigor you might 
apply to understand the causes of failure. As Coach Summitt said, “The reason success is so hard to 
duplicate is because we tend to stop doing the disciplined things that made us successful in the first 
place.”

Fear of Failure Is Logical and Real

Picture an actor, athlete, or business leader who has fought the good fight and is on the verge of a major win. Just at the last moment, they struggle and fail to claim the prize! We've all seen it happen. The person's hesitation or perhaps private self-sabotage can come out of an abiding fear of failure.

We can't always predict how an individual will respond as they near a victory. Consider a couple of examples:

- A peak performer stands in the face of a seemingly impossible, pressure-filled situation. They respond with smiling grace, deliver stellar performance, and win without reservation.
- A team plays in the championship and the star player stands with the ball on the line. Victory comes down to one shot. The player, who has easily and routinely made the same shot before, misses and the team loses the championship.

The truth is, this fear is real. Fear is a psychological dynamic that’s present in everyone to some degree.

Overcoming the Fear

There are many ways to overcome psychological fears. Arguably, the most often-applied method is familiarity. As our familiarity with what we fear increases, our fear likely decreases.

Think of a child with her family at the pool. She wants to have fun with her family and friends, but she has a problem: she's afraid of the water. If someone teaches her to swim, she will face her fears and the familiarity will relieve her uncertainties. Imagine all the fun she'll have, splashing and playing in the water. Her family may have to make this child, who was previously held back by fear, leave the pool to go do other activities.

The same is true in other life situations. Imagine it's the first day of your new job. You know you can do the work and you are excited to start this new adventure. At the same time, though, now that your first day is here, deep down, you are a bit fearful something may go wrong today. As you perform the expected job functions, though, you become more and more confident and your fear of failure is relieved.

How do we breed familiarity? Practice! When we practice, we develop the skills needed to respond in any related situation.

Suppose Charles works for a brokerage firm that sells exchange securities. He enjoys his job and is relatively successful, but every time he faces a customer, he feels anxious that they may ask him a question that is he unable to answer. How does he resolve that fear? Preparation and hard work. He might:

- meet with his manager or a trusted colleague for some tips and pointers;
- study documentation available on the company's internal information hub to be sure he's ready for most anything customers may ask; or
- watch some online videos or take some refresher courses on relevant topics in his industry.

Coaches often help their players overcome fear of taking the last shot in a game by having them practice that scenario beforehand. The coach sets up a buzzer and creates the conditions of an all-on-the-line, game-defining moment, giving players the opportunity to simulate that kind of pressure.

Fear of Success

Psychologists have found that the fear of success is more common than many of us may realize. Outwardly, we may declare that we want to succeed at something, yet subconsciously, we may be a bit nervous about what will happen if we do succeed. We might have thoughts like:

- If I succeed, I might get asked to do it again. What if I can't?
- What if I succeed and I'm suddenly thrown into a leadership role? What if I can't continue holding up to those standards or consistently succeed at this enormous goal? Will I end up looking foolish?
- What if I achieve my goals and people start feeling uncomfortable around me or even reject me?

Fear of success can be difficult to understand because most of us tell ourselves we want success. We may not even be aware that, deep down, we would rather not achieve that big goal because we're afraid of what might happen if we do. Strong leaders get themselves ready to respond to both victories and failures. The more they openly discuss it with team members, the better prepared they will be for both wins and losses.

Failure Is a Friend That Teaches

Have you ever had a particularly communicative friend? You likely valued your personal connection with them and learned from their trusted wisdom. Great leaders and peak performers understand that failure can actually be that kind of friend. It's not that you'll want to welcome failure, but when it arrives, treat it like a friend. Renew your acquaintance and learn whatever you can from the situation.

There's no advantage to beating yourself up after a failure. Instead, you'll end up psychologically exhausted and less energized to meet future challenges. Your effort is better spent on allowing that failure to be a communicative friend, ready to inform and teach you. Maybe you gave it your all, but you focused on the wrong things. Maybe you had excellent intensity, but a poor strategy that just didn't work. Let that failure be your friend to help discover what to do better next time.

The key to doing this effectively is to minimize the emotional tumult you bring to the analysis. Even if you discover you poorly executed some things that were in your control, acknowledge it and analyze what you can do differently next time. Let the failure motivate and energize you. Unfortunately, good leaders have been known to give up their sport or career out of the pain of having failed in a season. Had they listened to their failure as a friend, they may have discovered they were only a slight adjustment away from a complete reversal of fortunes.

Take Some Risks and Plan to Hit the Floor

You never would have learned to walk if, as a baby, you hadn't stood up and risked falling. You might have fallen and cried, but you gathered yourself and tried again. One of the great merits of youth is that we know we might get knocked down, but we'll hopefully have the energy to get back up. Unfortunately, as time marches on, we see "falling" as beneath us. We expect that we know enough by now and shouldn't have to put ourselves out there anymore. In reality, though, we will only see better outcomes once we are willing to "wobble before we walk" and gather ourselves when we fall (because we will, at times).

Excelling as a leader in any profession requires taking some risks:

- Are there some things that you're doing even now that make you a bit nervous?
- Are you asking yourself, "Am I crazy for trying this? Is this out of line?" If you don't feel a little bit of that along the way, you're probably not risking enough.

Successful people are willing to take some risks. In fact, the most successful people on the planet are willing to do things every day that might make those around them nervous. If you are brave enough to try new things, you undoubtedly will hit the floor more than someone who doesn't. But you're also likely to get over the wobbling, start walking, and maybe even run — all while the competition is still crawling and unwilling to risk failure.

Has Success Gone to Your Head? Let’s Hope So

Have you ever received the advice, "Don't let success go to your head?" Typically, someone saying this is suggesting not to take success for granted, become conceited, look down on others, or act superior or unbecoming. From that regard, it's good advice. But we're going to challenge it just for a moment and suggest that success does need to go to your head in one particular way: when you succeed, think about how you got there.

- How did you arrive at success?
- What created the conditions that led to success?

The point here is that success is the reward for hard work, discipline, and focus. Following success, the best leaders stop to consider what it took to get there and what needs to be done to ensure it wasn't luck or coincidence.

Note: Your response to the following question is for self-reflection only and will not be saved or graded.

Pinpoint the Sources of Success

While reflecting on a success, it's critical to identify what factors enabled that success.

- Was it your effort?
- Was it the effort of your team members?
- Were there environmental factors or other variables that created the conditions for success?
- If so, are those variables likely to still be at play and is success replicable?

Consider an example:

Suppose you are the head of a department in a company that just experienced an unbelievably fortuitous windfall.

This resulted in a large influx of cash and greatly improved the bottom line for the quarter.

The board of directors reviews the results and congratulates you on a fantastic quarter.

You take a step back and think about what brought about such amazing results.

What if the success came as a result of market conditions or environmental variables outside your control and you just happened to be the one in charge?

That kind of success may or may not be sustainable or replicable.

Gratitude in Success

Earlier, we challenged the adage of not letting success go to one's head solely in the sense that we should reflect deeply upon our success. However, we would be amiss to ignore the face value of the saying as well: there's a certain humility in a leader who looks at success with gratitude.

Have you ever known of someone who entered the winner's circle and failed to give credit to the biggest contributors to that success? They have forgotten, ignored, or taken for granted the myriad of other people and factors that led to their success. When a leader takes all the credit in this way, it brings about two obvious problems:

- The success is less likely to be repeated.
- The people and mechanisms that supported the success may be weakened or even absent next time.

Instead, if a championship coach or successful leader wins big and credits, by name, those who were instrumental to their success, it is deeply motivating, rewarding, and forever memorable to those individuals. This can have a powerful and lasting impact on the organization.

Individual success is a myth. No one succeeds all by themselves.

Moments of Reflection: Was It Luck?

It’s a fair question. Granted, the best teams or most successful organizations who work hard find themselves in lucky positions more often than their less successful counterparts. They are usually the ones who stumble upon an innovation because they are awake and active. In other words, luck is out there, but finding it requires kicking over a few stones and trying a few things. However, it's important to ask:

- Is the win due to luck? Is it replicable?
- If the win came down to luck, what can be done to make sure that lucky break happens again?
- How might we move beyond luck and make our success a strategic imperative?

Wow! That Worked Better Than We Thought!

Sometimes success can bring about unexpectedly great results. A logistics company once produced a television ad illustrating a startup company launching their website. Leaders and team members watched their first order come in — followed by 10 more, followed by 100 more, and then followed by 10,000 orders. The mood in the room went from excited to suddenly overwhelmed. Their innovation had worked much better than they thought, and now they had to figure out how to fulfill all those orders with thousands more on the way!

Indeed, sometimes our plans for success work even better than we expect. It would behoove the leaders of the company that launched the successful website to study their win to identify what worked. Identifying the tactical elements of any success helps build exponential success that could be game-changing. This is critical because, unlike the actors in the TV commercial, we want to avoid being ill-prepared for potential success. Plan for success carefully to avoid a future scenario where success ends up overwhelming you. Some companies have ended up bankrupt when their leaders became so overwhelmed with initial success that they were unable to scale and fulfill orders.

When the celebrations end, bring the leaders and team members together to carefully study their success with just as much scrutiny as if it had been a loss. This helps determine the essential moving parts and the replicable elements to inform future planning so that victory is much more than a lucky, episodical, or random event.

would be the perfect vehicle for Apple to learn about and perfect touch devices. So, you guessed it. 
Apple went on to market the iPhone first, and after learning all the lessons that came with developing 
the iPhone, Apple was poised to then release the iPad.

and both trigger further investigation that help us revise our assumptions, models, and theories. I'd ask
