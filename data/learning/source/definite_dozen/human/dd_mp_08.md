Coach Summitt discovered the hard way the importance for leaders to know their people. Her program used a yearly Predictive Index — a personality profile — to better understand her players. This not only informed how she interacted with her team, but also how she could best leverage individual team member strengths for a more effective team overall. The best leaders today have a solid understanding of themselves and their teams. There a few ways this can be done:

Personality Assessments and Insights

Leaders today have learned about the advantages of personality assessments and using those assessments to make critical decisions about team interaction. There is a wide variety of assessments available to choose from. Successful leaders use them all the time and use the information gathered to shape their strategies, understandings, and engagement with their team.

Self-Assessments — Leaders Go First

One of the best first steps a leader can do is to take the assessment themselves. Coming away with an analysis such as the Predictive Index used by Coach Summitt creates an increased sense of knowledge and understanding of your own communication and personality styles. Forward-thinking leaders are usually willing to take these assessments and share them with their team so that they can have a better idea about how to work with the leader or CEO.

Testing Others and Sharing

Of course, taking these same assessments with others in your organization is helpful. Many forward-thinking organizations today maintain databases or files of the personality and assessment profiles of everyone who works for the organization. Sometimes they will even use this information to help coordinate and build different types of teams.

Continued

Avoid “Arrival”

One of the most important elements of developing a comprehensive awareness of personality types is to "avoid arrival." The best leaders understand that the only way to remain competitive is to ensure that no one in the organization ever feels they have learned all they need to know in a particular area. It's quite the opposite, in fact. The best organizations understand that situations and personalities evolve, and they are on a never-ending journey of growth and discovery.

Working Smart Is Just as Important as Working Hard

You won't get anywhere as a leader or a peak performer without working hard. Conversely, you can work really hard at the wrong things and never make progress. This is why, after a leader conceptualizes the merits of hard work, they need to learn to work smart. Let's consider the following example to reflect on the concept of working smarter, not harder.

Maria is the VP of Strategy at her corporation.
The company has been losing ground to its competition.
Maria's expertise is in strategy, certainly. And she knows her company's existing products and services pretty well.
However, she hasn't kept up with the latest emerging technologies — while the competition has. It turns out that their losses to the competition are due to a technological deficit.
Still, Maria is working hard to revamp their strategies and figure out how to make a strong comeback.

Continued

Maria's knowledge of technology and emerging possibilities would need to be substantial enough to help her make the right decisions. If she is hard at work to resolve the technology deficit without any knowledge of the technology options available, Maria would be working hard, but not necessarily smart. The best leaders understand that they must continuously learn, grow, and make decisions on a consistent basis with new, emerging knowledge flowing through them continuously. So what is ‘working smart?’

To Work Smart, Commit to Learning and Growth

Leaders and top performers understand that leaders must have the ability to make a number of critical decisions on the growth trajectory. In order to make those decisions, the leader has to be knowledgeable and prepared. There are numerous ways to build that knowledge base, but simply the conscious act of remaining committed to expanding your knowledge in the first place allows you to be in a better position than most.

Reading and Professional Development

CEOs and peak performers are voracious readers. They are well aware of the trends in their industry and are constantly mining thought leadership and new technological opportunities for new insights. It would be very rare to find highly successful, competitive leaders and performers who didn’t read the news each day and carefully monitor essential trends that specifically relate to their goal. Highly impactful leaders always prioritize such professional development activities. They take classes, even if they have elevated degrees and experiences. They go to webinars or seminars and are always honing their craft by acting as a student.

Learning from Mentors

Great leaders almost always have a network of mentors at their disposal. In many cases, they are more experienced and give advice that helps in the moment. Sometimes having a variety of mentors is helpful as well. But some mentors don’t have to be older than you. You might have a mentor who simply knows more than you do about a topic and is willing to share. You also are probably somebody’s mentor right now, even if you don’t know it yet. What’s important about having a mentor is being open to behaving like a student. Strong leaders and peak performers desire to be taught and to learn from the masters.

Learning from Peers and Competitors

Your competition can tell you a lot. Studying the trends, tendencies, strengths, and weaknesses of your competitors will also help you to grow as a leader. This might involve studying a team or an entire organization; examining the peak performance skills of individuals you know. For example, if you are a CEO of a company that competes with another company thousands of miles away, you may try to study the moves of that competitor’s CEO. This could be to help anticipate their next step or better understand what they are doing to be successful. Does your competitor’s CEO have some skills that you’re not able to demonstrate? Are they skills that you could develop? Or, are they skills that you could bring in through a valued vice president or specialist? The message here is pretty simple. The strongest leaders and peak performers are not self-absorbed. They look around and try to figure out what they can learn from other people, even from those they compete against. Even those who are at the absolute top of their game must remain doggedly committed to learning from others and elevating what they bring to the table.

Learning from Strong Assistants, VPs, or Team Members

Another great place to learn is from those you work with. In corporate America, it’s common to perform a series of confidential, 360-degree assessments. An outside agent comes into the organization and interviews individuals who work with various leaders or top performers. These interviews are designed to establish perception checks throughout the organization to give leaders or peak performers feedback on how their engagement with others is affecting morale and performance. These exercises are incredibly helpful because they give leaders unfettered feedback and allow them to make adjustments that can make an impact. Leaders can also gain formal and/or informal feedback from other members of the organization. Administrative assistants who feel secure in their positions might provide valuable input. A CEO’s vice president or a coach’s assistant can provide helpful feedback. In other words, the best leaders understand that surrounding themselves with strong people only makes them better, and listening to their advice will only make them wiser. Finding people around you who are good at things that you’re not good at will not only grow your competency in those areas, but may also serve the team. We all have our strengths. Part of being a great leader is letting the people around you show theirs.

The best leaders and top performers are knowledgeable, prepared, and always learning and growing. Some powerful sources of learning include: Reading and Professional Development; Learning from Mentors; Learning from Peers and Competitors; Learning from Strong Assistants, VPs, or Team Members.

Continued

Remain Open to Growth, Forever

Receiving a degree from a university is a wonderful blessing. It indicates the conclusion of a learning journey. However, we live in a world today where the commitment to learning is continuous. With so much changing so fast, it's clear that the commitment to continuous learning is a non-negotiable, not just to be excellent, but to even remain competitive. The most competitive systems and teams understand that they must remain open to the opportunity to learn and grow, and grow and learn continuously both on their own and through the hearts and efforts of others around them if they're going to be at their best, and help the organization to perform at its best.

Muscle Memory

With muscle memory, you can avoid spinning your wheels, thinking about what comes next — and simply go into action. Muscle memory doesn't necessarily guarantee you'll get exactly where you want to go. Shared Values Allow for Personal Investment
Leaders must establish shared values within the organization. While every individual in the organization may have their own learning style and learning preference, it is important that organizations establish the values that they will live by collectively.

What values does your organization collectively share? How do they impact your approach to your work?

If, say, your organization values timeliness, honesty, lifelong learning, and gratitude, those values will have a deep and significant impact on everything you do. Even though each individual may express those values differently, maintaining shared, collective values is extremely important.

When presented and shared, values create guardrails for behaviors and may ultimately shape strategy. For example, if honesty is one of the more important shared values, and a company can advance its interests by lying to their clients, it's a non-starter. Even if a short-term benefit can be observed by violating a set of values, the long-term implications are drastic because without alignment to a set of values, then values have no place in the organization. Everyone's values then become only situationally applied. Worse yet, the lack of shared values almost always results in a gradual lowering of the bar. Think of it this way. You have values in your life that guide you and (hopefully) allow you the pleasure of a good, honest life. If you begin ignoring those values when making decisions, you will undermine your long-term progress and, ultimately, diminish who you are as a human being.

What If the Values in the Organization Don't Align With My Own?
This is the question every leader should ask before they take a new job. Consider two potential sources of value misalignment:

The organization hasn't articulated the values, or they haven't been challenged in quite some time. A new leader may come in and create value alignment, helping things improve.
The organization has values in place, but an individual team member disagrees with them. A leader may establish values and articulate them, but if an individual team member doesn't believe in those values, a misalignment has occurred. Usually, that means the team member will have to either operate within this value system or find a new job.

Obviously, not every organization is the same. But in general, leaders shouldn't sacrifice their values. They should clearly articulate values, and seek to build an organization that meets and exceeds those values.

When we have aspirational value statements guiding us, we become aspirational people capable of doing and becoming more. Leadership Reflections: Defining Success for You

As discussed, defining success can be harder than it looks. As humans, we often think in very linear terms about next steps, such as promotion opportunities. However, getting really clear about what "success" actually means to you is altogether different than goals or even promotions. Put another way, getting a promotion doesn't automatically mean you are "successful." If, however, a certain job or position is your dream job, then it's your definition of success. This distinction makes all the difference. For example, you have likely known someone who became angry, toxic, and despondent because they didn't get a particular job they wanted. Prioritize Problems
In an organization with very little communication, where the leader is largely disconnected from the group, the leader may be unaware of problems in the organization. As a leader, this is not working under some sort of happy, blissful ignorance. It's like floating on a ship without awareness of the iceberg ahead. Keeping lines of communication open and desiring growth will help ensure problems are more readily revealed. Blind spots, skill deficits, and other problems are going to emerge eventually. Teams need to learn to resolve these problems once they've been identified through problem prioritization. By prioritizing the problems in front of us, we have a much better chance of potentially resolving them. The priority helps us determine the order in which we will attend to certain problems. This allows the organization to understand where to put its energy, the problems to address, and in which order. There are several possible approaches to prioritizing problems:

Pick a Strategic Priority
Pick an objective that is entirely strategic. Think of playing a game of Jenga — pull out the wrong block and the entire column will fall. When you are strategic in making changes, you may have limited time and resources, but picking the right change can sometimes have a huge impact in the organization. In some cases, a simple realignment in work process or the elimination of a key obstacle, or in some cases, a toxic individual, can create momentum.

Pick an Easily-Executed Priority
Establish a priority that is perhaps simpler, shorter, and more easily achieved. In other words, a leader may be facing a dilemma and observe a number of problems that need to be solved. When a team is relatively new in working together, or its members are stuck and haven't made progress in a while, sometimes a leader is wise to pick a more easily-resolved priority for the team to tackle first. The advantages here are obvious. Choosing smaller commitments helps the team build confidence and momentum, leading to even more confidence before taking on bigger and more challenging objectives.

Decide If the Problem Is Short-Term or Long-Term
Another question strong leaders ask is whether or not this particular problem is of a long-term nature. For a long-term problem, you can either: Prioritize the long-term problem. If the long-term problem is likely game-changing, consider prioritizing it; or break the long-term problem into smaller, shorter-term problems that should be addressed first. For a short-term problem, consider the potential return on solving the problem. You can: plan how to resolve it and execute the plan, especially if it is a stepping stone toward a larger problem; quickly fix a small problem that could build momentum; limit time and energy on a short-term problem with little return; or allow it to resolve itself due to market conditions or other situations.

Example: An Auto Manufacturer
Suppose the auto maker has been in business for more than 100 years. They've been dealing with a particular problem for several decades. Leaders expect it will take at least five years to address the problem, but the outcome would be game-changing for the company. Strong leaders in this company would have to decide if this long-term problem should be a priority. Since it will take at least five years, these leaders may need to break the problem down into shorter-term challenges that will lead to progress toward solving that longer-term problem. This is the stuff of leadership — reflecting on these different possibilities to better understand how to strategically solve problems and help their organization win.

Leaders Know When to Compromise
One of the mistakes that leaders make is assuming that strength is shown by will and absolute rigidity. In fact, one of the greatest signs of strength and confidence is a leader's ability to learn, grow, and compromise. Leaders should remain steadfast when it comes to values, ethics, and commitments. However, they should remain flexible when it comes to business strategies and approaches. The ability to compromise as a leader demonstrates your ability to grow. It's also a wonderful way to demonstrate the expectations of compromise throughout the organization. Organizations are worse off when warring factions within won't compromise. The healthiest organizations have significant debates at times about the best pathway forward. Well-intended and completely loyal team members may have diametrically opposed positions, but this does not mean that they can't still be valued teammates, friends, and valued confidants. These diametrically opposed individuals must also learn to compromise.

How many times have peak performers on a basketball team had to sacrifice points per game in order to keep their teammates involved in the game plan? These sacrifices make everyone stronger and more evolved.

You Can’t Fix Everything at Once
Especially with new leaders, there's a tendency sometimes try to fix everything at once. This is a mistake. Recall our automobile example. If the manufacturer has faced many decades of challenges in a certain area of product development, that problem likely won't be resolved overnight. This doesn't mean that heroic steps can't be taken — but trying to fix everything at once can sometimes cause a great deal of confusion and leave the organization feeling overwhelmed.

Trust Is the Key
When leaders build trust, they once again create increased levels of momentum. As discussed earlier, momentum can be created when expectations are clear. When trust is developed, there is less hesitation and fear. When team members are operating from a position of fear, they are less courageous and innovative.

Strategy and Clear Communication
Working frantically doesn't take you anywhere. Working hard on the right things makes all the difference. This is why it is so essential for leaders to develop clear and actionable strategies. These strategies must be easily communicated throughout the organization. This clarity has two important benefits:

With Clarity Comes Momentum
As discussed earlier, one of the most significant hindrances to building momentum is the lack of clarity. If your team members are uncertain about the strategy, expectation, goals, and aspirations, they are left with the difficult dilemma of trying to look like they're working hard towards a destination that they're just not sure about. Unfortunately, this dynamic is more common than any of us would like to admit. Confusion over the goal creates disconnects, and, under the worst of circumstances, can create situations where the strategy is hijacked and individuals begin to insert their own agendas.

Team Clarity Creates Team Culture
Another advantage of momentum-building clarity is that it can serve as a force to help build and develop culture within an organization. Simply put, organizational culture is built through a series of collective experiences that result in an understanding of expected behaviors and values. When a team has clarity about the strategy ahead and the strategy is built on organizational values, the execution not only moves the organization closer to their goals, but it also serves to build team culture. Teams get stronger and the culture is reinforced. This level of alignment may sound difficult to achieve, but it shows up when leaders consistently adopt strategies with clarity.

Happy Cultures, Predictability, and Progress

What comes to mind when you think of the phrase happy culture? Does your organization or team have a happy culture? How so?

A happy culture is not solely dedicated to having fun. A happy culture is a content culture, and that contentment can come from the predictability of working under conditions that everyone knows. For example, Coach Summitt expected nothing less than rigorous engagement from everyone who ever played basketball at the University of Tennessee. Because everyone knew and shared in those ideas and expectations, the culture was established and excellence emerged as a result.

Players celebrate Coach Summitt's 500th career victory.

Invest in Deep, Collaborative Planning
Another element of working hard and smart is the investment in deep and collaborative planning. This effort involves the establishment of goals and collaborative development of the step-by-step process required to make those goals come to fruition. It puts participants in the position of owning the journey and owning the plan. By being part of the planning process, the commitment to the goal increases dramatically.

Find Smart People to Surround You
Ineffective leaders sometimes have reputations for surrounding themselves with people who are less talented than them. Perhaps a leader lacks confidence or mistakenly assumes they need to know more than everyone else around them. In fact, nothing could be further from the truth. Ineffective leaders will likely stay that way if they follow this model because intentionally surrounding themselves with less-talented people will deny them opportunities for valuable insight. The very best leaders surround themselves with incredibly smart people. Ideally, they like to find people who are actually better than they are in areas where they are weak.

Coach Summitt took pride in surrounding herself with an extremely talented staff.

For example, if you are a leader in corporate America and have a blind spot when it comes to logistics, you might bring in a vice president with a truly world-class understanding of logistics. This would not be announcing your weakness, but rather announcing your ability to identify a need and recruit people who can meet that need. In an era where information is ever-expanding, it is frankly absurd to assume a leader can always know it all. The more information expands, the more we need expansive people willing to lead.

Influence and Example

An organization brings in a new leader who presents a highly polished and professional demeanor.
This new leader has a really positive attitude with everyone they meet. They remember details about people's personal lives. They're friendly and engaging, never missing an opportunity to make people feel good.
This individual is clearly a strong leader for the organization, not only showing both talent and hard work, but also a commitment to the entire team.

Chances are, that leader's positivity and engagement would literally influence the rest of the organization and serve as an example for others to follow.

A new leader comes into an organization. This person seems rather angry and is secretive about their work.
Employees begin to notice this leader has a political agenda to drive certain people out of the organization.
This leader has great talent for the work, but clearly brings some baggage and negativity with them.

That negativity is just as contagious as the positivity in the earlier example, isn't it? Perhaps it is even more contagious. Others will likely start operating this way and bring more of the same to the organization. Positively or negatively, no matter what a leader does, their leadership style, preferences, and habits will create influence and serve as examples. As such, leaders must be very thoughtful and self-aware of their actions and the messages they send to those around them.

Leadership Reflections: Open Your Eyes

In this module, we discussed that we all have weaknesses and blind spots and the importance of being able to identify them. Equally important, in turn, is identifying strategies to help prevent your weaknesses and blind spots from impeding your success. If... | Then you might consider...
you struggle with communicating in writing | consistently using an editor and planning accordingly
you consistently get anxious when speaking to large groups | allowing extra time to prepare
part of your job is evaluating marketing opportunities, and you don't have much experience or expertise in that area | identifying learning and professional development opportunities in the area of marketing and allowing extra time to develop

For this reflection exercise, spend some time honestly reflecting on your weaknesses and blind spots.

For each weakness or blind spot, identify some simple habits or strategies you could employ that would allow you to live with this weakness and keep it from hindering your ability to perform. How might you adjust your strategies so that your blind spots don't keep you from being successful?
