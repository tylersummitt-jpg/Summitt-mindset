Outstanding Work Ethic

It’s difficult to argue the importance of an outstanding work ethic — a consistent commitment around working with energy and focus towards things that matter. People with an outstanding work ethic usually have outrageous levels of success in life, both personally and professionally. The reason is fairly simple — with hard work comes the ability to learn new things along the way, try things and fail, and then rebuild strategies. Individuals and teams with less talent, but with a strong work ethic, will often prevail over more talented teams that lack an outstanding work ethic.

As a leader, you can't necessarily control your and your team's innate gifts or talents. However, what is within your control is the development and cultivation of an outstanding work ethic. With a great work ethic, almost anyone can at least reach some level of competence and success. When talented people come together and demonstrate stellar work ethic, the sky is truly the limit. People with Talent

Sometimes you'll find people with outstanding talent who don't seem to work as hard as the rest of the group. If you've ever found yourself on a team observing a person with great talent, but a poor work ethic, it may have been tempting to feel a little jealous of their raw talent. However, in business and in life, work ethic makes a huge difference in your overall success. Simply put, you can't be successful in life without consistently developing your work ethic. Period. So don't be jealous of people with outstanding talent. Just commit to working as hard as you can, and if you're on a team with these talented people, encourage them to do the same!

Work Ethic Is a Daily Investment That Pays

Compounding interest is arguably a pretty good analogy for a work ethic. If you find a simple investment calculator and evaluate the power of compound interest each month over a period of years or decades, the accumulation of wealth is surprising — $100 a month for 30 years becomes $100,000. An investment of $1,000 a month for 30 years becomes a million. What starts off as a modest sum, with a modest effort, becomes a formidable financial force! It's the same with work ethic! You might barely notice the commitment you make in putting in that extra effort each day, similar to setting aside a few dollars each month. Over time, with that extra effort or funding, you'll end up with a fortune.

Work Hard Every Day

To that end, leaders should demonstrate a commitment to themselves and with others to work hard every day. By making that investment, teams or groups can overcome even more talented adversaries or competitors.

Everyone Needs to Work Hard

If everyone on a team isn’t working hard, a lack of synergy emerges. For example, if a leader permits a more talented team member to stop working hard, the other members of the team will become demotivated. On the other hand, there is nothing more motivating to a team than to look around and realize that everyone, including the leader, is working absolutely as hard as they can. Therefore, it's important for a leader to build the expectation within a group that hard work is a consistent expectation within the organization. Once that's established, it's very difficult to do anything but continuously progress.

The Importance of Discipline

At the most basic level, discipline is a term that relates to the ability to strictly adhere to a set of standards or obligations. If you have a health challenge and your doctor indicates you must avoid certain foods, the gift of discipline is what gives you the psychological certainty to purposefully and predictably avoid those foods that would make you unhealthy. So, in this context, to be a good leader, you need to be used to practicing and applying the benefits of discipline. Without question, in order to understand and embrace the benefits of discipline, you also have to have the maturity to realize that discipline isn’t about taking things away. It’s about growing and improving by eliminating, saying "no," or simply making different choices. If you are under doctor’s orders to eat different things, you’re not saying "no" to food. You are, however, making different choices at breakfast, lunch, and dinner — and you have the mental ability, or the discipline, to navigate that choice.

Her external reinforcement, carefully established and clearly articulated, helped her players live and work with the level of discipline required to be champions.

Self-Discipline: Who Needs Drive?

Ultimately, self-discipline is the primary destination. Organizations are far more effective when they are teaming with individuals who don’t need to be driven, but instead drive themselves. This is a powerful differentiator. Like our earlier example, without question, well-behaved children aren't born that way. Similarly, in organizational settings, even the most creative and innovative companies have expectations of behavior and effort. Yet even with those expectations, the most powerful companies employ people that don’t just comply with the requested behaviors out of a sense of duty. They behave that way because those expectations are in alignment with who they are as people. This is why it is so important to go to work for organizations that have a climate, culture, and expectations aligned with your own. Furthermore, it’s essential to be in charge of your own internal discipline and drive. So who needs drive? We all do, and leadership should be looking for individuals in their organization who are willing to hold the standard of discipline without having to be monitored every minute by the leader.

We've established that leaders need to lead with expectations of a highly disciplined environment, starting with themselves. Pat Summitt believed in setting some specific parameters around discipline practices for leaders — that they are firm, fair, and consistent.

Firm

To be firm means to be certain. If, for example, as a leader you expect everyone to arrive on time and to deliver their work to a certain standard, to be firm means to meet the expectation, take it seriously, and maintain it. If an unmet expectation is not addressed by the leader, it sends the message that the expectation really wasn't that firm. It moves from being an "expectation" to a casual recommendation or desire.

Fair

To be fair means that the expectation is reasonable. To have a fair expectation of someone means that even if the expectation is rigorous it’s not impossible or unattainable for them. It might require growth or stretching on the team member's part, but it is achievable.

Consistent

The expectation of consistency goes back to keeping your word. Favoring an individual or group over another and allowing them to slack off violates the consistency clause. The best coaches and leaders understand that a consistent standard for all creates a culture or climate where everyone sees discipline as something that is equally shared and expected.

Discipline Is All About Setting Standards, for Performance and Practice

These standards of discipline become the guideposts for performance. When mistakes are made or opportunities to grow present themselves, standards of discipline create the conditions to work on growth in these areas. The highest performing teams set high standards for their practice and are very disciplined about the steps they take to get themselves to the next level. Leaders in these organizations set high standards, speak to these high standards, and lead to these high standards. They have high standards for the outcomes they hope to achieve, and for the standards of practice that must be executed on a consistent level to achieve these outcomes. However, these standards have little value unless they meet four criteria:

Standards must be collectively established

Once again, depending on the level of sophistication within the team or group, one of the best things a leader can do is to work collectively with the team to establish the standards for performance on the team. When team members or group members are involved in the process, there is an even greater likelihood that they will be energized and engaged in making sure that these standards are met.

Standards must be understood

One of the most basic mistakes some leaders make in establishing discipline standards revolves around the failure to make clear what the standards actually mean. This can cause frustration both for the leader and within the team. It's hard to get a team or individual to comply with a set of expectations that they truly don't understand. So, a highly functional leader spends the time to make sure that the standards for performance are thoroughly explained. Ideally, team members or members of the group are given a chance to ask questions and clarify any misunderstandings they may have. Once again, with greater levels of understanding comes greater fluidity and execution.

Standards must be automatic

In families who have a very consistent standard, there are certain habits and rituals that don't ever need to be discussed. There is a standard set of expectations and habits that are undeniable and completely automated.

EXAMPLE: If a family has already established they consistently go to religious services each weekend, they won't find themselves wondering what they'll be doing at 8am on Sunday. It has become automatic.

With high functioning teams or groups, this level of automation also revolves around punishments or consequences for the lack of compliance to the standard.

EXAMPLE: Football coaches may develop consequences in practice for mistakes made during the game, especially mistakes within the players' control.

The fact that they are automated is what's extraordinarily important in this section. Automated consequences take the debate off the table.

EXAMPLE: If the family with four children preparing for church on Sunday had to individually negotiate with each child, each Sunday, about each church service, it would take a while to engage in any faith-based activities. However, if there is a common set of expectations and an understanding about what happens when results aren't achieved, the automation becomes the formative element to once again build momentum.

Therefore, the message here is that, as a leader, we must build standards and create automated responses that are non-negotiable, without regard to the circumstances or the people involved. This keeps everyone honest and moving forward and allows them to more consistently know what to expect on their team and for themselves.

Standards must be non-negotiable

If a family of four has established a standard of attending weekly religious services, time spent negotiating with each family member on a Sunday morning would result in a rather chaotic exchange. When standards become non-negotiable, they become bigger than the individual and even bigger than the leader. It becomes an overall dynamic that exists in the organization. It becomes all about the individual’s awareness of what they need to do to get to where they need to go collectively to enjoy everyone's desired outcomes.

EXAMPLE: If a family or team practices certain behaviors (hand washing, faith, exercise, or other health practices), these behaviors become a standard they collectively work toward. They aren't the nagging expectations of Mom, Dad, or a leader. Instead, each individual is aware of where the family or team is going collectively and what they must do, as individuals, to help them get there. This is truly an example of what happens when leaders elevate the conversation. In the case of the family of four, the weekly standard has already been established, so there's no need for wasting time negotiating with each family member on Sunday mornings.

The Discipline Formula

Creating a disciplined environment isn’t easy. One of the keys to making discipline happen regularly and with quality is a very simple formula:

1. Setting a goal
2. Creating a reward
3. Leveraging that reward against the potential penalty, and
4. Remaining consistent to that choice on a daily basis.

Let’s break each of these down and reflect on how they can have an impact on individual and team or group success.

Step 1
Setting a goal

Goals are very important. The best leaders understand that chasing a goal gives direction to both individuals and a group. The goal of getting in shape for a vacation puts a long-range aspiration in mind and impacts how an individual might respond in making daily food choices. Therefore, "long-term" goals are really good at helping to establish daily or "short-term" goals.

The great thing about a daily goal is that it can sit right in front of you and require immediate action. Daily goals are simple that way. Long-term goals are harder because they're bigger and can be overwhelming. This is why having a daily goal is so important. A daily goal allows you to look at that one solitary day and make decisions on what’s important for that day in alignment to these long-term aspirations.

EXAMPLE: If an individual wants to hike the Grand Canyon from rim to rim and that person is 50 pounds overweight, they won't be ready to do that in a day. It will take days, weeks, even months to achieve that long-term goal. The prospect of being lean and fit enough to enjoy this experience may seem nearly unimaginable given where this person is in their journey.

This aspirational hiker can only control the next 24 hours, a short-term goal. Making good decisions over a 24-hour period brings them one day closer to their bigger goal.

Step 2
Setting a reward

Rewards matter! The very best teams, organizations, and Fortune 100 companies rigorously celebrate the accomplishment of big goals. When annual, quarterly, or even monthly benchmarks are met, the best companies don’t miss out on the celebration! Celebrations provide a psychological reinforcement for the behaviors that made that achievement possible. This is important, because sometimes highly disciplined, high-achieving people will set high standards for themselves...and fail to celebrate benchmark progress. This is a mistake. Even though these highly motivated achievers might make their mark, there is tremendous advantage psychologically in celebrating milestones.

Smartwatches and fitness trackers are incredibly popular today. They offer advanced feedback opportunities on your sleep quality, how many steps you've taken each day, and how long it's been since you moved around. They reward the wearer for achieving daily or weekly fitness goals. These simple digital celebrations are popular because they work! We respond well to even simple rewards, and they make us more likely to repeat the behavior.

So, whether you are used to having rewards in your life or not, you need to include them if you’re going to be an effective leader. Reward yourself and others when goals are achieved. The quality of the reward is less important than the recognition itself in making the greatest difference. How many of us have saved little notes along a journey or other seemingly insignificant recognitions of progress toward a goal, only because these small recognitions meant a lot to us?

By giving ourselves a little reward, our hearts and minds feel good and that will give us the discipline to continue doing what was expected to get the reward. Small rewards, day by day, lead to accomplishment of the overall goal.

EXAMPLE: If the aspirational hiker wants to tackle their short- and long-term goals, perhaps they'll invest in and take advantage of a smartwatch for daily fitness tracking. Those simple daily rewards from the fitness tracker help motivate this individual to do it again tomorrow!

Perhaps they'll go on some short, basic local hikes at first, gradually increasing the distance and challenge. Building strength and endurance through these increasingly challenging workouts help further feed the individual's drive and reward their progress. All the while, they're getting better equipped to tackle that upcoming challenge of hiking the Grand Canyon rim to rim.

Just think, though — if this person failed to celebrate the wins along the way, would they ever feel ready to go for their long-term goal? Rewards matter!

Step 3
Leveraging the reward against the potential penalty

Okay, so you didn’t meet expectations today! Ugh! It doesn't feel good to fail to meet expectations. This is why a simple structure established on a daily basis can reinforce the power of self-discipline. Decide up front what penalty may apply to missing the mark on your short-term goals along the way to your longer-term goals. The penalty should consist of some form of discomfort that serves to remind you in small and simple ways of the expectations you've held for yourself and that failing to meet your daily disciplined objective is not an option. The penalty reinforces how important it is to be vigilant each day if you're going to achieve your bigger goals!

EXAMPLE: Remember our aspirational hiker? If they start slacking on their daily or weekly workouts and fail to make short-term progress, what will get them back on track? This individual might decide they have to execute an even more rigorous diet and exercise schedule the day after a miss to make up for the mistake. Or they might decide to temporarily lose a privilege for a day or two to help regain their focus. Or perhaps they revisit some self-portraits from when they began their weight loss journey to remind them where they came from and where they could return if they don't get back on track.

Step 4
Remaining consistent to that choice on a daily basis

You've set the goal, determined how you'll reward progress, and decided how you'll prevent the penalty for not successfully moving toward the goal. Now, it comes down to consistently making those choices every day and taking those steps that bring you closer to your goal.

EXAMPLE: Our Grand Canyon hiker has to get out there daily and do the work. It's as simple as that, right? We know it may not necessarily be easy. We also know that working toward our short-term goals is the only way we'll move toward the longer-term goals. So, now this individual has to continue getting into better shape to be ready to tackle that amazing and challenging canyon!

Summary

Creating a disciplined environment isn’t easy. One of the keys to making discipline happen regularly and with quality is a very simple formula:

1. Setting a goal
2. Creating a reward
3. Leveraging that reward against the potential penalty, and
4. Remaining consistent to that choice on a daily basis.

Ever-Present, but Unnecessary

Pat Summitt’s goal for her players was to be "ever-present," meaning her players knew that she was always there to maintain the standard—daily, weekly, and so on. However, she also believed that she was doing her job as a leader if eventually her presence became unnecessary at times. In other words, her players would respond when she wasn't looking by maintaining the standard as if she was. This is the true mark of excellent, successful leadership.

You Get to Keep What You Do for Yourself

Many of Pat Summitt’s players went on to become highly successful coaches. Many went on to become successful at business and other endeavors. Great leaders create conditions through discipline where high levels of individual performance occur daily. Self-discipline becomes a permanent internal feature for those who embrace it. So, as a leader, you have an opportunity to touch the future by helping other people find an even better version of themselves through discipline.

Be firm. You need to be willing to set a precedent. It’s not good just announcing a set of rules. Trust me, whether you are dealing with colleagues, or your own child, or yourself, the best way to discourage anarchy is the uncompromising enforcing the regulations... The first timeout will prevent a repeat offense. — Pat Summitt
