Coach Pat Summitt 
You know, I always tell the story about when I came home and he (my son Tyler) was 9 years 
old and I go in and he’s upset and I walk in the room and I said, ‘Tyler, what’s wrong with you?’

Well, my first thought is—What coach in East Tennessee would cut my son? I mean, think about

And then I guess the coach came out in me and I looked at Tyler and I said (voice trails off) …  
He had a basketball under each arm and he was crying so hard and I said, ‘Tyler, if you wear out 
both of those basketballs I promise you, I guarantee, you will make the team next year. Do you 
hear me?’  
And he goes, ‘Yes, ma’am.’  
And I said, ‘Well, then let’s get it done.’  
And he goes, ‘Mom, will you help me?’  
I said, ‘Son, I will help you but I will not start your engine. You must start your engine every day. 
Do you understand that?’  
And he goes, ‘Yes, ma’am.’  
(It was) a defining moment in his life. It changed him. It changed his focus, it changed his work 
ethic, his drive, and I haven’t had to motivate him since then. Not at all. It’s a pretty incredible 
story.  
But, you know, as I look back and I tell people about it they’re like, ‘Well, it’s not that easy, is 
it?’ And I said, ‘It is, if you’re driven to be successful.’

Successful Leaders Commit to Learning and Growth

In this video, we addressed the startling statistic that over half of the executives assigned to a new position are unsuccessful in that position and either move on or are removed from their post in just a couple of years. This would suggest a couple of things. First, leadership positions are very difficult. Second, perhaps not everyone is cut out to be a leader. That would seem pretty obvious. Third, and perhaps most importantly, the job of being a leader requires a lot of what you're engaged in right in this moment - learning, reflection, practice, and growth.

If there's one thing you will see that is consistent through the Definite Dozen made famous by Coach Pat Summitt, it's that commitment to growth is non-negotiable. Even highly successful leaders will eventually fail if they don't commit to growth, so keep this leadership statistic in mind. Failure is remarkably consistent in the positions you strive for. Own the fact that the job is going to be tough, believe in yourself, and commit to growth. You definitely want to be on the right side of that 50% statistic!

Having a Title Isn't Enough

One of the mistakes leaders make is assuming that once the title is ascribed to them, the world around them changes. Granted, if suddenly you earn a huge promotion and much more responsibility is put upon you, the people around you may look at you a bit differently. However, the overall effectiveness of a leader or the power and influence they are able to wield has a lot more to do with their influence and authority than their title. In other words, your ability to adopt the right practices and to treat people in a way that gives you the best opportunity to be successful as a leader will have a greater impact than any change in job title that you are given.

Responsibility and Modeling: Or Born Knowing?

Sometimes when thoughts or skills are so ingrained within us, or are such an integral part of a specific team or group, they can almost be taken for granted. Take riding a bike as an example. You weren't born with the knowledge of riding a bike. However, after some trial and error, you began to get the hang of it and eventually you rode a bike effortlessly. This is the aspiration of skill development on a team or with a group: developing skills that may be foreign in the beginning, but altogether commonplace and easily accessible at the end.

Watching someone else riding a bike shows a future rider how it should be done. It also provides a sense of engagement and motivation.

One of the most powerful ways of developing skills like this is through modeling. We can't assume, for example, that a child who wants to learn to ride a bike would have any idea what to do if they hadn't seen their older brother and sister or parent riding a bike before them.

Watching someone else ride a bike gives the would-be rider a model to follow and maybe even an incentive to get up on that bike seat. Modeling not only shows us the skills and dispositions needed to demonstrate the behavior, but it also provides, in some cases, a sense of engagement and motivation, driving us to work even harder to get to where we want to go.

Is There a Risk of Burnout?

We’ve all known leaders who fail to take responsibility. They are quick to lash out, blame others, and look for excuses. This can create an environment where the leader can quickly feel burned out in their job. This may seem a bit counterintuitive. Wouldn’t it be more exhausting to take full responsibility? Wouldn’t rejecting responsibility be more relaxing? Actually, the answer is no. In leadership positions, arguably the most stressful part of the job revolves around aspects of the work that are beyond your control. If the leader is in control of the surroundings and can make changes, they at least have a fighting chance to be competitive. The problem with individual performers or leaders who fail to take full responsibility is that without knowing it, they are creating a lot of stress for themselves.

Think about your own life. Are you happier when you sit back and blame others for your personal circumstance, or do you feel better and perhaps less stressed when you take responsibility for your life and take action to resolve whatever personal goals or objectives you might have? This same dynamic exists in organizations or on teams.

If you want to see less turnover, support those you work with by taking full responsibility.

Help Wanted

Toni, a very energetic, middle manager in a manufacturing company in the Midwest, found that her division was facing a logistical nightmare as suppliers had failed to fulfill an order, and their warehouse was completely backlogged. She observed her line workers working as fast as they could to meet the demand. With only four members on her shift team, there was real concern that their backlog was going to affect the delivery of the goods to the client. So, in that moment, she rolled up her sleeves and jumped in and helped. Her presence increased productivity, and there was real hope that they could get caught up. 

To their surprise, a company vice president and the CEO also heard about the backlog and showed up and eagerly lent a hand. By jumping in and getting involved, the message was sent that there was no job too mundane for anyone in the organization. Furthermore, everyone understood that the leaders in the organization saw themselves as responsible — for everything. This creates a deep sense of ownership about the work and makes it more likely for everyone to embrace responsibility in the future.

To that end, organizations who don’t show support for their employees and are led by leaders who don’t take responsibility are more likely to see a great amount of employee turnover. If you want to minimize the amount of "Help Wanted" signs you hang as a leader, support those you work with by taking full responsibility and showing them that this is indeed the type of culture that you’re trying to create.

We Adopt What We See

Model the behaviors that you want others to exhibit.

Once leaders begin to display certain behaviors, it's highly likely that others will follow suit. We tend to follow the leader and observe the modeling that's going on. Therefore, if you're a leader in an organization and you don't like how things are going, one of the very first, and perhaps the most powerful steps you can take, is to model the kinds of behaviors that you want to see others exhibit. It's a formula that works with coaching, parenting, and indeed in the leadership realm.

This also is an important dynamic to consider when you think about the fact that unchecked behaviors are also modeled. If you are a leader and you have a very important person underneath you (perhaps another leader who reports to you), operating in a way that is unbecoming of the organization, their impact will have a cascading influence on others and potentially have a much more negative result than you could have ever imagined.

Careful Modeling Requires Clarity 

Define behaviors to set clear expectations for the performance.

If the goal is to provide modeling that shapes behavior, then that behavior has to be clearly defined. In the case of coaching basketball, if you want a strong team captain, what specific behaviors do you expect from any team captain? If you can't define those behaviors, then it's hard to make expectations for anybody that does the job! So, one of the more important steps that a leader can take to reach expectation with this request is to get really clear about the kinds of performance they expect, so that the expectation of modeling can emerge.

Comfort and Aspiration Don’t Go Together

Ambition is uncomfortable.

If you aspire to something that isn't easy to obtain, it's going to require something special. It might require that you develop a special set of skills that others don't have or are unwilling to commit themselves to develop.

Are you willing to aspire to something difficult and deal with the discomfort that comes with both the pursuit and realization of peak performance?

Socrates came to his conclusion because he learned so much and realized there was so much more to consume. Replication: Let’s Not Make Success an Accident

In business today, one of the most important questions asked is whether the task at hand, or the strategy and skill being implemented, is replicable. Put another way, will the success being experienced in this moment be an episodic accident? Will we experience the success and then be unsure as to how to make that success happen again? Or, can we be strategic and find ways of purposefully replicating the steps necessary to make sure that we are in fact engaged and performing at the highest levels?

This is why leaders today must have an eye on modeling and replication. We have to model the behaviors or skills we know lead to success, and we need to make sure that whatever systems we put in place give us a fighting chance to make replication a predictable, systematic set of behaviors.

The most successful leaders or peak performers understand that the more they can replicate in terms of the specific practices that lead to success, the greater chance there is that deeper levels of innovation and profound and lasting levels of success can and will emerge.

Practice the Art of Taking Full Responsibility

Taking responsibility isn't necessarily a natural thing. In many cases, before becoming a leader, your responsibilities may revolve around making sure that your own performance is up to speed without necessarily owning the results of others. However, once you become a leader, the shift happens. You suddenly have your own set of responsibilities to be executed consistently, as well as purview over the work and ultimately the outcomes of others.

This obviously is challenging because leaders aren't afforded the opportunity in most cases to stand idly by with your arms crossed, watching the work of others, and owning every element of process and product. Instead, in the midst of executing your own numerous tasks and responsibilities, you have to make sure that the performance, and performance products of others, meets the standards and expectations of the organization. You are ultimately responsible for their work.

This can be frustrating for a new leader, and there may even be a quiet voice within that wants to resist it. There is obviously always a chance of a colossal failure. Let's say that this new leader is chugging along and suddenly, one of the members of the team has a rather significant failure, the leader's supervisor observes the failure, and confronts the leader. While the leader may provide the details about what happened, identifying the individual at the center of the conflict, it's essential that the leader own both the process and the product in relationship to the shortcoming happening at the moment.

It would be a mistake of the leader to speak to his or her superior and absolve themselves of the responsibility, simply blaming the person in their unit. This does not breed confidence in anyone and sends the message that responsibility is something that can shift depending on the circumstance.

Good leaders own the responsibility and share the credit.

Wear the Responsibility, Share and Give Away the Credit

With this idea of responsibility comes a very interesting juxtaposition. As a leader you must wear the responsibility, owning the problems or missteps of your team. You also must be willing to share and preferably give away the credit when the team or group or unit does exceptionally well. 

This means, if a significant mistake is made on a team, the team leader ultimately needs to own the mistake and address those issues with the individual(s) at hand.

Second, this also means that if there is a significant victory, it's very important that the leader give credit where credit is due and ensure that warm assurance of praise and compliments shines down on those who are on the frontline executing a majority of the work. Again, to a novice leader, this may seem unfair. There may be a quiet voice inside of you who wishes you could get the credit and avoid the blame. In fact, however, it just doesn't work that way.

Leaders who own their mistakes and give credit for the victories tend to be on the winning side time and time again, and respect and adulation will indeed follow.

Responsibility and Holding People Accountable

One of the most powerful things a leader can do to improve the performance of an organization or a team is to give away responsibility. If you give away both power and responsibility, you will develop significant skills in others.

Most of the work we do today requires a greater degree of technical acumen, and the old system of waiting for the boss to identify problems and recommend innovations no longer works. Most jobs today are such that the boss might have a general idea about what needs to be done, but in many cases the person in the layman’s position has to establish priorities, create schedules, and identify how they can be most efficient and effective.

It’s pretty clear: leaders need to give away power and let their people innovate, experiment, and grow. Furthermore, leaders need to measure results and hold accountable those who are asking for change and improvement.

When we visualize what it means to be a leader, we rarely see ourselves successfully charging towards our destination with our teams, mired by exhaustion. Leadership can also be very isolating. You may be the only leader at your level in your organization. This is why it's so important for leaders to have strong social networks outside of work to allow them to connect, debrief, and maintain personal connections. Professional organizations also serve in this role and can't be forgotten.

Let’s Not Work Overtime at Finding Fault!

Evidence of the inability to take responsibility will show up on teams who become experts at finding fault. Put another way, organizations who are weak in creating a culture of responsibility will consistently find a scapegoat for whoever is going to take the blame for the latest rounds of failure. This becomes an extremely toxic environment, because with every new initiative, employees look around and prepare themselves for a default person to blame if things go south. This is the opposite of what great leaders promote. If you have a culture of responsibility at your work, everyone "owns" every issue. They are willing to jump in and help to make sure no one fails. Furthermore, when there is an overall failure, they all work together to try to fix the issue.

Culture of Full Responsibility

Work cultures that are willing to embrace responsibility feel very different than those that are finding fault. There's a degree of safety in groups who find solutions and work together to solve problems rather than finding fault. Furthermore, in those situations, there's probably a greater likelihood that fewer mistakes will be made. If you are trying to perform in front of a group, don't you usually perform a little bit better if you aren't surrounded by people who are praying that either you fail, or if you fail that they're not going to be in any way involved? That kind of thinking makes you feel very alone. A culture of responsibility creates conditions where everyone feels "in it" together, allowing for momentum to build and teams to work together in new and solidified ways.

principles into practice will create and reinforce a culture of full responsibility. If you study 
success long enough and closely enough, when mediocrity starts to creep in, you can identify 
and address it early. In the assessment phase, if you set specific goals, boundaries, and 
standards of behavior that facilitate the outcomes you want for yourself and others, you and 
your team will begin to develop an appetite for full responsibility.  
Accountability is essential to personal growth, as well as team growth. How can you improve if 
you’re never wrong? If you don’t admit a mistake and take responsibility for it, you’re bound to 
make the same one again. You learn as much from getting it wrong as you do from getting it 
right. But you must be willing to take responsibility for failure and be willing to examine it 
closely no matter how excruciating or embarrassing.  
Ask this question: What does success look and feel like on my team, in my company, in my life? 
What’s the customer experience like? What’s the product cycle like? How do the teams 
behave? What are my performance review scores like?” 
Maybe at this point you are silently saying, “But, Katy, you don’t understand. I don’t have a 
team that I can trust.” I understand. Taking full responsibility means that everyone on the team 
has to be committed to doing the work, upholding the standards, or whatever else it is that 
you’ve identified as the driver of success. 
As a leader, ask yourself this question: Do I have the right team? If not, what criteria would 
make up the right team? What courageous steps do I need to take to get the right team in 
place? When am I going to start taking those steps? 
As a team member, ask yourself if you’re contributing to the mission of your organization. Are 
you part of a high-functioning team that delivers results and contributes to the overall success 
of the organization? If not, what steps could you take to make a greater contribution? 
Whether you’re a leader of a large unit or a team member on a very small team, it is up to each 
of us to take full responsibility.

Leadership Reflections: Foiling the Fault-Finder

Let's discuss fault-finding. Have you ever known an expert fault-finder? An expert fault-finder makes it his or her mission to consistently look out for maladies or mistakes in others. They are quick to point out a weakness or a struggle. When the team fails to perform, they look for somebody to blame rather than looking collectively for a way to improve.

For this reflection, let's imagine that you are a leader who is seeking growth, and your team struggles to meet expectation on an important project. You notice that one of your senior team members once again demonstrates a consistent habit of finding fault in others and reflecting on the failure.

What would you do about this fault-finder who consistently brings these grievances to the team in a very public way?
