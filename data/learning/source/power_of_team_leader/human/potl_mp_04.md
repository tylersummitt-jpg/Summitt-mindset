Lesson 3: Learning To Macromanage 
Petra Dunn 
Okay, so if we're all acting like owners, some things need to change, right? You need to be 
involved in every detail, every decision. Nothing is too small or too large to escape your notice. 
Be careful, my friend, you are on a slippery slope to micromanagement. Just saying the word 
causes most team members to cringe. 
Did you know there's a way to take ownership without micromanaging? It's called 
macromanaging, and the best leaders learn how to do it well. As a leader, Coach Summit 
realized she couldn't be everywhere. It's impossible. And yet the buck stopped with her for the 
well-being and safety of nearly a dozen young, inexperienced athletes, many of whom were 
only vaguely familiar with discipline and some of whom had never been away from home 
before. It was physically impossible to stand over them at all times. This is where instilling a 
sense of responsibility and ownership in your team and knowing when to back off comes in 
handy. 
You may not be leading athletes like Pat. You're leading employees, but the same principle still 
applies. If you rush into every situation to help dot every I, cross every T, and help get the ox 
out of the ditch, will employees learn the buck stops with them or with you? 
So, what are some examples of micromanaging versus macromanaging?  
 
Micromanaging might look something like: 
• Approving every email before it is sent. 
• Frequently redoing employees' work instead of providing feedback. 
• Dictating the exact method for completing a task or giving overly detailed instructions 
for simple tasks. 
• Requiring excessive approval for small expenses. 
• Requiring employees to track their daily activities in minute detail. 
• You know the signs. Likely you've seen this before.  
 
By contrast, macromanaging might look something like:  
• Setting clear overall goals and letting employees determine the best way to achieve 
them. 
• Holding periodic check-ins rather than constant monitoring.

• Encouraging autonomy and decision-making for projects someone is owning 
• Evaluating employees based on results and outcomes, not time spent on an activity or 
doing an activity a certain way. 
Take a minute to stop and think where you fall on the scale of micromanagement versus 
macromanagement. 
So how do you take the mantle of authority as a leader while also empowering your team to be 
mini-owners and experts in their craft? We'll take a closer look in this lesson.

Lesson 2 of 7
Knowing the Signs

Heading

The truth is, some leaders — well-meaning leaders — get this far into understanding responsibility and ownership and then get stuck. Their extreme sense of responsibility spills over into the dreaded world of micromanaging.
Click on each circle in the illustration below for examples of micromanaging.

Being in every meeting

Approving every marketing asset

Being copied on every email

Weighing in on every problem

Making every decision

Sitting in on every new hire interview

Approving every purchase

Maybe this rings a bell with you or someone you know. After all, that’s unconditional ownership, right?
Not necessarily.

If you have a company of 10 people total, maybe some of these responsibilities do fall on you. Chances are, though, you’re not in that category. Or maybe you started in that category and grew out of it long ago, but you’re still operating like you’re a one-person band.

And know what’s worse? It starts to crush those you lead. With your fingers in every pie, the team never learns how to cook, metaphorically speaking. People either gravitate toward two areas: They don’t think for themselves (why would they, if you’re going to wind up making every decision for the group?) or they’re so terrified to make a mistake they can’t do anything without approval. They default to order-takers, and any unique characteristics or skills you hired them for in the first place get buried.

If your time had a dollar amount of what you’re worth to the company (not your literal hourly wage, but the amount of money your decisions bring in per month), would the best use of your time be researching costs for a new type of coffee in the break room or rewriting the second paragraph of the company newsletter? What about sitting in four meetings each week about processes and procedures? Deciding whether a button on a website will be green or blue? What picture should go in the new brochure?Probably none of the above. There are certain things that only you can do, and it’s most important to make time for those.

## potl_mp_04_st_003 — Manage Things, Lead People

Type: reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 7
Manage Things, Lead People

Heading

When leaders realize that wearing themselves out over details like the above is not only making them tired, but squashing their team, it can be a freeing revelation.
So, how do you hold yourself responsible for all the things that can go right or wrong without being involved in the day-to-day operations?
Part of it goes back to the classic definition of leadership. You may have heard it before:
You manage things, and you lead people.

The people as a team do the thing, whether it’s running a company or winning a national championship, and you lead them to do it well, like Pat did. Their achievement is your achievement, the team’s achievement, so you train them to do it well. Then get out of the way.

That’s it. That doesn’t mean you don’t step in in an emergency. That doesn’t mean you’re always in the dark. You don’t do it for them, you don’t do it with them. You hire smart people, people who are a good fit, and make sure they have what they need to do their job well.

## potl_mp_04_st_004 — Being Hands Off

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 7
Being Hands Off

What Does It Take to Be "Hands-Off"?

Understanding the importance of being “hands off” is one of the most fundamental (and often overlooked) tenets of good leadership.

Sometimes as a leader, we think our strength lies in getting the team to produce a certain level of output — after all, it’s what we’re all responsible for. You may even be tempted to jump in and help them. But at the end of the day, you can’t make people do things. You have to lead them. All you can really do is hire well.

We’ll say it again. All you can really do is get the right people in the right seat on the bus and lead them well to do their jobs. You can’t force them to do it with excellence, innovation, positivity, super-service, or creativity. All you can do is fire them if they don’t and replace them with someone else who will. Then the cycle starts again.

Heading

As the leader, you have to lead them in attitude, vision, excellence, motivation — all the principles Pat championed in the Definite Dozen — guiding them to the level of self-discipline, autonomy, or ingenuity you want.
They will know what that level is because it will be modeled by you. Not done by you on behalf of the team or for the team but encouraged by the level of leadership you provide.

Lesson 3: Delegating 
Petra Dunn 
One of the most transformational moments in my early career was when I was given my first 
independent assignment after completing a management training program. I got called up to 
the executive vice president's office and he said, “Petra, we have an assignment we'd like to 
offer you. We have a branch that has been losing money for well over a year. The branch 
manager passed away and we've seen a lot of client attrition, but we think you could develop 
strong relationships and turn it around. I won't sugarcoat this. It's going to be tough. And if 
you're not able to turn it around, no one will look negatively on you. However, if you succeed, it 
will be an opportunity for you to shine.” 
I had it turned around in six months, and it felt great. 
I remember when I brought my partner on about 10 years ago. She had a great resume, good 
character, and a strong work ethic, but no experience in my industry. However, I knew I could 
teach her those skills. In our business, mistakes can be very costly, so it was no surprise that she 
wanted to run every decision by me. Soon, I realized I needed to teach her to take 
accountability for making her own decisions, but I also realized I would have to train her to 
carefully assess risk and have the confidence to own her own decisions. 
I told her that as long as she followed the process I had taught her, I would always have her 
back, even if she made a mistake. She developed into a great advisor and a wonderful business 
partner.
