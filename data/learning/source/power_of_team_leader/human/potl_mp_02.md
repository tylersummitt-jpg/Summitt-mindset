In Reach for the Summitt, Coach Summitt is famous for saying, “If you don’t want responsibility, don’t sit in the big chair.”1 Whether it’s a corner office, an executive board room, or a mere cubicle away from the people you manage, “the big chair” comes in all shapes and sizes nowadays.

“Here’s part two,” Pat admonished. “The most successful you are, the more responsibility you must assume. Responsibility never ends… you don’t finish it and move on to something more interesting. Responsibility is a constant state of being.”

“You want to talk about responsibility, let’s talk about [college] kids,” she quipped. “You really want to talk about responsibility, let’s talk about being in charge of 12 kids at a time, a dozen young women from all over the country, each with their own varying dispositions, backgrounds, problems, quarrels, and love interests. Some of them with cars.”

Pat once famously told a story about her friends deciding to throw her a 16th birthday party at a country club in Ashland City when she was in high school. Thrilled, she told her dad she needed the day off from her chores around the farm, but he told her she couldn’t go because they needed to load hay bales.
“I spent that birthday sitting on the tractor. There were about 1,000 bales of hay that needed loading,” she recalled. “My father’s point was, you can’t pick and choose the days you feel like being responsible. It’s not something that disappears when you’re tired.”

Lesson 1: The Big Chair 
Dr. Roberto Fernandez-Romero 
Ah, responsibility. It's a word you've heard over and over and over again. If you're watching 
this video, chances are you know a thing or two about responsibility at your organization. 
Pat believed responsibility was key to having success as a leader. Not just personal 
success, but going beyond that to instill a sense of responsibility in your team. Easier said 
than done, right? There are certain steps to walking through this art of cultivating 
responsibility, which we'll get to in a moment. 
But first, take a minute to think about where you are at your organization. In what areas 
does responsibility come most naturally? In what areas do you wish you could step up your 
own responsibility or encourage a little more responsibility among your team members? 
Even more important, what could your group accomplish if everyone understood the 
concept of being responsible? Not only for the output in their area, but the shared mission 
of the team. Pat believed a few simple principles were necessary when it comes to being a 
leader. 
You have to have responsibility for everything. If the ship is on fire, it's your responsibility. If 
the boat doesn't have enough gas, it's your responsibility. If the team didn't bring enough 
snacks for the trip, it's your responsibility.  
You have to instill responsibility in your team. As a leader, you can't go around shouldering 
100% of the burden. Not if you want to be able to lead effectively and grow a capable team 
underneath you. They, too, must have levels of responsibility and tasks they are uniquely 
accountable for. You have to look for intentional ways to encourage responsibility on your 
team. 
That brings us to the last point. You have to clearly define who is responsible for what. And 
you have to actively cultivate different levels of responsibility. 
Pat believed in starting small and working team members into greater levels of 
accountability. Everyone on the team had things they were accountable for, with no one 
being a spectator. We'll explore these concepts and more in the following lessons.

## potl_mp_02_st_003 — Sitting in the Chair

Type: video_and_reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 5
Sitting in the Chair

Sitting in the Chair

In a nutshell, Pat believed that to have responsibility and overall success as a leader, you had to consider these main elements.
(Scroll through to complete this element)

Conclusion:

Consider how you can implement these main points with your team in order to cultivate increasing levels of responsibility.

You have to have responsibility. For everything.

Pat’s message was simple: You have to have responsibility. For everything. “To be successful, you must accept full responsibility,” she said. “For everything. Headaches, problems, crises. Even when it doesn’t seem fair.”

You have to instill responsibility in your team.

Pat believed the best way to do this was bit by bit, one task at a time, rather than projecting a lot of responsibility on one person overnight. At the University of Tennessee, she famously increased her players’ responsibilities to each other slowly and incrementally over four years. By the time each player was a senior, they were assigned mentoring roles and had personal charge of an incoming freshman.

You have to clearly define who is responsible for what.

“I must make sure that the responsibilities of our players and staff are clearly delineated and that everyone understands them. That way, when there is a breakdown, we know what happened,” Pat once said. If you don’t know who is responsible for what, when there’s a problem in a particular area, you won’t know who the clear, “wringable neck” is for that area.

You have to actively cultivate different levels of responsibility.

Granting your team responsibility doesn’t have to be like switching a faucet on and off, or delegating entire areas at a time. This sounds like common sense, but it’s amazing how many companies do just that — take a team member with little responsibility and abruptly thrust them into the spotlight where all or most of the responsibility for a particular area now falls on them. Pat would argue that responsibility should be like gradually watering a plant, whereas weeks of drought followed by a deluge of water is overwhelming. Sometimes “too much too fast” even sinks team members with the most potential.

We'll explore more of the view from the big chair, including:
How to instill responsibility in your team
How to own your area without micromanaging
Responsibility for your personal brand, and why it matters

Lesson 1: Knowing Your Priorities 
Joan Cronan 
You know, in life, it's important that you know your priorities and you live your priorities. If Pat 
Summitt was standing here right now, she would tell you her priorities were academics and 
then athletics. She looked at the basketball court as her classroom. 
  
If you ask her what she was proudest of, she would say, I'm proudest of the academic success 
that every young lady who played for me for four years graduated. She wouldn't say, I won 
eight national championships, I went to 18 final fours. Folks, people dream of going to one final 
four. Pat Summitt went to 18 final fours. Pat Summitt believed in academics.  
 
One of my favorite stories on Pat is when she was recruiting a young lady from Kentucky.  
This young lady was a great athlete, but she came from a very poor part of the world and a very 
poor academic background. And she mentioned to me that she was worried about this young 
lady being able to succeed at Tennessee. Well, in our world, you can go in and visit in their 
home, then you can visit, bring them in and visit on campus. 
  
She went to visit her and she said, Joan, she really wants to come. Of course, as an AD, I said, 
how good is she? And she said, she's really good. So we brought her in to visit on campus. I'll 
never forget that Saturday. It was a Saturday that we had 107,000 people running around in 
Orange. It was a football Saturday. But I went to my office to meet with this young lady. And 
standing there was a tall, attractive, blonde young lady dressed in one of the most expensive 
looking suits that I'd ever seen. You can imagine my mind talk as I visited with her. I was 
picturing poverty and here was this model sitting in front of me. So finally, I said, you know, 
that's one of the prettiest suits I've ever seen. 
  
She gave me the biggest smile. And she said, Ms. Cronin, when Pat came in to visit me, my 
mother drew a picture of the suit that Pat had on. And my mother made this suit for me to 
come visit. 
  
Well, she came and she was successful. I was so excited. And her junior year, she was 
struggling. She was struggling academically. It wasn't that she wasn't trying. It wasn't that she 
wasn't capable. It was kind of like that treadmill you get on. You couldn't catch up. Because I 
called a summit conference.

I called Pat in, I called her academic advisor, and I called the young lady. And we talked about 
what it would take to be successful. And as a team, we decided that we would pull her out of 
practice and pull her off the road for three weeks where she would have time to catch up. 
  
But we'd let her start at every home game. It's that lollipop we gave her to talk about success. 
The academic advisor came back. She said, Joan, she's doing great. So I said, okay. And she 
started competing and she started traveling. 
  
We went down to the SEC tournament, and she made All-Tournament. We lost in the finals, but 
she had made all tournament. I was so glad we hadn't denied her an opportunity to be the best 
she could be. 
  
Fast forward to her senior year, we're playing in the championship game. I'm trying to be sure 
everybody's got their tickets, the alumni are having the reception, the president's plane have 
landed, the coaches have what they need, and my phone rings at the hotel. And I answer it, and 
it's this young lady, and she's crying. 
  
And I said, what's wrong? She said, I wanted you to be the first one to know that UT just called, 
and I made the Dean's List. So she made the Dean's List. She went on to help us win a national 
championship, and she became the first person in her family ever to graduate from college. 
  
That's what I call success. So remember, we are teaching young people to be successful, just 
like you are teaching your employees to be successful and grow.
