Heading

It’s a fact of life: Not all people communicate in the same way, and it's important to understand different personality types and adjust your own communication style accordingly. Each personality type has its own style, preferences, and tendencies, and if you fail to adjust your approach, you may end up inadvertently alienating a team member.

For example, let's say you’re very direct and assertive, and you’re communicating with someone who is more reserved. If you come on too strong and don't take the time to listen to their perspective, you may make them feel intimidated. On the other hand, if you’re too passive and don't express your opinions clearly, the other person may perceive you as indecisive.

Communicating differently to different types can help you build stronger relationships and encourage greater understanding. When you tailor your approach to fit another person’s needs, you can help them feel valued and respected.

In another example, let's say you’re working on a team project with someone who is very detail-oriented and analytical. You may find that they prefer to receive information in a highly structured and organized format, even though this isn’t your style. By taking the time to present your ideas in a clear and logical way, you can help them feel more comfortable and engaged in the process.

It’s a question of personalities and finding the most effective language to use with a player. Some people, you can rip their heads off, and they still won’t give you their full attention. Others will break at a single word.”

Extroverts vs. Introverts

One of the simplest differences in personality styles is extroverts vs. introverts. Extroverts are outgoing, talkative, and enjoy being around other people. They often think out loud and process information verbally. In contrast, introverts are more reserved and introspective. They tend to think before they speak and may need time to process information internally. To communicate effectively with extroverts, try to engage them in conversation and give them opportunities to express their ideas. Use open-ended questions and active listening skills to show you’re interested in what they have to say.

On the other hand, to communicate effectively with introverts, try to give them space and time to process information. Give them clear, concise instructions and allow them to work independently. For example, if you are working with an introverted colleague, you might send them an email outlining the project requirements and asking them to review the information before meeting to discuss further. This can give them the time they need to think through their ideas and come to the conversation prepared.

Lesson 4 of 9
Thinkers vs. Feelers

Heading

Another difference is between “thinkers” and “feelers.” Thinkers are logical and analytical. They prefer to focus on facts and data and may have a more detached approach to problem-solving. Feelers, on the other hand, are more emotional and empathetic. They are focused on relationships and may prioritize the feelings and needs of others.

To communicate effectively with thinkers, try to provide them with clear, data-driven information. Use objective language and avoid emotional appeals. If you are presenting an idea to a group of thinkers, you might start by providing them with a data-driven analysis of the situation and the potential benefits of your idea. You can then invite questions and discussion to help them explore the idea more deeply.

On the other hand, to communicate effectively with feelers, try to acknowledge their emotions and show empathy for their perspective. Use language that highlights the potential impact on people and relationships. If you’re presenting an idea to a group of feelers, you might start by acknowledging the emotional impact of the situation and how your idea could help improve the situation. You can then invite questions and discussion to help them explore the idea more deeply from an emotional perspective.

Personality Testing

Heading

To go beyond the general assessments of “introvert-extrovert” or “thinker-feeler,” you can also gain insight into personality types through personality trait assessments. Pat famously used the Predictive Index assessment with her players in the 1990s, relying on the tool to understand each player’s natural motivations. But there are many tools and assessments to choose from nowadays.

Summary

Consider what personality trait assessment tool might work best for your team members, and how you can share those results with the team.

Predictive Index (PI)

The Predictive Index (PI) is based on the theory of behavioral drives, which suggests that individuals have innate needs that drive their behavior in the workplace. It measures four primary personality factors:
Dominance – the drive to exert influence and control over one's environment
Extraversion – the drive to interact with and gain energy from one's environment
Patience – the drive to maintain stability and consistency in one's environment
Formality – the drive to conform to rules and structure in one's environment
Based on an individual's responses to the PI assessment, their scores on these four factors are plotted on a grid that generates a personality profile. The profile helps predict their leadership style, communication style, decision-making approach, and ability to work independently or as part of a team. For example, an individual with a high Dominance score is likely to be assertive and confident, while someone with a high Patience score may prefer a steady and predictable environment.

Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator is a personality trait assessment that measures an individual's preferences on four dimensions: Extraversion vs. Introversion, Sensing vs. Intuition, Thinking vs. Feeling, and Judging vs. Perceiving. Based on these preferences, an individual is assigned one of 16 possible personality types, such as ISTJ (Introverted, Sensing, Thinking, Judging) or ENFP (Extraverted, Intuitive, Feeling, Perceiving). Knowing an individual's MBTI type can help you understand their communication style and how they generally prefer to receive information, in addition to other personality insights.

The Enneagram

The Enneagram is a personality assessment that categorizes individuals into nine different personality types based on their core fears, desires, and motivations. These types include the Reformer, the Helper, the Achiever, the Individualist, the Investigator, the Loyalist, the Enthusiast, the Challenger, and the Peacemaker. Understanding an individual's Enneagram type can help you understand their underlying motivations and needs. For example, a Peacemaker may prioritize harmony and avoiding conflict, while someone with an Enthusiast type might prioritize relationships and fun.

DISC Profile

The DISC profile is another traditionally used personality trait assessment that measures an individual's Dominance, Influence, Steadiness, and Conscientiousness. These factors are plotted on a graph to generate a personality profile that can help predict an individual's communication style and behavior in different situations with a percentage given for each type.

Big Five Personality Traits

The Big Five personality traits are a set of five broad dimensions that are used to describe human personality. These traits include Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism. Understanding an individual's Big Five personality traits can help you understand their communication style, values, and preferences. An individual with a high Openness score may be more creative and open to new ideas, while someone with a high Conscientiousness score might prioritize organization and planning.

StrengthsFinder

StrengthsFinder is a personality trait assessment that measures an individual's top five unique strengths across 34 different themes, such as Strategic, Achiever, Empathy, or Adaptability. Understanding an individual's strengths can help you communicate with them in a way that acknowledges their unique skills and abilities in the workplace.

Hogan Personality Inventory (HPI)

The Hogan Personality Inventory is a personality trait assessment that measures an individual's personality in terms of three main categories: Reputation, Identity, and Motives. Reputation refers to how an individual is perceived by others, Identity refers to how an individual sees themselves, and Motives refers to the underlying drivers of an individual's behavior. Understanding an individual's HPI profile can help you understand their communication style, their strengths, and their potential weaknesses.

Lesson 7: The Importance of Learning Styles 
Danni Varlan 
In 2000, I was the program director for a leadership program and Pat invited us to come over 
and do a learning styles assessment with her players. We were very excited about that, as you 
can imagine, and were very surprised to find out what everybody's learning style was, as well as 
Pat. 
But the fun part about it was as we did it, she learned some skills that were going to give her an 
opportunity for communicating better with several of her players. One in particular was just 
very kind, very nice, loved people, but wanted everybody to be happy. So sometimes that got in 
the way of a perfect shot and she would stop and pass it off to someone. So, and you know, Pat 
just wasn't crazy about that. So this was giving her an opportunity to learn how she could more 
effectively communicate with this player. 
On the other hand, she had another player that was a great point guard and she was skilled and 
was very comfortable making decisions on the fly. In fact, at the end of every game, she would 
write a three-page legal pad and give Pat her assessment of that game. 
Now with her staff, she also found out some very interesting information. You know, 
sometimes there would be a good cop, bad cop scenario with the team and it became very 
clear who was going to play what role when they did learning styles. But it also became clear in 
terms of how comfortable they were with certain subjects with their teams. 
So, learning styles became a part of Pat's everyday involvement with her team and actually in 
her life. She was very interested in how people wanted to communicate and how to best 
communicate with them.

## potl_mp_08_st_007 — Criticism and Praise

Type: reading · Source sequence: 7

Lesson 7 of 9
Criticism and Praise

Heading

Finally, when learning to communicate with different personality types, one principle Pat always believed in was supplementing any necessary criticisms with praise and knowing how to use both wisely.

When is it appropriate to use harsh words? Only you can be the judge of that. But I’ll say this: They must be used in combination with praise. Negative reinforcement must be used sparingly.”

(Please listen to complete this element)

Click to hear the quote above.

Realizing that praise was necessary to keep her team going as well as the routine critiques and criticisms, Pat developed a system called “rebound and two points” to help her players remember the use of both. Whenever an opportunity for improvement or chastisement was issued, the player would say, “Rebound!” to show they understood and knew the criticism was for the best. When praise was given, they were to say, “Two points!” to indicate that they heard the praise and it had been received. The verbal reinforcement was necessary, Pat believed, to ensure the players weren’t hearing and internalizing only the criticism.

In summary, whether using praise or criticism, communicating with extroverts, introverts, or other diverse personalities, using the right communication style can make all the difference. The next time you're in a conversation with someone, take a moment to consider their communication style and adjust your approach accordingly. You might be surprised at how much more effective your communication becomes.
