Lesson 8: Listening to Lead 
Steve Hamer 
As a coach, Pat Summitt was known for her commanding tone and strong leadership style. But 
did you know that she also spent a lot of time learning to listen? Listening well is one of the 
best kept secrets to being a leader. How many of us go through a day without saying a word? 
None of us. But maybe we ought to try it, she once said. Communicating is not just about giving 
great speeches, it's about allowing others to express themselves. If a championship coach like 
Pat Summitt can take the time to slow down and listen to those she was leading, you can too. 
One of the things that I have learned over the years, especially being a married man, is that 
there is a reason why we have two ears and one mouth. That means that we should listen twice 
as much as we should speak. For me, that has really resonated a great deal because as an 
effective leader, I want to make sure that I listen to everyone that I lead. Again, there is a 
difference between hearing and listening. Actively listening means that you are paying 
attention to what is being said, and you ingest what is being said, thus making you a more 
impactful leader. 
So, as I work every single day, I make sure that I put the needs of others before myself, 
especially when it comes to operating in the highest capacity. That means that I'm listening, I'm 
actively listening instead of just thinking of the next thing to say. As a leader, your ability to 
listen actively and sympathetically plays a significant role in encouraging a positive work 
environment, promoting collaboration, and ultimately achieving success. Listening is the 
foundation of building trust and respect on your team. 
When you actively listen to your employees, they feel value and it creates a sense of 
psychological safety. Take the time to engage in open conversations where employees feel 
comfortable expressing their thoughts and concerns. By valuing their feedback, you are 
demonstrating that their contributions matter to the team. Rather than simply having everyone 
feel heard, understanding your team's needs and concerns is essential for having the 
knowledge to effectively address potential issues proactively as a leader. 
And how you listen is just as important as the act of listening itself. No lesson on 
communication would be complete without a closer look at this important topic. So, take a 
breath and let's learn more on how you can become an active listener in the workplace.

Listening as a Leader

Heading

Now, we know what you're thinking. I already have too much on my plate. Who has time to listen? Well, my friend, if you don't make the time to listen to your team, you're going to have a lot more on your plate in the long run. Trust us.

So, let's talk about what it means to listen as a leader. First and foremost, it means being present. That means putting down your phone, closing your laptop, and actually looking at the person speaking to you. But listening isn't just about being physically present — it's also about being mentally present. That means clearing your mind of distractions and focusing on what the person is saying. That can be tough. Sometimes, it feels like our minds are like a web browser with dozens of tabs open at once. But just like how we close some tabs to avoid a crash, we need to focus on one thing at a time to avoid a “mental crash.”

As a leader, your job isn't to have all the answers. It's to help your team find the answers themselves. That means listening to their ideas and helping them think through solutions. And if they come up with a solution that's different from what you would have done, that's OK. In fact, it might even be better. Leaders who are too afraid to listen to their teams risk stifling creativity and innovation.
“Often a strong, dominant leader is the worst listener. He or she is too busy telling everyone else what to do and what to think,” Pat once noted.1

As a leader, it's important to listen to your employees. Not only does it make them feel valued and respected, but it also helps you make more informed decisions.
Active listening is a technique that involves concentrating on what someone is saying and being fully present. Passive listening is when the listener isn’t actively engaging in the conversation. The listener may be physically present, but their mind is elsewhere.

Set Aside Time for 1:1 Conversations

One-on-one conversations are a great way to get to know your employees on a personal level and understand their perspective. As your schedule allows, make it a priority to work them into your regular routine.
Here are some tips to make the most of the time in your 1:1s.

Summary

Consider which of these you can work in to meetings with your team to help model active listening the most.

Avoid distractions.

When you're having a conversation with an employee, avoid distractions like checking your phone or looking at your computer. Give them your undivided attention.

Ask open-ended questions.

Open-ended questions can help you get a better understanding of your employee's perspective. Ask questions like, "How do you feel about this situation?" or "What do you think we can do to improve?"

Don't interrupt.

Interrupting someone when they’re speaking makes them feel like their opinions aren’t valued. Wait until they have finished speaking before responding.

Take notes.

Taking notes during a conversation shows the other person that you’re taking their concerns seriously and you value their input. It also helps you remember important details.

Be empathetic.

Empathy is the ability to understand and share someone else's feelings. When you're listening to an employee, try to put yourself in their shoes and understand how they are feeling.

Repeat/confirm the issue.

To show the speaker you understand the issue being communicated and to avoid any miscommunication, listen to the employee as they share, then repeat back the problem or issue being discussed. (“Just to make sure I understand, you’re saying the problem is with the new supply chain. Is that the issue?”) This shows the employee that you’re listening and gives them a chance to clarify any additional details if needed. (“Yes, it’s mainly the supply chain, but there’s also an issue with the details on the packages out of the Denver office.”)

Follow up.

After a conversation, follow up with your employee to let them know that you have taken their concerns into consideration. This helps build trust and shows that you are committed to addressing their concerns.

Create a Culture of Open Communication

Encourage your employees to speak up and share their ideas by creating a culture of open communication.
Here are some things to keep in mind when encouraging openness.

Summary

In the end, listening as a leader is about creating a culture of openness and respect. It's about being willing to hear perspectives that are different from your own and using those perspectives to make better decisions. So, take the time to listen to your team — you might just be surprised by what you hear!

Seek regular feedback.

Asking your employees for feedback on your leadership style shows that you’re open to growth and improvement. It also helps you better understand their needs.

Don’t punish solicited feedback.

Even if feedback may be negative in nature, avoid punishing honest feedback that you asked for, as long as it’s given respectfully.

Thank them for sharing.

Thanking your employees for sharing their concerns or ideas shows that you value their input and appreciate their willingness to speak up.

Go on a listening tour.

To get a full sense of issues facing those you manage, once or twice a year go on a "listening tour" of your workplace to understand the perspectives and concerns of your team members. Make listening a conscious priority, whether by a series of structured feedback sessions or informal 1:1s. This helps uncover problems or feedback that may not arise solely through casual conversation.
