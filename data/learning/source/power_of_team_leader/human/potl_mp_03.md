Lesson 2: Responsibility = Accountability = Ownership 
Nicky Anosike 
Responsibility, accountability, ownership. These words are interchangeable, right? Not so 
fast. What if we told you that these three words were a progression that can help you and 
your team get to the next level? 
 
Imagine this. Two customers walk into a busy lobby. There are two frazzled employees 
working the front desk, one answering the phones, the other trying to help another 
customer with some paperwork. As soon as one call ends, the employee picks up the 
phone again to make a follow-up call. The employee helping the previous customer never 
looks up from the computer. They call an employee out of the back to answer a question. 
Neither looks up or acknowledges the growing line of two to three people in front of them. 
When the question is answered, the third employee immediately retreats to the back 
office. After all, he doesn't work in the lobby. The employee on the phone turns her back to 
make it clear that she can't take customers in her line right now. The employee handling 
the customer is growing more and more stressed, cutting the customer off as they try to 
ask what he sees as unnecessary questions. Two additional employees walk in the door for 
their shift, oblivious to the growing line, laughing and joking as they head for the break 
room. Meanwhile, the line grows. 
 
Then the owner of the business walks in. Seeing the growing line of customers, he 
immediately springs into action, paging two additional employees. He then asks 
customers with one set of needs to take the right line, while customers with another set of 
needs to take the left line, smiling and making eye contact with each customer. He quickly 
processes some of the small requests first while asking the others to step aside for more 
detailed service. 
 
When he asked the team members about it later, he gets blank stares. 
• “I had to make some phone calls,” the first employee says. 
• “I was going as fast as I could,” the second employee says. 
• The third employee stares at him blankly. “I don't work the front desk.” 
• The fourth and fifth employees? “We were just getting back from lunch. We weren't 
even on the clock yet, they protest.” 
 
What happened here? A breakdown in teamwork. Yes, customer service, maybe. But Pat 
would say the growing pileup in this circumstance is around the concept of responsibility

versus accountability versus ownership. Everyone might have been responsible for their 
task at hand, but not the overall goal of getting customers in and out with great service. 
 
My freshman year, Pat called me into her office and she said, “Nikki, I know you're used to 
winning a lot of awards, but on this level, you probably won't win those same awards.” And 
she told me that she needed me to be the defensive stopper. So that became my 
responsibility. The game would start, and Pat would put me in the game and say, stop the 
other team's best player. And that was my responsibility. After the game, Pat would tell me 
if I did a good job or if I didn't do such a good job. And sometimes she would actually have 
the other player's statistics to show me how many points I allowed. That was to ensure that 
I would not only be accountable, but that I would take ownership for my performance. And 
the next game, I would do my best to improve. 
 
So how do you exhibit or encourage ownership among your own work team? Let's take a 
look.

As a leader, how many times have you heard or experienced any of the following among your team:

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 8
Your Employees

Heading

Put yourself in your employee’s shoes for a moment. Has the team been told super-serving is an expectation, for example, or are they simply going “by-the-book?” Are team members rewarded for going the extra mile, or are the consequences and rewards for average service and above-average service basically the same? Are there ways to step into greater levels of responsibility in each role before actually being promoted, or do you have to wait for the title? Are employees encouraged to speak up in a professional way with suggestions for improvement, or rebuked when they say anything?

There’s a famous saying, “You get what you promote.” The reverse can be true as well: “You get what you allow.” If you treat your team members like order-takers or pencil-pushers, that’s generally what you’ll get. If you teach them that they aren’t supposed to think for themselves … they won’t. If you allow mediocre behavior to be the norm, it will become the norm.

On the flip side, if you teach them that they are responsible for the outcome — that their actions have a direct effect on the outcomes of the business and their daily environment — they’re more likely to feel motivated to work hard.
The elusive quality we’re talking about here is ownership.

## potl_mp_03_st_004 — Learning RAO

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 8
Learning RAO

Learning RAO

People don’t just wake up each day with a sense of ownership. Whether it’s in business or our personal lives, there is a natural progression from responsibility to ownership.
When teaching the Definite Dozen, Pat used the simple formula below:

Responsibility = Accountability = Ownership

When we show up to the present moment with all of our senses, we invite the world to fill us with joy. The pains of the past are behind us. The future has yet to unfold. But the now is full of beauty simply waiting for our attention.

It’s in your jurisdiction, and you have both the blessing and ability to control it.

You realize you are answerable for the outcome of the task. “The buck stops with you,” to use the classic phrase. In other words, responsibility is task-oriented, while accountability is outcome-based.

The dictionary defines ownership as “the act, state, or right of possessing something.” It means claiming a particular process, situation, or outcome as your own. Not only are you responsible for it in general, the way a guardian or steward might be, but you actually own it. You are bought-in and fully invested in the outcome or task at hand because it is yours — whether we’re talking about a sports team, a building, or a business. Managers may have responsibility to see that the deck gets polished, but owners are responsible for how the ship looks.

So why is creating a sense of ownership among your team — as much as possible — important? A team full of mini-owners creates an atmosphere of initiative and enthusiasm you can’t cultivate by simply doling out tasks. It’s not as hard to convince someone to keep the house in good shape when they own it.

Heading

And a sense of ownership implies a direct correlation between a team member’s decisions and the outcome of the business. It may not be a direct line — there may be other steps or factors along the way — but there is a connection, and they can start to visualize how their level of performance impacts customer satisfaction or profits.

## potl_mp_03_st_006 — Creating a Sense of Ownership

Creating a Sense of Ownership

Creating a Sense of Ownership Among Your Team

When it comes to the formula above, Pat knew you couldn’t skip steps.
Commanding people to simply be owners usually doesn’t work. Start where you are — meaning, cultivate responsibility first.
(Scroll through to complete this element)

Summary

In a way, ownership forces team members to think about the big picture — and helps you think about the big picture in a way you may not have been able to before. You have a myriad of owners looking at the goals from a diversity of angles now, in a way that one department manager or leader couldn’t hope to cover alone. You don’t have to preach buy-in; you already have it. All by not being afraid to instill responsibility in your team … and committing to champion them as they step into it.

Does everyone on the team know what they’re responsible for?

Not merely what they’re supposed to be doing or not doing with their time “on the clock,” but do they know what exact areas they’re responsible for?

Is everyone uniquely responsible for something?

This may vary by organization, but take a step back. Do you have six people responsible for steps 1, 2, and 5, for example, but no one responsible for steps 3 and 4? Sometimes articulating responsibility means making some corrections or implementing temporary duties to make sure every person is responsible for something that’s driving toward the bottom line, and no task or area is left without an owner.

Does everyone understand that they are not only responsible, but also accountable for the results of the tasks?

Does the person stocking the grocery bags understand that they aren’t simply putting bags in holsters, but that if the registers run out, they are accountable? Being accountable prompts an entirely new level of thought: How many bags are we going through per day? Do we have enough in the back to last through the week? We went through bags much faster than expected yesterday; should I tell our supply purchaser? Such thoughts are well on their way to ownership.
Does the person responsible for running the social media account understand that they aren’t simply responsible for making posts, but accountable for how those posts are performing — and making the necessary adjustment if the results are subpar? Does the person designing the website know they will be held accountable for the bounce rate — how many customers visit the site and abandon the page without engaging or clicking on anything? Or are they too absorbed with slick graphics and a nice logo? You get the idea.

Corinne Milien 
One time I remember going to the Virgin Islands. Playing in a regular tournament. And just like

Lesson 2: The Power of the T-Shirt Cannon 
Jimmy Delaney 
Over the course of my time with Tennessee Athletics and with women's athletics and with the 
Lady Vols, I was very fortunate to go from being a part of the game atmosphere and doing 
things. Early in my career, I started as the person that dressed up as the mascot and I was the 
person that shot the t-shirt cannon. 
And I remember one game I was in the moment just a little bit too much and I got a little bit too 
close to the team huddle and I shot some t-shirts. And the next day in the office I was called 
into, at that point it was Coach Summit's office. And I sat down and she said, “What happened 
last night?” And I was racking my brain and I couldn't quite remember. 
And she said, “I was coaching in the huddle and I was distracted by the t-shirt cannon. And she 
was like, should I be distracted by that?” And I said, “No, coach, you shouldn't be.” And she 
said, “Look, you go be great at what you're doing and I need to be great at what I'm doing.” And 
she was like, those two things can coexist. 
But I just remember in that moment, I was like, this is it. My career is over with. I've just upset 
Coach Summit. And we were done. She said, "You got it. And I got it.” And she said, “Great.” 
She said, “But how far can you shoot a t-shirt?” 
And so for me, it was very relieving because where I thought I was about to get grilled and 
toasted and that was the end of my career, she communicated with me. She made her point. 
But then after that, she kind of cleared the air and made it very fun. And I did tell her that I can 
shoot a t-shirt all the way up into the upper deck.
