Heading

We live in the era of entrepreneurship, the solo enterprise, the gig economy, the individual contributor, etc. There’s been a shift over the last five to 10 years that places a premium on individual autonomy and flexibility. Workplaces are scrambling to catch up with this new normal, including retention of their current team members.

But going it solo can be a lonely place! While some thrive on it, others are forsaking the world of freelance and contractorship only to re-plug into the dynamic of a team. Many employees never left in the first place and are wondering if their current environment can hold up to the ideals of culture, loyalty, and sense of shared accomplishment that we all crave when we join a company.

Pat Summitt was no stranger to the power of team. Not only did she excel in taking freshmen athletes from vastly different backgrounds and turning them into well-oiled championship machines, she went beyond the traditional requirements of a functioning team with her infamous Definite Dozen. Coach Summitt embraced these unique principles as the 12 requirements for success in any organization, including the teams she coached.

As we learned in the previous course, all 12 of these principles are important. But now it’s time to take a closer look at one of the bedrocks of the modern workforce that seems to erode with each passing year — one that is still vital to a well-oiled organization and the overall health and satisfaction of those who work there: The Power of Team.

Introduction: The Power of Team 
Joan Cronan 
Did you grow up in a high-functioning team? Some people learn the power of teamwork 
through their high school sports. For some, the concept of team is new in the workforce. Still, 
others may have experienced the concept of team before, but a slightly dysfunctional one. 
Wherever you're starting from, the power of team in today's workforce isn't a skill that comes 
naturally. To say so much emphasis placed on honing individuals or subject matter expertise 
that qualities that become a world-class team often fade to the background. 
Pat Summitt was no stranger to the importance of team, and she was a social engineer at 
building championship-level performing teams. That included individuals from all walks of life. 
Pat once said, "Team building starts by recruiting good people and turning them into willing 
role models early on." 
How do you convince a group of highly talented individual performers to set aside their 
personal feelings, ambitions, and agendas in favor of a united effort? When I think about this 
and I think about Pat, I often think about what she thought was so important was people 
knowing their role. I remember one year we had three seniors on the team. All of them were 
great players. We had a freshman who was really better than the three seniors. And it came 
time to vote for All-American. Pat came in and said, "I need to vote for the freshman, but I hate 
to leave these two seniors who've had a great year off." And I said, "Let's talk about it." And she 
said, "I need to talk to them." So, she went in and she talked to them about why she was going 
to vote for the freshman and why the other two were such an important part of the team. 
Guess what? We went on into the championship. One of the seniors got the most valuable 
player at the SEC tournament. The next senior got the most valuable player at the regionals. 
And the freshman helped us win a national championship and was an All-American. That might 
not have happened if Pat didn't realize the importance of team and how it affected everybody 
else. 
Communication and the roles that they play are so important. In this program, we'll identify 
some important characteristics for building a great team wherever you are. We'll take a closer 
look specifically at Pat's Definite Dozen of loyalty, responsibility, and communication, three of 
the core principles for forming a healthy, functioning group. At the end of each lesson, you'll be 
challenged to build on these principles with personal reflections and short exercises. At the end 
of each module, there is a short quiz to test what you've learned. At the end of this course, 
you'll know more about teamwork and the key components that can take your teams to the 
next level. Let's get started.

Why Is Team Important?

Why Is the Concept of Team More Important Than Ever?

You’ve heard the old saying, “People don’t leave jobs, they leave leaders.” It could equally be said that people don’t leave missions, they leave dysfunctional teams.
Show me a person who is healthy, functional, plugged in to an organization’s overall mission — someone who truly enjoys working with their fellow team members and feels valued by the organization — and I’ll show you someone who is harder to entice away for a few thousand dollars more per year. These people aren’t even looking elsewhere. It’s the team member who feels isolated, undervalued, and disconnected from the overall mission who is looking over the fence for greener pastures.

So how do you build great teams? Pat would be the first to tell us that people aren’t born great team players. “Teamwork does not come naturally,” she once noted. “How do you convince a group of highly talented individual performers to set aside their personal feelings, ambitions, and agendas in favor of a unified effort?”1 As a coach, it was a problem she personally faced on an annual basis.

The truth is, not everyone grew up playing sports where we were taught the power of team. It’s not a skill you necessarily learn in school, as academic performance is up to one’s individual efforts. By contrast, some people grew up playing on dysfunctional teams or participating in extracurricular activities where there were only one or two stars and everyone else on the team was merely an accessory. Yet, if people were honest, it’s the very thing most of us crave — to join with others in pursuit of a healthy, fulfilling mission.

## potl_mp_01_st_004 — Team Involves More Than Output

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 7
Team Involves More Than Output

The Concept of Team Involves More Than Output

The goal of Pat’s championship programs wasn’t to develop star players; it was to have an unbeatable team year after year. “Team-building starts by recruiting good people and turning them into willing role players early on,” she taught.
Self-discipline, responsibility, hard work, and individual performance may come naturally to some, but how do you teach shared ownership, respect for others, loyalty, good communication, respect for the leader, and no gossip, just to name a few?
While all the Definite Dozen principles are important, there are three principles in particular whose lack arguably causes a majority of the problems for teams in the modern workforce.

Click to expand and complete this element.

Responsibility

How do you encourage your employees to take ownership for their work and embrace/internalize the shared vision of the company?

Loyalty

How do you cultivate employees who are loyal to the company and its mission?

Communication

Organizations move at the speed of communication and trust. How do you foster team communication — from the top down, from the bottom up, and from side-to-side among peers?

There’s a reason these are principles #2 to #4 on the Definite Dozen(c) wheel. We’ll call them the LCR (Loyalty, Communication, Responsibility) of team-building.

Team-building starts by recruiting good people and turning them into willing role players early on.”
