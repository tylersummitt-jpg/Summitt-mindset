Jimmy Delaney 
Few people would disagree. Communication is the cornerstone of success, whether you're in 
personal or professional settings. It's the lifeblood of any high-functioning team and a core 
principle in Pat's famous Definite Dozen leadership principles. Communication is more than just 
words. It's the bridge that connects team members. Clear and open communication builds 
trust, respect, and cooperation, whether it's face-to-face, virtual meetings, or written 
messages. Miscommunication can have severe consequences. When we fail to express 
ourselves clearly, it can lead to confusion, conflict, and missed opportunities. 
Here at Tennessee, communication is key to everything that we do for a successful event, 
whether we're having 50 people for a private event or 100,000 people for a football game. 
Communication ultimately is what drives our success. 
Earlier in my career, I realized how vital communication was. I was trying to do many things on 
my own, and I realized that teamwork and communication was going to bring all of our ideas 
together. By not communicating the entire plan to the entire team that was helping us, 
ultimately, I ended up taking more of the work on myself, dropping some of those balls, and 
ultimately it wasn't as successful as it was. In that, I learned to communicate with everybody, 
even somebody that may not be helping you or could have an opportunity to help you, still 
communicate with them because you never know where you might need them for us on a 
game day. 
To give another example, let's say John was preparing for an important client presentation but 
only mentioned it casually to a few colleagues in passing. He didn't really provide any context or 
seek their input formally. Then the day before the presentation, he realized he needed more 
information and hastily sent out a request for feedback. However, most team members were 
unprepared to provide useful input on such a short notice. They didn't have enough context on 
what he was asking to be able to help out in a meaningful way. 
Or let's say his colleague Marcy had a major project she was working on and sent a brief, vague 
email to the team, assuming they would understand their roles and responsibilities. And the 
email lacked supporting details, such as the why or the overall goal of the project. Marcy 
entered all of the tasks into the project management system but never followed up to make 
sure everyone had received them or understood the instructions in the tasks. What kind of 
results do you think she got? You guessed it. The cost of miscommunication or under-
communication can be high. You may have even experienced some of these outcomes yourself.

By contrast, over-communicating, especially when in doubt, is a powerful strategy to prevent 
miscommunication and bridge gaps in understanding. So is providing context. You may already 
be working several methods of this into your weekly rhythms, and that's great. Most teams use 
a variety of the following to make sure communication is running smoothly and flowing freely 
among the team: 
Daily stand-ups: brief, focused meetings where the team shares progress, upcoming tasks, and 
any obstacles they are facing. This keeps everyone aligned and aware of each other's activities, 
allowing for quick problem-solving. 
Regular feedback loops: regularly scheduled one-on-one meetings between team members and 
their managers provide a platform for constructive feedback. 
Quick-time communication tools: using tools like Microsoft Teams, Slack, or Trello for ongoing 
communication and project management ensures that all team members can collaborate 
effectively, regardless of their location. Here at Tennessee, we use both Slack and Trello, and 
because we are all over the place and working on many different things, not worried about 
which email is the most recent, Slack, a real-time communication tool, helps us out 
tremendously. 
Taking the time to communicate well at its core is a powerful mark of respect. Let's take a 
minute to learn more.

## potl_mp_06_st_003 — When in Doubt, Overcommunicate

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 9
When in Doubt, Overcommunicate

Learning to Overcommunicate

The heart of communicating to win can be summed up in four words: When in doubt, overcommunicate.
For best results, always err on the side of overcommunicating, rather than under-communicating. What does this look like?
It means that it’s better to communicate too much than too little, particularly when there’s ambiguity around a task or project. Overcommunicating means providing more information or updates than might be strictly necessary. When you overcommunicate, you ensure that everyone involved in a project or task has all the information they need to do their jobs effectively. This can help to prevent misunderstandings, delays, and mistakes.
Let’s take a look at two sample scenarios:

Scenario 1

To give a practical example, let’s say the vice president is asking for everyone’s weekly report or status updates, including a monthly revenue update, by Thursday morning instead of Monday morning, and you’re in charge of getting it from the team members you manage. You announce this in the next daily standup — revenue updates and estimates from their department are now due on Thursday. What’s to be misunderstood; it’s super clear, right?
For starters, three team members aren’t in the standup. One walked in late, and a few people were having a separate conversation in the back of the room about something urgent. Thursday morning rolls around and … you guessed it, you’re short five reports (including one person who “just forgot”). A few others turned in the weekly status update as usual but included the monthly revenue update as month-to-date instead of projected estimates for the end of the month like you asked. You think, This is unbelievable. People can’t follow simple instructions.
You now have to scramble to give the vice president the numbers she needs, furiously emailing the team members who have missing reports, demanding they send you something ASAP. The truth is, in the words of Cool Hand Luke, “What we have here, is a failure to communicate.”
With just an ounce of prevention — or rather, overcommunication — you might have had a different outcome.

Scenario 2

Let’s say you make the announcement in standup, followed by an email to everyone afterward reinforcing the announcement that you need reports by Thursday, including a brief, bulleted list of what needs to be in the report. Then, Wednesday you send a reminder, including a sample report attached to the email, asking those who can’t send you the report by Thursday morning to let you know in advance. Thursday rolls around, and this time you have all your reports, except for a team member who is at a funeral and communicated that in advance. Or if still you don’t have all your reports for some reason, it becomes easier to address the failure directly with the individuals involved, now that the excuse of communication is taken out of the loop.

Heading

These two examples show how the smallest things can make a difference in the area of communication at work.

## potl_mp_06_st_004 — An Ounce of Prevention

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 4

Lesson 4 of 9
An Ounce of Prevention

Heading

Overcommunicating can also help you avoid a lot of unnecessary drama on the team.

To give another example, let’s say you have to fire a salesperson who wasn’t getting the job done. When this was mentioned, the team member didn’t react kindly to the criticism and went off on an inappropriate tirade listing all the faults of your organization. You terminate them, but to help the employee save face, you decide to omit the details, sending a cryptic “Steve is no longer with our organization” email. After all, this is confidential, and it’s no one else’s business, right? You decide to follow up verbally with more details two days later in a staff meeting.

The trouble is, Steve was well-liked among the team. Several are good friends with Steve outside of work. The people reporting to Steve who find out in the email at the same time as everyone else are now shocked, confused, and worried about what will happen next. They received all their training from Steve. Did he do something wrong? Rumors are flying by lunchtime as to who will be taking Steve’s place. One person heard it was because Steve questioned the new learning management system’s efficacy and warns people it’s “not safe to speak up anymore with feedback.” A few people text Steve directly, who, still wounded, says the company “has had it out for him all along.” By 3 p.m., half the team is upset that the organization could do such a thing to poor Steve.

Sound familiar? The details may vary, but you may have experienced some version of this at your organization because information was scant and the details were fuzzy. People were left to fill in the gaps of what actually happened with the limited knowledge they had. Overcommunicating doesn’t mean violating someone’s privacy, but with a bit more communication, a lot of the drama above could have been avoided.

A Better Way

To overcommunicate might look something like this: The people who report to or are closest to Steve are told first and given the most information. Without getting into details about Steve’s performance or feelings about the company, they are told whether Steve left on good terms or bad and whether reaching out to Steve might be sensitive at the moment. They are told whether it was one-sided or mutual, and that Steve expressed discontent with his current situation and role. Then, an email goes out to the whole team letting them know of Steve’s departure, that Christine will be temporarily overseeing his area in the interim, and to please direct any questions about his departure or projects to Andrew. They are told that Steve was part of the discussion about leaving and expressed discontent in his area, but the company is grateful for his service and wishes him the very best in his next chapter.

## potl_mp_06_st_007 — Provide Context

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 7

Lesson 7 of 9
Provide Context

Heading

A wise person once said, “You get better results with a little of the why.” Inexperienced leaders tend to default to keeping people in the dark, but experienced leaders know team member buy-in is crucial for a healthy, functioning team. Pat made it clear on her teams that every task or mandate was working toward a particular goal — from the overall goal of national championships to the micro-goals of slowing down a star shooter on the other team or adjusting the defense strategy for an upcoming team who was more physical than they were.

“Because I said so” is usually easier than slowing down to explain the strategy or exact reason behind the ask, but remember: Your success rises and falls on the shoulders of your team. If they can do a better job with more context than less, this ought to be the standard.

For example, you could tell your social media coordinator to spotlight a certain product more, and they may add two or three more posts to the monthly calendar. But if you let them know the why — that this product is eventually going to become the foundation for replacing two other products and that sales need to exceed a certain point — you’re likely to get better results. What’s more, your team may surprise you by thinking of additional ways to reach the goal that you hadn’t considered.

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 8

Lesson 8 of 9
Set People Up to Win

Heading

Communicating frequently, with context, usually gives team members most of the information they need to win, paired with the practice of setting proper expectations. Don’t leave an abundance of dots for the team to connect on their own. When it comes to setting expectations: To be unclear is to be unkind. No one wants to charge the lane and realize that what they thought was the target wasn’t the target or the way they were expending all their time and energy wasn’t really what the organization wanted. Set your team members up to win.
When individuals win, the team wins. And when the team wins, your organization wins.
