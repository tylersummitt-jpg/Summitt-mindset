Lesson 6: Fairways and Forest Fires 
Michelle Marciniak 
We've all heard it said before, it's not just about what you say, but also how you say it. This is 
just as true in the workplace as it is anywhere else. Understanding the importance of situational 
and nonverbal communication can make a significant difference in developing a positive and 
productive team. And it all starts with you as the leader. 
Before we dive into the nitty-gritty, let's take a moment to understand the power of tone in 
communication. Tone refers to the emotional quality behind the words we use, whether in 
spoken or written form. It conveys our attitudes, emotions, and intentions impacting how our 
messages are received by others. 
Suppose you came up to a colleague after a presentation where they dropped their coffee on 
their notes. That didn't go well, you joke, smiling. Suppose you came up to the same colleague 
afterwards with a stern look on your face. That didn't go well, you announce in a serious, 
somewhat ominous tone. 
In the workplace, context matters. We need to be mindful of the situations we find ourselves in 
and tailor our communication accordingly. Take a team meeting, for instance. An overly 
aggressive tone might shut down ideas and squash your team's feedback. On the other hand, if 
a team member has been making inappropriate comments or getting out of hand, a firm tone 
may be entirely appropriate. 
In a one-on-one with your team members especially, it's essential to strike a balance between 
being supportive and providing constructive feedback. A dismissive tone can make the 
employee feel unimportant, while a caring and constructive tone can inspire growth. Feedback 
given in a harsh tone can be taken much differently than feedback in a positive tone. 
In general, there are four quadrants of vocal variety: 
Loud and slow conveys authority. You might use this when addressing an issue that is critical 
and there needs to be no room for misunderstanding. 
Loud and fast communicates enthusiasm. You might use this when you are introducing a new 
idea in a meeting and you need to generate excitement. 
Low and slow communicates sincerity and empathy. This tone you might use when you're 
consoling a team member or delivering difficult news. 
Soft and fast conveys anticipation. You might use this tone to communicate upcoming changes, 
almost as if you are letting someone in on a secret.

Knowing how and when to use different tones appropriately can be the difference between 
coming off as authoritative or weak, sensitive or insensitive, excited or unenthusiastic. 
Remember, our words are just one part of the message we convey. Research suggests that up 
to 93% of communication is nonverbal. That means that our body language, facial expressions, 
and tone of voice play a big part in how our messages are interpreted. 
So, how can we be a part of creating a positive communication culture in the workplace? Self-
awareness is the first step in becoming effective communicators. With that in mind, let's dive 
in.

Heading

The second component of personal communication is being able to appropriately assess each unique situation to determine the right level of communication.
“Frequently, knowing what to say to someone is all about time and location,” Pat once said. “You don’t always make the right call. There’s a judgment involved. You have to ask yourself, ‘What does this person need today?’ And you have to watch his or her body language.”

Context of the Communication

Understanding the context of a conversation includes not only the physical setting but also the emotional backdrop of the communication.

Knowing Your Audience

Audience awareness is at the heart of situational communication. Different audiences require different approaches, vocabulary, and levels of formality.

Timing of the Communication

Timing can significantly influence communication effectiveness. Not every moment is ideal for every type of message; situational communicators recognize when to wait and when to act.

Nonverbal Communication

Body language, eye contact, posture, gestures and facial expressions—plays an equally important role in situational communication. Research shows that a large portion of human communication is non-verbal, meaning that words alone do not fully capture the message being conveyed. The non-verbal cues of the receiver can also help you gauge how your communication is being received. Are they sitting with their arms crossed? Scowling? etc.

The point is, context can significantly affect how a message is received.We’ll go over this more in the upcoming lesson.

Pat once told a story of how the 1977 team had stayed up late one night drinking and partying until 4 a.m. in violation of team policies. When they showed up for practice the next day completely hungover, she debated how to respond. She considered giving them a tongue lashing but thought the better of it. Instead, she put trash cans on the baseline at the corners of the gym and conducted practice for four hours, by the end of which almost every player had vomited into one of the trash cans.
Her point was made.

“If all you ever do is yell, then you will be heard less and less,” she said. “That’s why sometimes, saying nothing at all can be just as powerful.”
One of Pat’s famous mannerisms is the “Summitt Stare” — a famous glare she often employed on the sidelines. The stare is said to have been so intense that it could make players and officials alike feel nervous or unsettled. Players and opponents knew that when they saw the stare, they were in for a tough game.

As a coach, Pat knew when to speak and when to be silent. The habit of communicating through body language is one that the best communicators have intentionally worked on over time, choosing which postures, attitude, or gestures to use in which situation so they convey their message in the most effective way.

Lesson 6: Tone and Nonverbal Communication 
Christina Gradillas 
Tone of voice is powerful when it comes to mastering the art of communication. How many 
times has a situation escalated simply because of the tone of voice? We all, male or female, fall 
prone to this, betraying our emotions when speaking or raising our voice when we don't mean 
to, when we get excited. There's nothing wrong with emotion. Don't get me wrong. Good 
leaders, skilled communicators, know how to master their emotion and be mindful of their 
tone. Pat Summit knew when to raise her voice during a game, but she also knew when to bring 
it down and remain calm. 
Okay, where are you in the area of body language? Pat once said, there is more to being vocal 
than simply being audible, especially if you are in a leadership role. You communicate with 
gestures and body language all the time without realizing it. How you sit in a meeting may send 
a message. Which chair you choose to sit in might send one also. You can unconsciously project 
confidence or uncertainty. “Generally, I don't sit on the sidelines. I stand because I feel closer to 
the game. Also, I'm aware that my body sends a message from 90 feet away. Good eye contact, 
a straight posture, and a thumbs up can impart conviction. It's not enough to say, I'm confident. 
You can say those words 100 times over and over, but no one will buy it if your shoulders are 
slumped and your voice cracks.” 
It turns out when it comes to success with professional body language, your mother was right. 
Stand up straight. Don't fidget. Smile and give a firm handshake. One way you can start to learn 
this is simply by observing others. Have you ever intentionally noticed this before? Who in your 
office has great body language? Take some time after this video to jot down a few people who 
come to mind. Now, why is this? What are they doing that sets them apart? Maybe they always 
appear cheerful and confident. Maybe it's the way they give you their full attention when 
someone is talking. Maybe they mimic your expressions to show they're with you when you're 
explaining something. It could be a variety of different things, but be intentional. Be on the 
lookout this week in your office. 
There are several books or videos out there that can help you work on your body language, but 
there are just a few techniques: 
• Good posture, not slouching, right? Not crossing your arms and looking closed off. No 
one wants to approach you like that. 
• Avoiding any nervous gestures like jiggling your leg or playing with your jewelry. 
• Face the person talking to you or lean in when they're speaking to show interest. 
• Proper use of your hands, not overdoing it or underdoing it with the hand motions.

It's hard to find someone who is a master at body language. We all have our unique quirks and 
traits, so it's important to figure out what yours are. And to do this, you may need a little bit of 
help. Have you ever watched yourself on a home movie and been horrified? Me too. Do I really 
talk like that? Do I really sound like that? You know who you are. So you want to do an audit 
where you are in the realm of body language. What are your strengths and what are your 
weaknesses? You also want to do the same for your facial expressions. It's easier than ever to 
see this now in the world of Zoom calls, but you may want to call in a friend or colleague you 
trust for backup. Tell them you're working on your body language and would they give you an 
honest opinion on two things you can improve. Or to let you know when your face is betraying 
you. And you'd be surprised at how many times our face is communicating something entirely 
different than what we think it is. You may have an expressive face, in which case every 
emotion, concern, frustration, anger, disappointment is visible on your face. Or you may have 
the opposite problem, a poker face, where there's little to no expression on your face and your 
coworkers can't read you that well. They may think on your face, and your coworkers can't read 
you that well. They may think you're displeased when you're not, or unfriendly when you're 
not. So, start with your face. 
You also want to be mindful of your eye contact. This may come very natural to you, but 
sometimes we can be naturally shy, perceived as less confident. If you're an introvert, male or 
female, good eye contact may not come naturally. A good rule of thumb is to make eye contact 
50% of the time when speaking, and 70% when listening. That's right, 70% when listening. 
If you find yourself struggling with eye contact at first, practice noticing the color of people's 
eyes. Hold eye contact for several seconds, as long as it takes to determine the color of their 
eyes. Then look away if you need to. If looking people in the eye is too stressful, look at the spot 
right between their eyes or their nose. Whatever it takes to start building the habit. 
Another technique to try is reading people's expression by looking only at their eyes. You'd be 
surprised by what someone's eyes can tell you. Do the eyes look sad? Tired? Excited? Nervous? 
There's a reason they say the eyes are the window to the soul. You'll find that in the process of 
trying to read people's eyes, you're working two skills: holding eye contact and learning to read 
other people's body language, which is a key part of emotional intelligence. 
So, that's several things to work on, but it's worth it. And even the greats still have to work on 
their body language and overall communication. In her memoir, Pat once wrote that she didn't 
consider herself starting off to be a very good communicator. “I was pretty helpless. It's a 
change I'm proud to have made, and it's the way in which I've grown most as a coach.” 
With all of that in mind, how will you grow?
