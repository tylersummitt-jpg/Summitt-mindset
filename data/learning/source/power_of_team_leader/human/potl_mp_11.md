Lesson 10: How to Be Loyal to Your Team 
Petra Dunn 
Do you have your employees back? That question could be taken several different ways. Do you 
mean in a disagreement? With benefits? Or in areas of promotion? In a word, yes. As Pat once 
said, loyalty is something that must be tended to on a daily basis. It's a bond that must continue 
to be strengthened throughout the inevitable highs and lows. 
But what does that mean specifically? In this lesson, we'll look at some specific ways you can 
champion your own team members. Think about the last time someone showed you loyalty in a 
troubled spot. What if you could recreate that feeling in the workplace? Loyalty is something 
that is caught more than taught, as they say. It's something team members have to feel or 
experience for themselves, not just something you can verbally tell them is important. 
The good news is there are several ways you can begin to show intentional loyalty to your team 
members. Loyalty can be shown with great benefits, yes. Who doesn't appreciate a great 
compensation package? But that's not the only way. And it's becoming harder and harder to 
stand out in the workplace solely with good benefits. We're talking about the unique loyalty 
you as a leader can model and pass on to your team. 
For example: 
Viewing everyone as a star player. As Pat once put it, “If you seek out quality people and 
convince them to be loyal to your organization, you will succeed, but you must be willing to 
acknowledge their talents and let them do their jobs.” This means giving them a level of trust 
from the beginning as part of the team, not making trust something that starts on zero and has 
to be earned. 
Championing team member growth. Pat did this excellently. She knew how to take 
underdeveloped, bright young athletes and give them increasing responsibilities. The pathways 
for growth on her team were clearly outlined. It wasn't a mystery. Team member growth 
should be a priority, not an afterthought when it comes to cultivating loyal employees. This 
means being slow to offboard or not overreacting to people's mistakes. Conflicts are going to 
happen. Mistakes are going to be made. Those times will test your loyalty to your team 
members. Do you cut the rope and turn them loose? Or do you give them a reasonable time to 
work on their behavior and correct? Nothing kills loyalty among team members more than 
watching other team members be axed or terminated for little to no reason. 
Finally, recognize your rock stars. This means celebrating wins and looking to catch people 
doing the right things. The best leaders know to not only do this, but how to do it well. You also

want to look for ways to cultivate an environment where team members are loyal to each 
other. 
For example: 
Communicate openly about company goals, challenges, and decisions, creating an environment 
where employees feel informed and included. When everyone has equal access to the 
information, it eliminates unnecessary rivalries and pecking orders. 
Hold regular team meetings where employees are encouraged to voice their opinions. This 
keeps things from being bottled and leaking into the gossip mill. It also prevents one employee 
from having the ear of senior leadership and everyone else feels excluded. 
Follow through on your promises. When leaders follow through on their promises, it sets a 
standard for the entire team. Clearly communicate job duties and roles among the team. 
Nothing creates contention and disloyalty faster than confusion among who is doing what. If 
someone has authority in a particular area, make sure the team knows it, rather than refusing 
to clarify. This can lead to team members thinking leadership has a teacher's pet and can cause 
disloyalty among your team. 
When conflict arises between team members, encourage them to work it out first between 
themselves before calling in upper management. This puts the responsibility on them for their 
own interactions and relationships with their team members without leveraging leadership as a 
referee. 
These are just a few things you can do to not only be loyal to your team, but encourage them to 
be loyal to each other.

Everyone a Star

The simplest way to show loyalty is to start by letting people do their jobs. In other words, a certain level of trust comes with the job. The message is clear: “We hired you. We believe in you.”
This sounds obvious, but many well-meaning organizations are crippled by inadvertent traditions of micromanagement or unreasonable expectations of seniority.
Let’s look at a few examples of what this can look like.

Summary

There are nuances to every story, but the one thing all these examples have in common is that the companies who hired Tyson, Shelby, and Juan didn’t have much initial loyalty to them as they tried to perform in their roles. There’s an old saying, “See everyone as a star.” In the workplace, this means remembering why you hired someone and looking for the ways they shine. It means celebrating what makes them unique, rather than trying to force them into the mold of their counterparts or predecessors.
Pat asked herself, What do my athletes need to deliver? So, ask yourself: What do my employees need to deliver in their roles?, How can I help?, and sometimes, How can I get out of their way?

Tyson

Let’s say Tyson, a highly skilled, associate-level marketer, has been hired  to create a marketing funnel for a new division of the company. The problem is, the director of marketing doesn’t really trust Tyson to execute.
Tyson patiently sits in meeting after meeting while the director explains what he would do. Tyson tries his best to follow through, including giving a few additional ideas that are new and innovative. The marketing director sends them to the board of directors for review, but they’re quickly shot down in an email. Six months later, Tyson is still struggling to build a funnel that will appease everyone. Tyson was hired to do a specific job, but the very people who hired him don’t show much loyalty to the expertise they hired him for. After 10 more months, he gets frustrated and quits.

Shelby

In another example, Shelby has been working as an entry-level graphic designer for three years, eagerly accepting every new project and working on improving the company’s brand standards. She helps her colleagues out consistently and is always willing to take on extra work. Her designs are well-received by stakeholders and have improved the customer experience on the website dramatically.
When it comes time to hire an associate design director, the company looks at outside candidates, settling on someone the same age and skill level as Shelby but who came highly recommended from a competitor. Shelby is crushed. It will be years until the department is big enough to hire another associate design director. She quietly starts putting in resumes elsewhere.

Juan

Juan is a highly sought-after product manager with 10 years of experience. He joins a company looking to take their existing line of products to the next level and expand into two new markets. The problem is, Juan is consistently left out of key conversations about the product by the C-suite team. They meet regularly to discuss the gaps and flaws in the current product, telling Juan what they would like him to do about it.
When Juan does manage to get invited to a meeting and share his thoughts, he’s told: “When you’ve been here at least five years, your opinion will count more.” Juan doesn’t want to wait five years to have a voice in his own role. When a recruiter calls two months later, he accepts a similar role at another company.

## potl_mp_11_st_003 — Championing Team Member Growth

Type: reading · Source sequence: 3

Lesson 3 of 8
Championing Team Member Growth

Heading

The second step in showing loyalty is a fairly obvious one: To show loyalty to your team, you have to invest in the growth of your team members. What does this look like specifically? For Pat and her players, it started with increasing levels of responsibility, as we discussed in the previous module on Responsibility. For your organization, it may mean delegating freely and giving team members a chance to increase their influence or responsibilities in their current roles, before being promoted.

Many times, this involves providing training and continuing education opportunities on the job to help people grow in their skills. A person who is on the cutting edge of a particular industry will quickly grow stale five to eight years later (through no character flaw of their own) if not given the opportunity to further their skills and stay current with the latest trends and technology. Don’t inadvertently punish talented and loyal employees for staying at your company for years by hiring someone from the outside solely because they know the latest trends. Give current employees the opportunity to further their skills on the job.

A large part of employee growth is providing opportunities for advancement. As we saw in Shelby’s example, nothing erodes loyalty faster than competent employees who have been with the company for several years being passed over for internal positions. Internal promotions may not always be feasible, but it’s worth taking a firm look at what the career paths are for your current employees. First, decide if ample opportunities exist. Then look around your own department or ask your HR team for what percentage of promotions are being hired externally and what percentage are being promoted from within — are those opportunities being realized?

## potl_mp_11_st_005 — Recognizing Your Stars

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 5

Lesson 5 of 8
Recognizing Your Stars

Being Slow to Offboard

OK, that’s all fine when you’ve got employees who are great at their jobs, you might think. But when you’ve worked hard to get an employee hired, and it’s not quite working out in a particular role, what do you do? Many employers simply cut their losses and move on. Employees are expendable, right? Not so fast!

If an employee is a good culture fit, it’s worth examining whether there might be another role that would be a better fit before jumping straight to a termination plan. As Pat would say, you’ve got to get people “in the right seat on the bus.” Being quick to promote and slow to offboard shows employees that the company is committed to them and will make every effort to ensure they can have a career at the organization.

Recognizing Your Rock Stars

No matter what their seat on the bus, a little recognition toward your employees goes a long way. The best companies know not only how to do this, but also how to do it well.
There are many different ways to recognize team members for excelling in their roles. Here are a few ideas.
Please click each to complete this element.

Verbal Praise and Appreciation

One of the simplest yet most effective ways is through sincere verbal praise. Acknowledge their accomplishments and express gratitude for their efforts.

Public Recognition

Publicly recognizing employees' achievements during team meetings, company-wide announcements, or newsletters can amplify the recognition… and inspire others to strive for excellence as well.

Written Recognition

It sounds traditional, but a handwritten note, an email, or a personalized message can have a significant impact. Some people keep cards of appreciation at their desk for years! Seize the opportunity to provide detailed feedback, and express appreciation in writing.

Awards

To standardize recognition efforts, many companies choose to institute a series of weekly, monthly or annual awards to recognize outstanding achievement in a certain area.

Reward Programs

Consider implementing reward programs that provide tangible incentives for exceptional performance. These can include gift cards, bonuses, extra time off or other rewards.

Peer-to-Peer Recognition

Encourage a culture of peer-to-peer recognition where employees can acknowledge and appreciate each other's contributions.

Celebrate Ordinary Accomplishments

Organize celebrations to mark significant milestones, project completions or team successes. Birthdays or company anniversaries are a start. These events can be formal or informal, such as team lunches and happy hours.

Heading

With just a little intentionality, you can make your team members feel valued and appreciated on a regular basis.

## potl_mp_11_st_007 — Showing Loyalty With Benefits

Type: interactive_reading · Source sequence: 7

Lesson 7 of 8
Showing Loyalty With Benefits

Heading

Finally, take a step back and reflect on whether your organization’s compensation/benefits say “loyalty,” or “bare minimum.” The fact is, it’s hard to be loyal to a company whose benefits don’t encourage long-term careers. A few of the key areas include:
(Please click each to complete this element)

Do employees have to show outstanding performance, just get what equates to a cost-of-living raise, or is there room for outstanding compensation?

Healthy pay increases

Otherwise known as “you do good work for us, we’ll let you rest."

PTO/Sick time

This can include dental, vision, life insurance, disability, long-term care, etc.

Health benefits

This says, “We value you, and you won’t have to leave to be able to save well for retirement.” Consider adding a company match.

Retirement plans

It sounds like a no-brainer, but very few businesses manage to do all of the above. Some only do two or three and try to make up for a deficiency in one area by giving away free sports tickets or adding a gym plan. Such perks are appreciated – but do not mistake the small things for big things. Employees won’t work for a company with subpar health insurance simply because they get a discount on live event tickets.

Your company benefits may or may not be directly up to you as a leader, but as a member of the leadership team, encourage upper management to consistently reevaluate what’s being offered and how to show employees that the company is dedicated to their long-term growth.
By championing your team members, publicly recognizing them, encouraging their growth, and providing first-class benefits, you’ll cultivate a culture of loyalty that’s hard to beat.
